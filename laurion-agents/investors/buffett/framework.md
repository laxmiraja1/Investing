# Warren Buffett: Investment Framework

> **Status: sourced.** Every rule below quotes Buffett verbatim from a file in `archive/private/`. Each citation gives the archive id, the date the document became public (`date_published` in `archive/manifest.csv`), and the file. Rules are marked **stated** (what he said) or **revealed** (what his trades show, in his own account of them) and say when they applied. Items with no quote in the archive are listed at the end under *Unsourced*.
>
> The agent at date T must use only rules whose evidence is dated before T. A 1965 Buffett and a 2011 Buffett would analyze the same company differently (see §10).

Citation format: `> quote` then `> — archive-id · date published · file`.

## 1. Universe & idea sourcing

- **Search the whole universe yourself, page by page.** *Stated in 2001 about the partnership years.*
  > When I started, I went through the pages of the manuals page by page. I mean, I probably went through 20,000 pages in the Moody's industrial, transportation, banks and finance manuals.
  > — `agm-2001` · 2001-04-28 · `private/agm/agm-2001.txt`

- **Partnership era: screen quantitatively for undervalued "generals", then check quality.** *Stated; 1957–1969.*
  > A category of generally undervalued stocks, determined primarily by quantitative standards, but with considerable attention also paid to the qualitative factor.
  > — `bpl-letter-1963` · 1964-01-18 · `private/partnership/bpl-letter-1963.txt`

- **Berkshire acquisition criteria.** *Stated; first printed in the 1982 letter (published 1983-03-03), repeated in later letters.*
  > We prefer: (1) large purchases (at least $5 million of after-tax earnings), (2) demonstrated consistent earning power (future projections are of little interest to us, nor are “turn-around” situations), (3) businesses earning good returns on equity while employing little or no debt, (4) management in place (we can’t supply it), (5) simple businesses (if there’s lots of technology, we won’t understand it), (6) an offering price
  > — `brk-letter-1982` · 1983-03-03 · `private/letters/brk-1982.txt`

- **Hunt "elephants".** *Stated. First used in 1987 about keeping capital ready for big deals; by the late 1990s it is about size: only very large ideas move Berkshire.* (The old lead dated this to the 2000s.)
  > Our basic principle is that if you want to shoot rare, fast-moving elephants, you should always carry a loaded gun.
  > — `brk-letter-1987` · 1988-02-29 · `private/letters/brk-1987.txt`

  > We need “elephants” to make significant gains now – and they are hard to find.
  > — `brk-letter-2001` · 2002-02-28 · `private/letters/brk-2001.txt`

## 2. Filters / gatekeepers

- **Circle of competence: know its boundaries.** *Stated; 1996 letter.*
  > You only have to be able to evaluate companies within your circle of competence. The size of that circle is not very important; knowing its boundaries, however, is vital.
  > — `brk-letter-1996` · 1997-02-28 · `private/letters/brk-1996.txt`

- **Turnarounds seldom turn.** *Stated; 1979 letter (after the Waumbec purchase).*
  > Both our operating and investment experience cause us to conclude that “turnarounds” seldom turn, and that the same energies and talent are much better employed in a good business purchased at a fair price than in a poor business purchased at a bargain price.
  > — `brk-letter-1979` · 1980-03-03 · `private/letters/brk-1979.txt`

- **Size: a position must be big enough to matter.** *Stated; from 2003 onward.*
  > Today, the number of stocks that can be purchased in large enough quantities to move the performance needle at Berkshire is a small fraction of the number that existed a decade ago.
  > — `brk-letter-2003` · 2004-02-27 · `private/letters/brk-2003.txt`

  > But huge sums forge their own anchor and our future advantage, if any, will be a small fraction of our historical edge.
  > — `brk-letter-2009` · 2010-02-26 · `private/letters/brk-2009.txt`

- **Technology is outside the circle, with two reasoned exceptions.** *Stated avoidance from 1982; IBM bought 2011; Apple from late 2016, treated as a consumer business.*
  > In this mea culpa, I am not talking about missing out on some company that depends upon an esoteric invention (such as Xerox), high-technology (Apple), or even brilliant merchandising (Wal-Mart). We will never develop the competence to spot such businesses early.
  > — `brk-letter-1991` · 1992-02-28 · `private/letters/brk-1991.txt`

  > As was the case with Coca-Cola in 1988 and the railroads in 2006, I was late to the IBM party. I have been reading the company’s annual report for more than 50 years, but it wasn’t until a Saturday in March last year that my thinking crystallized.
  > — `brk-letter-2011` · 2012-02-25 · `private/letters/brk-2011.txt`

  > I think Apple is much more of a consumer products business, in terms of the — in terms of sort of analyzing moats around it, and consumer behavior, and all that sort of thing.
  > — `agm-2017` · 2017-05-06 · `private/agm/agm-2017.txt`

- **Avoid businesses that need debt.** *Stated; criterion (3) in the 1982 list above, and the "without undue leverage" test in §3.*
  > businesses earning good returns on equity while employing little or no debt
  > — `brk-letter-1982` · 1983-03-03 · `private/letters/brk-1982.txt`

## 3. Business analysis

- **Return on equity, not EPS growth, is the primary test.** *Stated; 1979 letter.*
  > The primary test of managerial economic performance is the achievement of a high earnings rate on equity capital employed (without undue leverage, accounting gimmickry, etc.) and not the achievement of consistent gains in earnings per share.
  > — `brk-letter-1979` · 1980-03-03 · `private/letters/brk-1979.txt`

- **Durable competitive advantage: the moat around the castle.** *Stated; first in the 1986 letter (GEICO), not the 1990s as the old lead said.*
  > The difference between GEICO’s costs and those of its competitors is a kind of moat that protects a valuable and much- sought-after business castle.
  > — `brk-letter-1986` · 1987-02-27 · `private/letters/brk-1986.txt`

  > In business, I look for economic castles protected by unbreachable "moats."
  > — `brk-letter-1995` · 1996-03-01 · `private/letters/brk-1995.txt`

- **Pricing power and low capital needs (See's).** *Stated; 1991 and 2007 letters.*
  > In our See's purchase, Charlie and I had one important insight: We saw that the business had untapped pricing power.
  > — `brk-letter-1991` · 1992-02-28 · `private/letters/brk-1991.txt`

  > We bought See’s for $25 million when its sales were $30 million and pre-tax earnings were less than $5 million. The capital then required to conduct the business was $8 million.
  > — `brk-letter-2007` · 2008-02-29 · `private/letters/brk-2007.txt`

- **"The Inevitables".** *Stated; 1996 letter.*
  > Companies such as Coca-Cola and Gillette might well be labeled "The Inevitables."
  > — `brk-letter-1996` · 1997-02-28 · `private/letters/brk-1996.txt`

- **Owner earnings.** *Stated; appendix to the 1986 letter.*
  > If we think through these questions, we can gain some insights about what may be called "owner earnings." These represent (a) reported earnings plus (b) depreciation, depletion, amortization, and certain other non-cash charges such as Company N's items (1) and (4) less ( c) the average annual amount of capitalized expenditures for plant and equipment
  > — `brk-letter-1986` · 1987-02-27 · `private/letters/brk-1986.txt`

- **Look-through earnings.** *Stated; reported from the 1990 letter on.*
  > Thus our 1990 "look-through earnings" were about $590 million.
  > — `brk-letter-1990` · 1991-03-01 · `private/letters/brk-1990.txt`

## 4. Management & capital allocation

- **Honest, able management already in place; candid reporting.** *Stated; 1979 onward.*
  > We don’t expect a public relations document when our operating managers tell us what is going on, and we don’t feel you should receive such a document.
  > — `brk-letter-1979` · 1980-03-03 · `private/letters/brk-1979.txt`

  > the purchase at sensible prices of businesses that have good underlying economics and are run by honest and able people
  > — `brk-letter-1994` · 1995-03-07 · `private/letters/brk-1994.txt`

- **The institutional imperative.** *Stated; 1989 letter ("Mistakes of the First Twenty-five Years").*
  > My most surprising discovery: the overwhelming importance in business of an unseen force that we might call "the institutional imperative."
  > — `brk-letter-1989` · 1990-03-02 · `private/letters/brk-1989.txt`

- **Buybacks only well below intrinsic value.** *Stated; 1984 letter for investees, 1999 letter for Berkshire itself.*
  > major repurchases at prices well below per-share intrinsic business value immediately increase, in a highly significant way, that value.
  > — `brk-letter-1984` · 1985-02-25 · `private/letters/brk-1984.txt`

  > We will not repurchase shares unless we believe Berkshire stock is selling well below intrinsic value, conservatively calculated.
  > — `brk-letter-1999` · 2000-03-01 · `private/letters/brk-1999.txt`

- **Each $1 retained must create at least $1 of market value.** *Stated; first in the 1983 letter (the old lead credited only the 1996 Owner's Manual, which repeats it).*
  > We test the wisdom of retaining earnings by assessing whether retention, over time, delivers shareholders at least $1 of market value for each $1 retained.
  > — `brk-letter-1983` · 1984-03-14 · `private/letters/brk-1983.txt`

## 5. Valuation

- **Intrinsic value is the discounted cash a business will produce.** *Stated; this wording first appears in the 1994 letter and the 1996 Owner's Manual, not 1992 as the old lead said.*
  > We define intrinsic value as the discounted value of the cash that can be taken out of a business during its remaining life.
  > — `brk-letter-1994` · 1995-03-07 · `private/letters/brk-1994.txt`

- **Growth is part of value, not a separate style.** *Stated; 1992 letter.*
  > In our opinion, the two approaches are joined at the hip: Growth is always a component in the calculation of value, constituting a variable whose importance can range from negligible to enormous and whose impact can be negative as well as positive.
  > — `brk-letter-1992` · 1993-03-01 · `private/letters/brk-1992.txt`

- **Discount at the long-term government rate, with no equity risk premium.** *Stated; 1996 annual meeting, repeated in 1997 and 1998.*
  > We basically think in terms of the long-term government rate.
  > — `agm-1996` · 1996-05-06 · `private/agm/agm-1996.txt`

  > But we don't put the risk factor in, per se, because essentially, the purity of the idea is that you're discounting future cash.
  > — `agm-1996` · 1996-05-06 · `private/agm/agm-1996.txt`

- **A wonderful company at a fair price beats a fair company at a wonderful price.** *Stated; 1989 letter. Replaces the Graham-era bargain approach below.*
  > It's far better to buy a wonderful company at a fair price than a fair company at a wonderful price. Charlie understood this early; I was a slow learner.
  > — `brk-letter-1989` · 1990-03-02 · `private/letters/brk-1989.txt`

- **Graham-era methods: cigar butts and net working capital.** *Revealed in the partnership years; named and disowned in 1989.*
  > the company on December 31, 1965, had net working capital alone (before placing any value on the plants and equipment) of about $19 per share.
  > — `bpl-letter-1965` · 1966-01-20 · `private/partnership/bpl-letter-1965.txt`

  > I call this the "cigar butt" approach to investing.
  > — `brk-letter-1989` · 1990-03-02 · `private/letters/brk-1989.txt`

- **No elaborate models: the answer should be obvious.** *Stated; 1995 and 2009 annual meetings. Valuation reasoning should look like his, not like a sell-side DCF.*
  > But we really like the decision to be obvious enough to us that it doesn't require making a detailed calculation.
  > — `agm-1995` · 1995-05-01 · `private/agm/agm-1995.txt`

  > If you need to use a computer or a calculator to make the calculation, you shouldn't buy it.
  > — `agm-2009` · 2009-05-02 · `private/agm/agm-2009.txt`

## 6. Catalyst / timing

- **Berkshire era: no catalyst needed; the market eventually reflects business results.** *Stated; 1987 letter.*
  > The market may ignore business success for a while, but eventually will confirm it.
  > — `brk-letter-1987` · 1988-02-29 · `private/letters/brk-1987.txt`

  > Mr. Market is there to serve you, not to guide you.
  > — `brk-letter-1987` · 1988-02-29 · `private/letters/brk-1987.txt`

- **Partnership era: work-outs had explicit catalysts; controls were activist.** *Stated; 1957 and 1963 letters.*
  > A work-out is an investment which is dependent on a specific corporate action for its profit rather than a general advance in the price of the stock as in the case of undervalued situations. Work-outs come about through: sales, mergers, liquidations, tenders, etc.
  > — `bpl-letter-1957` · 1958-02-28 (letter undated; estimated) · `private/partnership/bpl-letter-1957.txt`

  > They result from situations where a cheap security does nothing price-wise for such an extended period of time that we are able to buy a significant percentage of the company's stock.
  > — `bpl-letter-1963` · 1964-01-18 · `private/partnership/bpl-letter-1963.txt`

## 7. Position sizing & portfolio construction

- **Concentrate; diversification is protection against ignorance.** *Stated; Ground Rule 7 added November 1965 (quoted in the January 1966 letter); the "ignorance" line is from the 1996 annual meeting.*
  > We might invest up to 40% of our net worth in a single security under conditions coupling an extremely high probability that our facts and reasoning are correct with a very low probability that anything could drastically change the underlying value of the investment.
  > — `bpl-letter-1965` · 1966-01-20 · `private/partnership/bpl-letter-1965.txt`

  > Diversification is a protection against ignorance.
  > — `agm-1996` · 1996-05-06 · `private/agm/agm-1996.txt`

- **Keep a large cash floor.** *Stated; from the 2000s (at least $20 billion by 2013). This constrains sizing in later years.*
  > we will always maintain supreme financial strength, operating with at least $20 billion of cash equivalents and never incurring material amounts of short-term obligations.
  > — `brk-letter-2013` · 2014-02-28 · `private/letters/brk-2013.txt`

## 8. Sell discipline

- **Default holding period: forever, for great businesses with great managers.** *Stated; 1987 (permanent holdings) and 1988 letters.*
  > And we will never find people we like and admire more than some of the main participants at the three companies - our permanent holdings - shown below:
  > — `brk-letter-1987` · 1988-02-29 · `private/letters/brk-1987.txt`

  > In fact, when we own portions of outstanding businesses with outstanding managements, our favorite holding period is forever.
  > — `brk-letter-1988` · 1989-02-28 · `private/letters/brk-1988.txt`

- **Sell when the economics change or he was wrong.** *Revealed, in his own words: Tesco (2014 letter), airlines (2020 meeting), IBM (2017 meeting; a partial sale at that point; the archive has no statement in his words of a full exit).*
  > An attentive investor, I’m embarrassed to report, would have sold Tesco shares earlier. I made a big mistake with this investment by dawdling.
  > — `brk-letter-2014` · 2015-02-27 · `private/letters/brk-2014.txt`

  > But the world changed for airlines.
  > — `agm-2020` · 2020-05-02 · `private/agm/agm-2020.txt`

  > But we have sold the entire positions.
  > — `agm-2020` · 2020-05-02 · `private/agm/agm-2020.txt`

  > I made a large investment in IBM, and — which has not turned out that well.
  > — `agm-2017` · 2017-05-06 · `private/agm/agm-2017.txt`

- **Sell when price reaches value.** *Revealed; PetroChina, 2007 letter.*
  > In the second half of last year, the market value of the company rose to $275 billion, about what we thought it was worth compared to other giant oil companies. So we sold our holdings for $4 billion.
  > — `brk-letter-2007` · 2008-02-29 · `private/letters/brk-2007.txt`

- **Deferred capital-gains tax works like an interest-free loan, which raises the bar for selling.** *Stated; 1989 letter.*
  > In economic terms, the liability resembles an interest-free loan from the U.S. Treasury that comes due only at our election
  > — `brk-letter-1989` · 1990-03-02 · `private/letters/brk-1989.txt`

## 9. Risk & temperament

- **Avoid permanent loss of capital.** *Stated; October 1967 partnership letter, explaining why he would not chase a style he didn't understand.*
  > an approach which I don’t fully understand, have not practiced successfully and which, possibly, could lead to substantial permanent loss of capital.
  > — `bpl-letter-1967-oct` · 1967-10-09 · `private/partnership/bpl-letter-1967-oct.txt`

- **Be fearful when others are greedy.** *Stated in the 1986 letter; applied publicly in October 2008, when he bought U.S. stocks for his personal account during the crisis.*
  > we simply attempt to be fearful when others are greedy and to be greedy only when others are fearful.
  > — `brk-letter-1986` · 1987-02-27 · `private/letters/brk-1986.txt`

  > A simple rule dictates my buying: Be fearful when others are greedy, and be greedy when others are fearful.
  > — `nyt-2008-buy-american` · 2008-10-16 · `private/articles/nyt-2008.txt`

  > In short, bad news is an investor’s best friend. It lets you buy a slice of America’s future at a marked-down price.
  > — `nyt-2008-buy-american` · 2008-10-16 · `private/articles/nyt-2008.txt`

- **Never depend on the kindness of strangers: no leverage, ample cash.** *Stated; 2008 letter.* (The phrase first appears in 1987, but there it describes Berkshire's insurance customers relying on it, not Berkshire's own funding.)
  > We never want to count on the kindness of strangers in order to meet tomorrow’s obligations.
  > — `brk-letter-2008` · 2009-02-27 · `private/letters/brk-2008.txt`

- **No market forecasting. His market-level pieces judge valuation, not timing.** *Stated: Ground Rule 6 in the partnership, 1992 letter, and the 1977 and 1999 Fortune pieces. This reconciles the old lead's contradiction: in 1999 he says up front he is talking about the level of the market, not its next move.*
  > I am not in the business of predicting general stock market or business fluctuations.
  > — `bpl-letter-1966-mid` · 1966-07-12 · `private/partnership/bpl-letter-1966-mid.txt`

  > Forecasts usually tell us more of the forecaster than of the future.
  > — `fortune-1977-inflation` · 1977-05-01 · `private/articles/fortune-1977-inflation.txt`

  > We've long felt that the only value of stock forecasters is to make fortune tellers look good.
  > — `brk-letter-1992` · 1993-03-01 · `private/letters/brk-1992.txt`

  > Though I will be talking about the level of the market, I will not be predicting its next moves.
  > — `fortune-1999-market` · 1999-11-22 · `private/articles/fortune-1999.txt`

## 11. Anti-patterns

A caricature of Buffett, such as the open-source "AI Hedge Fund", tends to get these wrong. Each correction points to the sourced rule.
- "He only buys cheap stocks." True of the partnership era only; from 1989 he says he prefers a wonderful company at a fair price (§5).
- "He never sells." Tesco, PetroChina, the airlines and a partial sale of IBM say otherwise (§8).
- "He never buys tech." He bought IBM and Apple, and explained why (§2).
- Ignoring Berkshire's size, which rules out most small companies from the late 1990s (§2, §10).
- Treating every Berkshire 13F position as Buffett's. Todd Combs (from 2010) and Ted Weschler (from 2012) manage their own portfolios:
  > Todd Combs and Ted Weschler, our new investment managers, have proved to be smart, models of integrity, helpful to Berkshire in many ways beyond portfolio management, and a perfect cultural fit.
  > — `brk-letter-2012` · 2013-03-01 · `private/letters/brk-2012.txt`
- Reciting quotes ("moat", "Mr. Market") instead of doing the analysis the quote points to.
