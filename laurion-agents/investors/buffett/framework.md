## 1. Universe & idea sourcing
- LEAD: In the partnership era, he read the Moody's manuals page by page and screened statistically cheap companies. Check the BPL letters and `superinvestors-1984`.
- LEAD: Berkshire letters from about 1982 print **acquisition criteria**: large purchases, consistent earning power, good returns on equity with little or no debt, management in place, simple businesses, an offering price. Find the first year this appears.
- LEAD: Later letters talk of hunting "elephants" because of Berkshire's size (2000s-2010s letters).

## 2. Filters / gatekeepers
- LEAD: Circle of competence. Stay within businesses you understand and can predict 10+ years out (1996 letter; UF 1998 talk).
- LEAD: Turnarounds seldom turn (1979 or 1980 letter).
- LEAD: Size. A position must be big enough to matter relative to Berkshire's capital. Recent letters repeatedly call size a handicap.
- LEAD: Technology was historically avoided as outside his circle. IBM (2011) and Apple (2016+) are exceptions; find how he justified them.
- LEAD: He avoids businesses that need heavy leverage.

## 3. Business analysis
- LEAD: The primary test of managerial performance is a high return on equity capital employed without undue leverage (1979 letter).
- LEAD: Durable competitive advantage ("economic castle protected by a moat"): 1990s letters and many AGMs.
- LEAD: Pricing power, and businesses that need little incremental capital (See's Candies in the 1983 and 2007 letters).
- LEAD: "The Inevitables" (1996 letter).
- LEAD: Owner earnings (appendix to the 1986 letter).
- LEAD: Look-through earnings (1990-1991 letters).

## 4. Management & capital allocation
- LEAD: Able, honest and owner-oriented management; candor in reporting (acquisition criteria; 1980s letters).
- LEAD: The "institutional imperative" (1989 letter, "Mistakes of the First Twenty-five Years").
- LEAD: Buybacks only below intrinsic value (1984 letter; 1999-2011 letters on Berkshire's own buybacks).
- LEAD: Retained earnings must create at least $1 of market value for each $1 retained (Owner's Manual).

## 5. Valuation
- LEAD: Intrinsic value is the discounted cash that can be taken out of a business over its remaining life (Owner's Manual; 1992 letter).
- LEAD: Growth is part of value, not a separate style (1992 letter).
- LEAD: Discount rate: AGMs mention using the long-term Treasury rate without an equity risk premium. Verify and note the year.
- LEAD: "A wonderful company at a fair price" over "a fair company at a wonderful price" (1989 letter). Graham's margin of safety is the earlier version.
- LEAD: Graham-era methods: net-net working capital and liquidation value (BPL letters, Dempster Mill).
- **Note for the agent:** Buffett said he doesn't build elaborate spreadsheets. Valuation reasoning should look like his, not like a sell-side DCF.

## 6. Catalyst / timing
- LEAD: Berkshire era: usually no catalyst needed; Mr. Market will eventually reprice (1987 letter on Mr. Market).
- LEAD: Partnership era: "workouts" had explicit catalysts (mergers, liquidations) and "controls" were activist (BPL letters describing generals, workouts and controls; ~1964).

## 7. Position sizing & portfolio construction
- LEAD: Concentration. "Diversification is protection against ignorance" (find the source). The partnership allowed up to 40% in one security (BPL letter ~1965-66).
- LEAD: Keeps a large cash floor for insurance obligations (2010s letters on minimum cash). This constrains sizing in later years.

## 8. Sell discipline
- LEAD: "Our favorite holding period is forever" (1988 letter). Some holdings declared permanent (1987 letter).
- LEAD: Sells when the economics have changed or he was wrong: Tesco (2014 letter), airlines (AGM 2020), IBM (AGM 2017-18).
- LEAD: Sells when price far exceeds value: PetroChina (2007 letter).
- LEAD: Taxes. Deferred capital-gains tax works as an interest-free loan, which raises the bar for selling (1989 letter).

## 9. Risk & temperament
- LEAD: Avoiding permanent loss of capital matters more than volatility (letters; "Rule No. 1").
- LEAD: Be fearful when others are greedy (1986 letter); "Buy American. I Am." (`nyt-2008-buy-american`).
- LEAD: Aversion to leverage; "never depend on the kindness of strangers" (2008 and 2010 letters).
- LEAD: Ignores macro forecasts (various AGMs), yet wrote market-level pieces in 1977, 1999 and 2001. Reconcile the two.

## 11. Anti-patterns
A caricature of Buffett, such as the open-source "AI Hedge Fund", tends to get these wrong:
- "He only buys cheap stocks." That's true of the partnership era only; later he paid up for quality.
- "He never sells." Airlines, IBM, Tesco, PetroChina and newspapers say otherwise.
- "He never buys tech." He bought IBM and Apple, with specific reasoning.
- Ignoring Berkshire's size, which rules out most small companies after the 1990s.
- Treating every Berkshire 13F position as Buffett's, when Combs and Weschler manage part of the portfolio after 2010.
- Reciting quotes ("moat", "Mr. Market") instead of doing the analysis the quote points to.
