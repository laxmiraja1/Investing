**Investment Memo: The Coca-Cola Company — Purchase Recommendation (as of February 27, 1989)**

We recommend buying shares of The Coca-Cola Company at around $46.56 and building toward roughly a quarter of our portfolio. This memo explains what the business is, why it passes our tests, what we think it is worth, and what would change our view. It relies only on the company's 1986 and 1987 annual reports and recent price data. We have not seen 1988 results.

At its core, Coca-Cola sells syrup and concentrate to independent and partly owned bottlers in more than 155 countries. The bottlers carry the heavy capital: plants, trucks, coolers. Coca-Cola supplies the formula, the trademark and the marketing. The company's products make up about 44 percent of soft drinks sold worldwide outside the Soviet Union and China, double the volume of the nearest competitor. In the United States it holds over 39 percent of the market and 59 percent of fountain sales. Soft drinks now produce more than 95 percent of operating income. The film business has been spun into a 49-percent-owned affiliate, and the orange-juice business is small and struggling.

The business passes our gatekeeping tests easily. It is not a turnaround: earnings per share rose every year from 1976 to 1987, from $0.79 to $2.43. It is simple, with no technology to misjudge. It is large enough to matter at $916 million of annual net income. Our main caution is debt. Gross debt rose to 45.6 percent of capital in 1987, but net of excess cash and receivables it is 15.3 percent, and it funded share repurchases, not operations. The business itself does not need to borrow.

Our first test of quality is return on equity, not growth in per-share earnings. Coca-Cola has earned roughly 19 to 21 percent on equity for more than a decade, rising to 27 percent in 1987. Part of the recent rise comes from buybacks and borrowing, so we looked through the consolidated figures. In 1987 the soft-drink operations earned about 34 percent pretax on their identifiable assets, and the international business about 52 percent. About $2.3 billion of the company's capital sits in bottling and film affiliates earning around 5 percent. Strip those out and the core concentrate business earns, by our rough estimate, 55 to 60 percent after tax on the capital it uses. Very few large businesses do that.

These returns are protected by a competitive advantage that is widening rather than narrowing. The trademark is the best known in the world. Gross margins on US concentrate are about 63 percent. New bottling contracts let the company set prices at its discretion, and management says it can raise prices to offset inflation. In 1987 the US cola lead reached its widest gap in more than ten years. International consumption per person is only 16 percent of the US level, which leaves a long runway. The franchise even survived management's own 1985 formula change: the original product, reintroduced as Coca-Cola classic, became the best-selling soft drink in the country within a year.

Management passes, with reservations. Roberto Goizueta and Donald Keough have run the company since 1981. They made mistakes, chiefly issuing stock to buy Columbia Pictures and then the formula change. But they have reversed most of the diversification, stated an explicit 12 percent cost-of-capital hurdle, and refocused on the core. The best evidence is the retained-earnings test. From 1982 through 1987 the company kept $5.18 per share of earnings it did not pay out, and the share price rose from $11.58 to $38.13 on an essentially unchanged share count. Even ignoring the higher multiple the market now pays, each retained dollar produced about two dollars of value. We are less comfortable with the reports' promotional tone, the habit of justifying buybacks by "excess cash" rather than by price against value, and the use of borrowing to lower the cost of capital. Unlike in some of our other holdings, we have no first-hand knowledge of these managers.

On value, the company fails the old test of buying below net working capital, which is essentially zero. That test is the wrong yardstick for a business like this. The question we ask is whether the price makes sense if the future looks like the past. We estimate 1987 owner earnings at $2.04 to $2.43 per share, depending on how much capital spending is needed just to maintain the business. At today's price that is a starting yield of 4.4 to 5.2 percent. If owner earnings grow at 12 percent for ten years, in line with the 11 to 13 percent record, that yield on today's price rises to 14 to 16 percent. Discounting such a stream at an assumed 9 percent long-term rate (the packet gives no interest rates) gives a value of about $94 to $112 per share. Cutting growth to 8 percent gives $68 to $81. Only if slow growth coincides with a 12 percent required return does value fall to the current price. Most of our margin of safety therefore rests on how predictable the franchise is, not on a statistical discount.

The position should be large but not at our 40 percent maximum, because 1988 results are unseen and the margin narrows at higher interest rates. We would stop adding if the price moved well above our estimate of value. We would reconsider the holding itself only if the business changed: local-currency volume or profit stalling, owner earnings proving much lower than estimated, renewed acquisitions outside soft drinks paid for in stock, debt beyond the stated target, or evidence that management's integrity falls short. A rising share price alone would not be a reason to sell. If our judgment of the business is right, this is a holding we would expect to keep indefinitely.
