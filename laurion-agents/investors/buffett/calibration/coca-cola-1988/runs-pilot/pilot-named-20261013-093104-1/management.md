# Stage 3: Management and capital allocation (Framework §4)

The three §4 rules are applied in order. Share issuance and acquisitions fall under rule 1's "able" test and draw on `brk-letter-1982#17`, so they are covered there.

---

## 4.1 Honest, able management in place; candid reporting

**In place.** Roberto Goizueta (Chairman and CEO) and Donald Keough (President and COO) have run the company since the 1981 team was "assembled" (`ar-1987`). The CFO is M. Douglas Ivester, and the international, US and foods sectors each have named heads. The framework's requirement is "management in place (we can't supply it)" (`brk-letter-1982`), and this is met.

**Ability: the record since 1981.**

*Core business:*
- ROE rose from 19.9% (1981) to 27.2% (1987).
- Soft-drink operating margins rose from 18.3% (1985) to 23.0% (1987).
- International volume grew 9–12% a year in each of the last three years.
- US per-capita servings went from 203 (1980) to 274 (1987).
- The bottler system was restructured to make it stronger (CCE, TCC, the British joint venture, consolidation of German bottlers).
- *Assessment:* strong.

*Diversification, the main mistakes:*
- **Columbia Pictures (1982).** Average shares rose from 372M in 1981 to 408M in 1983, so Coca-Cola stock was issued to buy a film studio. By 1986 entertainment earned about 10.8% pretax on identifiable assets ($235M on $2,170M), against about 34% for soft drinks. After the 1987 restructuring, Coke's 49% stake in CPE earned $48.9M on a $989M carrying value, about 5%.
- **Embassy/Tandem (1985).** 7.1M more shares were issued, along with $178M in cash.
- **Foods.** Earnings fell, a $36M charge was taken, and senior management at the unit was replaced in 1987.
- **New Coke (1985).** The formula change cost a year of extraordinary marketing spending.

`brk-letter-1982#17` warns that issuing shares to buy a weaker business costs owners twice. Coke did this with Columbia. The packet does not give Columbia's price, so I cannot measure the dilution.

*Course correction:*
- By 1987 management had spun 51% of the entertainment business out to shareholders as a $335M in-kind dividend.
- It sold 51% of US and Canadian bottling to the public.
- It now describes the soft-drink business as "more than 95 percent of operating income."
- It says it will be "preferring investments within our existing lines of business," judged against an explicit 12% after-tax cost of capital (`ar-1987`).

Net shares outstanding are back at 372M, the 1981 level, so the buybacks offset the earlier issuance. The management that made the diversification mistakes has largely reversed them. That is a point in its favor, though only partly.

**Candor.** The standard is "we don't expect a public relations document" (`brk-letter-1979`), together with Berkshire's own practice of spelling out its mistakes (`brk-letter-1985#6`, the textile shutdown).

*In Coke's favor:*
- One-time items are separated prominently, and management publishes ex-item growth figures (EPS +17.4%).
- It states its cost-of-capital hurdle.
- It says plainly that Columbia's films were "below expectations" (1986) and that Foods "is obviously in transition" (1987).

*Against Coke:*
- The 1985 formula change appears only as "the effort begun in 1985 to revitalize our sugar colas." It is never called a mistake.
- The 1987 report includes promotional filler, such as the system's sales "added an estimated $250 billion to the world economy" using a "multiplier factor of five."
- Both letters lead with total stock return against the S&P 500.
- Gains from selling subsidiary stock ($375M in 1986, $40M in 1987) are booked in earnings.
- There have been "one-time" provisions in two consecutive years and three restatement bases in two years.

These reports are more promotional than the standard calls for, but nothing in them looks misleading, because the material facts are disclosed.

## 4.2 Buybacks only well below intrinsic value

**Record (shares and cost from the equity statements):**

| Year | Shares bought | Cost | Avg price | P/E on that year's clean EPS |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1984 | 18.4M | $335.7M | ~$18.24 | ~11.6× ($1.57) |
| 1985 | 15.0M | $379.9M | ~$25.33 | ~14.7× ($1.72) |
| 1986 | 2.9M | $110.5M | ~$38.37 | ~18.5× (~$2.07 ex-items) |
| 1987 | 14.1M | $605.1M | ~$42.6 | ~17.5× ($2.43) |

In July 1987 the company authorized up to 40M shares (more than 10% of the company) to be bought by 1990. By year-end 6.6M had been bought at about $42.

**How this compares to the rule.** The rule says repurchases "at prices well below per-share intrinsic business value" create value (`brk-letter-1984`).

- Management's stated reason is about available funds, not about price relative to value. Repurchases "commenced after management determined that the Company's available cash and debt capacity exceeded the funds required" (`ar-1987`). In 1986 they were described as "reflecting our confidence."
- The 1987 letter comes closest to the right reasoning: "Often the best use of excess cash … has been to invest in our own stock."
- I find no statement that the stock was bought because it was cheap compared with what the business is worth.
- The buyback price has risen from about 12× to about 18× earnings. The 1987 purchases at about $42.6 bought owner earnings of $2.04–2.43 (Stage 2), a yield of about 4.8–5.7%.
- Whether that was "well below" intrinsic value depends on the Stage 4 valuation.
- For a business earning about 55–60% after tax on the capital its core operations employ, and growing, buying at 17–18× may well have been below value. But management did not show that reasoning.
- **Provisional verdict:** the 1984–85 purchases at about 12–15× look sound. The later purchases are probably sound if Stage 4 values the business well above 19×. The stated logic, "excess cash and debt capacity," is weaker than the framework requires.

**Leverage used to fund buybacks.** Management raised its net debt-to-capital target to 35% to "reduce the weighted average cost of capital." Gross debt/capital went from 9.1% (1981) to 45.6% (1987). That is the shoring-up-returns-with-debt pattern described in `fortune-1977-inflation`.

In practice:
- Net debt/capital is 15.3%.
- Operating income covers gross interest 4.7× ($1,324M vs. $279M).
- Net interest expense is only about $72M.

The leverage is affordable and is not needed for the business to operate. It is still a theory-driven policy that the framework's "little or no debt" preference (`brk-letter-1982`) would view with some caution.

## 4.3 Each $1 retained must create at least $1 of market value

The test is "whether retention, over time, delivers shareholders at least $1 of market value for each $1 retained" (`brk-letter-1983`). The period is the current management's tenure, end-1981 to now. Shares outstanding are about 372M at both ends, so per-share figures equal company totals.

| Per share | End-1981 to end-1987 |
|---|---|
| Net income 1982–87 (1.32+1.37+1.59+1.84+2.42+2.43) | $10.97 |
| Cash dividends 1982–87 (0.83+0.89+0.92+0.99+1.04+1.12) | $5.79 |
| **Retained** | **$5.18** (≈ $1.93B in total) |
| Market price change, $11.58 → $38.13 (end-1987) | +$26.55 (≈ $9.9B) |
| Market price change, $11.58 → $46.56 (now) | +$34.98 (≈ $13.0B) |

Each $1 retained produced about **$5–7 of market value**. That is before counting the $0.90 CPE in-kind dividend, which was also value delivered to owners.

**Stricter check.** Part of the gain comes from a re-rating: the P/E was about 8.9× in 1981 ($11.58 / $1.30) and is about 19× now. Applying the 1981 multiple to 1987 EPS of $2.43 gives about $21.60. That is still about $10 above the 1981 price, against $5.18 retained, or roughly **$2 per $1 retained** with no re-rating at all. Earnings grew about $1.13 per share on $5.18 retained, an incremental return of roughly 22% on retained earnings even after the low-return detours.

**Policy going forward.** Management is lowering the payout ratio toward 40% so it can retain more. The business's returns support retaining more, but only if the extra retention goes into the core business or into buybacks at sensible prices. The roughly $2.3B already in bottling and film affiliates earning about 5% would fail this test if looked at alone.

**Verdict:** a clear pass over the full management tenure.

## Where the investor would have relied on information the packet lacks

1. **A personal judgment of the managers.** The framework's judgments of character come from long acquaintance and direct dealing: "tops in both ability and integrity" for Murphy and Burke (`brk-letter-1984#31`), the handshake with Mrs. B (`brk-letter-1984#7`), and "people whom we strongly like and admire" (`brk-letter-1987#18`). I cannot judge Goizueta's and Keough's integrity, temperament, or attitude toward a large outside shareholder from annual reports alone.
2. **The proxy statement:** insider and director share ownership (whether they "eat their own cooking," as in `brk-letter-1983#0`), salaries, and the targets behind the incentive plans.
3. **Prices paid for acquisitions**, especially Columbia (1982), which is needed to measure the value lost or gained from that share issuance.
4. **Buyback activity and prices in 1988**, and any statement management made about price relative to value.
5. **Management's planned uses of the roughly $1.2B of unused borrowing capacity** and the rest of the 40M-share authorization.
6. **Succession and the ages of the senior managers.**
7. **The 1988 annual report** and the most recent letter from management.

## Stage 3 conclusion

Management passes §4, with reservations.

- **Strengths:** The team is able and has been in place since 1981. It treats the soft-drink business as the core, runs it with an explicit cost-of-capital discipline, and has reversed most of its diversification mistakes. Shares outstanding are back at 1981 levels.
- **Retained earnings:** retention has produced roughly $2–7 of market value per $1, depending on whether the re-rating is counted. This is a decisive pass.
- **Reservations:**
  - Buybacks are justified by "excess cash and debt capacity," not by an explicit price-versus-value argument.
  - Leverage was raised on cost-of-capital theory rather than business need.
  - The reports are promotional and stay silent on the 1985 formula mistake.
  - About $2.3B of capital remains in affiliates earning around 5%.
  - The character judgment the investor would base on personal knowledge is not available in the packet.

None of these rules the company out. Whether the current buyback program, and our own purchase, are happening "well below" intrinsic value is the Stage 4 question.
