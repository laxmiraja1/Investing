# Stage 2: Business analysis (Framework §3)

I apply the three §3 rules in the framework's order: return on equity, then the moat, then owner earnings. All figures come from `ar-1986`, `ar-1987` and `fin-summary`, using the 1987 restated basis unless I say otherwise.

---

## 3.1 Return on equity, not EPS growth, is the primary test

The test is "a high earnings rate on equity capital employed (without undue leverage, accounting gimmickry, etc.)" (`brk-letter-1979`). Each of those qualifiers needs checking.

**Reported ROE (income from continuing operations ÷ average equity, `ar-1987` selected data):**

| | 1977 | 1979 | 1981 | 1983 | 1984 | 1985 | 1986 | 1987 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| ROE | 20.6% | 21.1% | 19.9% | 19.4% | 21.8% | 23.5% | 28.8% | 27.2% |
| Total debt / total capital | 3.5% | 6.3% | 9.1% | 15.1% | 30.7% | 27.7% | 31.4% | 45.6% |

ROE never fell below about 19% over eleven years. For scale, `brk-letter-1987#1` notes that only six of the Fortune 1,000 averaged above 30% over a decade.

**Accounting items to strip out:**
- 1986 includes a $375M pretax gain from the CCE share sale (+$0.68/share) and $180M of provisions (−$0.33/share). Management's own ex-items figure implies 1986 clean earnings of about $800M (916.1 ÷ 1.145). On average equity of about $3.25B, clean 1986 ROE is about **24.6%**, not 28.8%.
- 1987 has a $40M gain on the TCC share sale and a $36M Foods provision. These roughly cancel, so 1987's **27.2%** stands.
- Other things that complicate the numbers: "one-time" restructuring provisions in consecutive years; three restatement bases in two years; receivables sold with recourse ($618M uncollected at end-1986); $394M of contingent guarantees; an unconsolidated finance subsidiary that will add about $300M of debt when consolidated in 1988. All of these are disclosed and none changes the economics, but the reported figures have to be cleaned before use.

**Leverage and shrinking equity:** In 1987 the company paid out $421.5M in cash dividends, $335M as the CPE in-kind dividend, and $605M in buybacks. That totals about $1.36B against $916M of earnings, so equity fell from $3,515M to $3,224M. Gross debt/capital rose to 45.6%, though net of excess cash, CPE notes and temporary investments it is 15.3%. Part of the move from about 20% to about 27% ROE is therefore financial engineering, not business improvement.

**The underlying business test.** The more telling measure is what the soft-drink operation earns on the assets it uses:

| Soft drinks (USA + International) | 1985 | 1986 (ex-$180M provisions) | 1987 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Revenue | $4,541M | $5,645M | $6,229M |
| Operating income | $830M | $1,181M | $1,433M |
| Operating margin | 18.3% | 20.9% | 23.0% |
| Pretax return on identifiable assets | — | ~36% | ~34% |

- International alone earned $1,109M on $2,127M of identifiable assets in 1987, about **52% pretax**.
- USA identifiable assets rose by about $620M in 1987. That increase probably includes the Miami bottler held for resale, which was sold in January 1988, so the US return is understated.
- None of these segment returns depends on debt. Rough check: capital employed = equity $3,224M + net debt about $582M = about $3.8B. Of that, about $2.26B is equity-method affiliates (CPE $989M, CCE $749M, TCC $84M, other $435M). That leaves about **$1.55B** running soft drinks, foods and corporate. Those operations earned about $1,360M pretax before provisions, or about $884M after tax at a 35% rate. That is roughly **55–60% after tax**, the same order of magnitude as Berkshire's own seven best units (`brk-letter-1987#1`).
- The $2.26B in affiliates earned $118.5M of equity income, about **5%**. CCE earned $88.4M on $1,526M of net assets, 5.8%, while carrying $2.7B of liabilities. Bottling and film are the low-return, capital-hungry businesses. Burlington is the cautionary example (`brk-letter-1985#9`). Coca-Cola's consolidated ROE is held down by these holdings, not lifted by them.

**EPS for context only:** EPS rose from $1.17 (1981) to $2.43 (1987), about 13% compounded, helped by buybacks. Under the framework this is secondary. The important point is that earnings doubled ($432M → $916M) while equity rose only about $950M ($2,271M → $3,224M), even as large amounts were paid out.

**Verdict on 3.1:** The test is passed strongly at the level of the core business. Consolidated ROE is somewhat flattered by leverage and buybacks and held down by low-return affiliates. The concentrate business itself earns exceptional unleveraged returns.

## 3.2 Durable competitive advantage: is there a moat, and is it being widened?

`brk-letter-1986#16` asks two questions: what protects the castle, and is management widening the moat?

**What the packet shows protects the castle:**
- **Brand and share.** The company calls Coca-Cola "the world's best-known trademark." Company brands are about 44% of world soft drinks outside the USSR and China, and volume is double the nearest competitor's. US share is above 39%, 9 points ahead of the main competitor (1986). In 1986 Coca-Cola classic was the number-one US soft drink and the company had five of the top ten.
- **Fountain and distribution.** US fountain share is 59% (1986), more than 2-to-1 over the nearest rival. The company took about three-quarters of the segment's growth over four years and added 14,000 outlets in 1987. International post-mix volume has grown at double digits for 12 straight years. The bottler system reaches 155+ countries, and management claims "low-cost producer in most of the 155 countries."
- **Pricing power.** Gross margin on concentrate to US bottlers is about 63% (1986). New CCE contracts let the company "adjust prices at its discretion," and it used them in October 1986. International concentrate contracts "generally do not contain restrictions." Management states it "is able to adjust prices to compensate for increasing costs." That is the inflation-resistant, low-capital profile `fortune-1977-inflation` says ordinary businesses lack.
- **Proof of durability under stress.** In 1985 management changed the formula. The original formula, brought back as "Coca-Cola classic," became the best-selling US soft drink by 1986, while the new taste merely "stabilized." The franchise survived a serious management error, and consumers' preference for the unchanged original product is itself evidence of the moat. It also fits "severe change and exceptional returns usually don't mix" (`brk-letter-1987#3`): the product is essentially a century old.
- **Franchise test using the investor's own reasoning on taxes** (`brk-letter-1986#22`). A strong, unregulated franchise keeps the benefit of tax cuts. Soft-drink margins rose from 18.3% to 23.0% as the US rate fell from 46% to 40% (heading to 34%). This is only a partial test: about 75% of pretax income ($1,054M of $1,410M) is earned abroad, so the US cut matters less here.

**Is the moat widening?** On the packet's evidence, yes:
- In 1987 the US food-store cola lead was "the widest gap in more than 10 years."
- US per-capita consumption of company drinks rose from 203 servings (1980) to 274 (1987), with a target of 315 by 1990.
- International per-capita consumption is 16% of the US level, and volume grew 9–12% a year in 1985–87.
- Management is consolidating fragmented bottlers (Germany went from 102 bottlers to 68) to make the system more efficient.

**Limits to the moat, visible in the packet:**
- Volume is price-sensitive at the margin: Germany fell 4% after price increases, and Mexico's price increases "hindered" volume.
- Latin America is "tremendous unit volume and low profit margins," and hyperinflation is a constant problem there.
- Keeping the castle costs money every year: $720M in media advertising in 1987, plus cooperative advertising for US bottlers of about 19–21% of related revenue (1986).
- Standard contracts with most US bottlers other than CCE use formula pricing for Coca-Cola syrup, which limits domestic pricing discretion.
- FX gave a large tailwind: key currencies rose 27% in 1986 and 16% in 1987, and about 77% of soft-drink operating income is earned abroad. Part of the recent profit growth is currency, not franchise.
- Moving into film and orange juice diluted the franchise. Foods earned about 16% pretax on its assets in 1987 even before its $36M charge, against 34%+ for soft drinks. This is mostly a capital-allocation question, taken up in §4.

**Verdict on 3.2:** The packet shows a wide moat that management is actively widening, built on brand, distribution scale and pricing power. It is not a cost moat in the GEICO sense; the packet makes the "low-cost producer" claim but gives no competitor cost figures to verify it.

## 3.3 Owner earnings

The definition from `brk-letter-1986` is: (a) reported earnings + (b) depreciation, amortization and other non-cash charges − (c) average capital spending needed to maintain competitive position and volume.

The packet does not split capital spending between maintenance and growth, so I show a range.

| $M | 1985 | 1986 | 1987 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Earnings, cleaned of one-off gains and provisions | 677.6* | ~800 | ~916 |
| + Depreciation & goodwill amortization | 133.4 | 154.4 | 153.5 |
| − Total capital expenditure | (411.8) | (345.8) | (299.6) |
| **Owner earnings, treating all capex as required** | ~399 | ~609 | **~770** |
| Per share (average shares) | $1.01 | $1.57 | **$2.04** |

*1985 has not been cleaned of about $67M of pretax bottling-sale gains, and its capex still includes bottling assets.

**Adjustments around the 1987 midpoint:**
- *Generous:* if required capex is about equal to depreciation, owner earnings are about $916M, or **$2.43/share**. Much of the spending is plainly growth: international infrastructure, a new US syrup and concentrate network, and corporate buildings and computers ($73.9M of capex against $22.0M of corporate depreciation).
- *Conservative:* deduct the $79.7M of equity income not received as cash dividends, and the $28.7M by which cash taxes exceeded book taxes. That gives about $660–690M, or **$1.75–1.83/share**.
- *Capital intensity of the core:* soft-drink capex was $170M in 1987 against soft-drink operating income of $1,433M, about 12%. Very little of what this business earns has to be reinvested just to stand still.
- *Trend:* capex has fallen from 3.1× depreciation (1985) to 1.95× (1987) as bottling moved into 49%-owned affiliates. Owner earnings per share roughly doubled in two years. Part of that comes from moving capital-hungry bottling off the parent's books, not from the franchise itself.

My working figure is **1987 owner earnings of about $2.00–2.40 per share**. The unknown maintenance share of capex is the main reason the range is that wide.

## Facts the framework wants that the packet lacks

1. **Any 1988 results.** As of February 1989 the 1988 report should exist, but the packet stops at fiscal 1987. Every figure above is about 14 months old, and I am assuming 1988 continued the 1987 trend. That is an assumption, not evidence.
2. **Maintenance vs. growth capex**, and the core business's working-capital needs. These are needed to pin down owner earnings (c).
3. **Soft-drink segment liabilities**, needed for a true return on net capital employed rather than return on identifiable assets.
4. **Competitor figures** (the main rival's margins, costs, advertising spend). These are needed to test the "low-cost producer" claim the way GEICO's expense ratio was tested.
5. **Profit growth in local currency**, to separate franchise growth from the 1986–87 dollar decline.
6. **Concentrate gross margins and price history outside the US**, and the term structure of US bottler contracts.
7. **Long-run (10+ year) share and volume history.** The per-capita chart in `ar-1987` is unreadable in the packet.
8. **The current-cost (inflation-adjusted) earnings figure.** Management says it is lower than reported but gives no number.
9. **Dividends received from affiliates by investee**, to compute look-through earnings precisely.

---

## Stage 2 conclusion

On the §3 tests, the core business is of the highest quality. ROE has stayed around 20% or more for more than a decade with little debt until recently. The concentrate operation earns roughly 55–60% after tax on the capital it actually uses. The moat rests on brand, distribution and pricing power and is visibly widening. Owner earnings are high relative to reported earnings because the core needs little capital.

Two qualifications carry forward. Reported ROE is partly flattered by leverage and buybacks, and about $2.3B of capital sits in bottling and film affiliates earning around 5%. Both belong to §4, management and capital allocation, rather than counting against the franchise.
