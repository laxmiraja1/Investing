# Stage 4: Valuation (Framework §5) and Catalyst / timing (§6)

## 5.1 The framework's stated valuation rule: buy below net working capital

The only rule in §5 is the Partnership-era bargain test: net working capital per share, "before placing any value on the plants and equipment" (`bpl-letter-1965`).

| End-1987, $M (`ar-1987` balance sheet) | |
|---|---|
| Total current assets | 4,136.2 |
| Total current liabilities | (4,118.5) |
| **Net working capital** | **17.7**, about **$0.05/share** |
| Price, 1989-02-24 | **$46.56** |

Coca-Cola fails this test completely. Book value is $8.66 a share, so the stock trades at about 5.4× book. Taken literally, the framework's valuation section gives a clear no.

**Why this is not the test in force on 1989-02-27.** By this date the investor's own writings have set the net-working-capital method aside for businesses of this kind:

- "Our goal is to find an outstanding business at a sensible price, not a mediocre business at a bargain price… In the interim, I searched for 'bargains' – and had the misfortune to find some" (`brk-letter-1987#18`).
- "We may very well pay a fairly fancy price for a Category 1 business if we are reasonably confident of what we are getting." The test is to buy "at prices that will make sense, even should the future of the acquired enterprise develop much along the lines of its past" (`brk-letter-1981#3`).
- The yardstick is per-share **intrinsic business value** (`brk-letter-1983#0`, `brk-letter-1985#2`). Owner earnings (§3) are the earnings figure that intrinsic value is built on. Businesses like this one are "princes" bought "at toad-like prices" only occasionally (`brk-letter-1981#3`). That is why the price has to be checked against value, not against the balance sheet.

So I record the net-working-capital failure, then apply the test that governed at the as-of date. That test asks whether the price makes sense if the future develops along the lines of the past. It is a check against the record, not a set of forecasts. The framework treats "future projections" as of "little interest" (`brk-letter-1986#10`).

**Missing from the packet, with my assumptions:**

1. **1988 results.** I assume they are in line with the trend, but I value on the 1987 base, which is conservative because it ignores a year of growth. The only 1988 fact in the packet is the $1.20 dividend.
2. **Long-term government bond yield.** This is the investor's natural alternative and is not given. I assume **about 9%** and also show management's own **12%** cost of capital, which is in the packet.
3. **The maintenance/growth split of capex.** I bracket it with the two owner-earnings bases above.

**Affiliates and debt.** Equity income from the bottling and film affiliates is already inside owner earnings. So I do not add separately management's figure of about $2.3B ($6.20/share) for the market value of those affiliates. Owner earnings are measured after interest, so net debt of about $582M (about $1.56/share) is already reflected.

### Cross-check without a discount rate: the rising coupon

At $46.56, the buyer gets an owner-earnings yield of **4.4–5.2%**. If owner earnings compound at 12% for ten years, the 1997 owner earnings come to $6.34–7.55 a share. That is a **13.6–16.2% yield on today's purchase price**. A bond at an assumed 9% has a fixed coupon. Here the business's growth is paid for out of retained earnings that earn very high returns (Stage 2: about 55–60% after tax on the capital the core business uses). Very little of what the business earns has to be put back in just to stand still.

The roughly 19× trailing P/E (about 17× on an assumed trend 1988) is therefore a reasonable price for this business, not a stretched one, as long as the record holds.

## 5.3 Margin of safety against the hurdle

**The hurdle.** For businesses of this quality the framework sets no numeric threshold. Its tests are a "comfortable margin of safety" (`bpl-letter-1962#4`) and a price that "will make sense" if the past continues (`brk-letter-1981#3`). The net-working-capital hurdle is not met and is not the governing test (see 5.1).

**The result:**

- **Past-continues test at a bond-like rate (Case A):** value is $94–112, a **margin of about 50–58%**. This is comfortable.
- **Even with growth cut by a third (Case B):** the margin is still about **32–43%**.
- **At management's 12% hurdle (Case C):** the margin falls to about **9–24%**. It goes negative only when slow growth is combined with a high discount rate (Case D).
- **Reading:** the price makes sense if the future resembles the past, and it leaves a real cushion against a moderate shortfall. It does **not** protect against both a sharply weaker franchise and a high required return at the same time.

That risk rests mainly on the durability of the moat, which Stage 2 found strong and widening, rather than on the price. This matches the framework's reasoning: for a "prince," the margin of safety comes as much from the predictability of the business as from the discount (`bpl-letter-1965` concentration rule: "very low probability that anything could drastically change the underlying value").

**Uncertainties that could move value:**

- About 77% of soft-drink operating income is earned abroad, so a stronger dollar would reverse part of the 1986–87 tailwind.
- 1988 is unseen.
- Owner earnings range by about ±10% depending on the maintenance-capex assumption.

None of these reverses the conclusion in Cases A, B or E.

## 6. Catalyst / timing

- **No catalyst needed** (`brk-letter-1987`): "The market may ignore business success for a while, but eventually will confirm it." Coca-Cola is a "general"-type holding, an outstanding business bought for its compounding. Nothing has to happen to unlock value. The company's own program to repurchase 40M shares (more than 10% of the company) by 1990 will keep increasing each remaining share's claim on the business while we hold.
- **Work-out** (`bpl-letter-1957`): not applicable. There is no announced merger, liquidation or tender. The bottler and CPE spin-outs are finished.
- **Control** (`bpl-letter-1963`): not applicable. At a $17.3B market value, a meaningful Berkshire stake would be a few percent of the company, which is a voice at most, not control.
- **Mr. Market and temperament** (`brk-letter-1987`, `brk-letter-1986`):
  - The stock is at $46.56, near the top of its 52-week range ($35.04–49.12) and about 30% above most of the 1988 closes.
  - Better prices were available during the October 1987 low of $28.96 and through 1988 at about $35–38.
  - The framework does not allow waiting for a pullback: "I am not in the business of predicting general stock market or business fluctuations" (`bpl-letter-1966-mid`). The test is valuation, not timing.
  - Nothing in the packet suggests a speculative mania in this stock. The P/E is moderate for the business's quality.
  - A recent rise is not a reason to buy, and it is not a reason to pass.

## Stage 4 conclusion

- **Net working capital test:** fails decisively. Coca-Cola is not a Graham bargain, and that rule does not govern this kind of business on the as-of date.
- **Governing test** ("sensible price for an outstanding business," judged on owner earnings with the past extended forward):
  - At $46.56 the price is about **42–50% of value** under past-continues growth and an assumed bond-like 9% rate.
  - It is about 57–68% of value even with growth cut to 8%.
  - It is about 76–91% of value at management's 12% hurdle.
- **Margin of safety:** comfortable against any single reasonable shortfall. It runs out only if a weakening franchise and a high required rate occur together.
- **Timing:** no catalyst is needed and timing is not a factor.

The valuation supports buying. The amount depends on how certain the facts are and on Berkshire's capital, which is the position-sizing stage.
