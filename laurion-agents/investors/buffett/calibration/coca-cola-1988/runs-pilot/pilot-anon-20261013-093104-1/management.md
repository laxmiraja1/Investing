# Stage 3: Management and capital allocation (Section 4)

## 4.1 Honest, able management already in place; candid reporting

**Management is in place.** The current team was assembled in 1981, and the 1987 letter says so explicitly (`ar-1987`). The acquisition criterion "management in place (we can't supply it)" (`brk-letter-1986#10`) is met.

**Operating ability.** The packet supports it. Under this team:
- ROE rose from 19.9% (1981) to 27.2% (1987).
- Soft drink operating margin rose from 18.3% (1985) to 23.0% (1987).
- Share gains were broad. US share reached a record of more than 39%, and fountain share reached 59%.
- US operating income rose 10% in 1987 on 1% shipment growth, driven by efficiency (`ar-1986`, `ar-1987`).

These people run the core business well.

**Capital allocation record.** It is mixed early and improves later.

**Distributions in aggregate (1984–87).**
- Earnings totalled $3,201M.
- Distributions totalled $3,344M: cash dividends $1,578M, the CPE in-kind dividend $335M at carrying value, and buybacks $1,431M.

Management has handed back more than 100% of earnings over four years. It has not been hoarding retained cash. Debt and asset sales funded the investments.

**Dividend policy.** Management is cutting the payout toward 40% "to reinvest more substantially in opportunities offering returns in excess of the cost of capital." The core business needs little capital (1987 capex $300M against D&A of $154M). The extra retention will therefore go mainly into buybacks or affiliates, and its value depends on which. That leads to the tests in 4.2 and 4.3.

**Candor in reports.** The standard is "We don't expect a public relations document" (`brk-letter-1979`) and "tell you the business facts that we would want to know if our positions were reversed" (`brk-letter-1983#2`).

*In favour of candor:*
- Currency effects are quantified every year, with tables of exchange-rate changes.
- Unusual items are broken out per share. The 1987 letter separates out "35 cents per share of unusual items in 1986."
- The cost of capital (12% after tax) and the debt ceiling (35% net) are stated openly.
- The reports admit film studio results were "below expectations" with a leadership change, and call Foods "obviously in transition" with a "decline in earnings."
- The inflation note warns that monetary gains "do not necessarily imply that incurring more debt would be beneficial."

*Against:*
- The reports are heavily promotional: "$50 billion in sales added an estimated $250 billion to the world economy" via a "multiplier factor of five," "the potential appears limitless," "we are assured of our abilities to quickly seize opportunities."
- Shareholders are pointed to stock-price total return (38% in 1986, 25% a year since 1981) instead of business value.
- Gains from IPOs of subsidiaries ($375M, $40M) are run through income and celebrated as "creative, decisive actions."
- The 1985 formula change is described as "the effort begun in 1985 to revitalize our sugar colas." That is a gloss on a major mistake.
- In 1986 entertainment was described as bound into "a single, powerful enterprise." In 1987 it was spun out with no account of what changed.

The reports are substantively informative but clearly fall short of the framework's standard of candor. I found nothing suggesting dishonesty. The audit opinions are clean and the disclosures reconcile.

**Compensation and ownership.** The packet shows only plan mechanics:
- Stock option plans covering 8M shares, with 5.6M options outstanding.
- A restricted stock plan of 3M shares.
- Performance units on 555,000 shares, paying cash on stock price above a $20.63 base.

Annual dilution from employee issuance is about 1.2–1.3M shares, roughly 0.35%, which is modest. Pay tied to share price rather than reported EPS is at least directionally aligned with owners. The packet does not give salary or bonus levels, the officers' or directors' own holdings, or how much of their net worth is in the stock. That is the framework's "we eat our own cooking" test (`brk-letter-1983#0`), and I cannot apply it.

## 4.2 Buybacks only well below intrinsic value

| Year | Shares (M) | Cost ($M) | Avg price | EPS that year | P/E paid |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1984 | 18.4 | 336 | $18.24 | $1.57 | 11.6x |
| 1985 | 15.0 | 380 | $25.33 | $1.72 | 14.7x |
| 1986 | 2.9 | 110 | $38.37 | ~$2.07 (ex-items) | ~18.5x |
| 1987 | 14.1 | 605 | ~$42.80 | $2.43 | 17.6x |

A further 40M-share program (more than 10% of shares) runs to 1990. By end-1987, 6.6M shares had been bought under it at about $42.

**Reading against the framework.** The buybacks are large and sustained. That is the demonstrated pro-shareholder behaviour `brk-letter-1984#4` rewards. Every purchase was below today's $46.56, so all of them have been accretive to date. The 1984–85 purchases at 12–15x earnings, for a business Stage 2 found earns more than 25% on capital, were very likely well below value.

The stated rationale, however, is cash and debt capacity exceeding needs, not price against value. The 1987 purchases at about 17.6x look less like "well below" value. Management also kept buying near highs (Q3 1987 range $42.88–53.13). The packet does not show whether they bought heavily at the October 1987 lows ($28.96). Part of the program is debt-funded: short-term debt rose $1.2B in 1987. With net debt at 15% this is acceptable, but it is something to watch.

Whether the buybacks clear "well below intrinsic value" depends on the Stage 4 value estimate. At this stage they pass.

## 4.3 Each $1 retained must create at least $1 of market value

I measure this per share from 1981 year-end (team assembled) to 1987 year-end, the last audited figure.

- Net income per share, 1982–87: $1.32 + 1.37 + 1.59 + 1.84 + 2.42 + 2.43 = **$10.97**
- Dividends: cash $5.79 plus in-kind $0.90 = **$6.69**
- **Retained: about $4.28 per share**
- Market price: $11.58 → $38.13, a gain of **$26.55**, or **about 6.2 times** the amount retained. Measured to the as-of price of $46.56 (+$34.98), the multiple is about 8x even if 1988 retention is around $1.50.

Much of this comes from the market re-rating the stock, from about 8.9x to 19.2x earnings. To remove that, hold the 1981 multiple constant: $2.43 × 8.9 ≈ $21.60. That is a gain of about $10 on $4.28 retained, **about 2.3 times**. Measured another way, EPS rose $1.26 on $4.28 retained, about 29% incremental return per share.

The test passes clearly on any reasonable basis. It passes because the core business is so strong, despite the diversification. The low-return entertainment and bottler capital drags on the result and does not drive it.

## What the investor would have relied on that the packet lacks

1. **Direct personal judgment of the CEO and president.** The framework weights "people we like and admire" heavily (`brk-letter-1987#18`). The packet gives no access to their character, their private view of past errors (formula change, entertainment), or how they think about intrinsic value when they buy back stock.
2. **The proxy statement.** Salaries, bonus formulas, option grant sizes, and insider and director share ownership.
3. **1988 buyback execution.** Shares bought, prices paid, and how purchases were paced against the 1987 crash lows and the 1988 trading range of about $35–40.
4. **Terms and post-mortems on the film studio and Embassy deals.** The value given against the value received.
5. **Management's intentions.** Their plans for the unused debt capacity of about $1.2B (more buybacks against new acquisitions) and their attitude toward a large, passive long-term holder.
6. **Succession and depth** beyond the two top officers.

## Stage 3 conclusion

Management is able at running the core business. Its capital allocation started poorly, with diversification paid partly in stock, and has visibly turned toward owners: exits, refocus, more than 100% of earnings returned in 1984–87, and a large ongoing buyback program. The $1-retained test passes by a wide margin even after removing the market's re-rating. The weaker points are:
- Promotional reporting that falls short of the framework's standard of candor.
- Affiliate stakes earning below management's own hurdle rate.
- Buybacks justified by surplus cash rather than by price against value.
- No packet data on pay or insider ownership.

None of these disqualifies the company. Stage 2 also showed that the franchise survived a serious management error, so the business depends less on management than most. Management is acceptable to good. It is not yet shown to be a "permanent holding" calibre partner of the kind described in `brk-letter-1987#18`; that judgment would come from the personal assessment I cannot make.
