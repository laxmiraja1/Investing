**Memo: Purchase of Company A common stock, as of February 27, 1989**

We recommend buying Company A common stock and building the position over time toward about a quarter of the equity portfolio. At the current price of $46.56 that means roughly half a billion to a billion dollars. The reasoning follows the order we always use: what the business is, whether it passes our filters, what it is worth, and what would change our view.

Company A sells soft-drink syrups and concentrates to bottlers in more than 155 countries. That activity produces over 95 percent of its operating income. It is a simple business. The company makes a flavoring, protects and advertises a trademark, and lets independent and partly owned bottlers handle the capital-heavy work of filling and trucking. It sells more than 44 percent of the world's soft drinks, about twice the volume of its nearest competitor. In the United States it holds 39 percent of the market against roughly 30 percent for its main rival, and 59 percent of fountain sales. Gross margin on concentrate is about 63 percent. Capital spending in 1987 was $300 million, under 4 percent of revenue.

The company passes our filters without difficulty. It is not a turnaround. Return on equity has been near 20 percent every year since 1976 and was 27 percent in 1987. Earnings have grown each year, from $278 million in 1976 to $916 million in 1987. We checked carefully whether the recent rise in returns came from borrowing. It did not. Measured against total net capital with interest added back, the return is still about 25 percent. In the late 1970s, when debt was under 10 percent of capital, the company earned around 20 percent on equity. The capital tied up in the core concentrate business earns well above 50 percent. Those results compare with the best businesses we own outright. The strongest evidence that the advantage lasts comes from management's own error. In 1985 the company changed its flagship formula, and consumers forced the original back. Within a year the restored product was the best-selling soft drink in the country. A franchise that survives a mistake of that size does not depend on perfect stewardship. There is also a long runway: international consumption per person is about one-sixth of the American level.

Management deserves a qualified endorsement. The team that took over in 1981 has run the core business very well, widening margins and gaining share. Its capital allocation started badly. In the early 1980s it moved into film and television, paying partly with its own valuable stock, for businesses that earn far less. Since then it has reversed course. It sold the plastics and pasta businesses, spun the entertainment business off to shareholders, and repurchased about 50 million shares since 1984, with authority for 40 million more by 1990. From 1984 through 1987 it returned more than all of its earnings to shareholders through dividends and buybacks. Each dollar retained per share since 1981 is matched by roughly six dollars of added market value, and by more than two dollars even if we assume the stock's valuation multiple never rose. We have two reservations. The annual reports read more like promotion than plain reporting. And about $2.3 billion is invested in bottling and entertainment affiliates earning around 5 percent, below the company's own stated 12 percent hurdle. Neither changes our conclusion.

The stock fails the old test of selling below net working capital, and it trades at more than five times book value. We no longer apply that test to outstanding businesses. The question we now ask is whether the price makes sense if the company's future simply resembles its past. We estimate owner earnings for the core business at about $1.88 per share for 1987. That is reported earnings, plus depreciation, minus maintenance-level capital spending, minus affiliate earnings the company does not receive in cash. Over the past decade earnings per share grew about 11 percent a year. If that continues for ten years and then slows to 4 or 5 percent, and we discount at an assumed long-term government bond rate of about 9 percent, the core business is worth roughly $68 to $80 per share. Adding the affiliates at carrying value, about $6 per share, gives an intrinsic value of $74 to $86. The current price is 37 to 46 percent below that. Under more cautious assumptions (8 percent growth, a 10 percent discount rate) the value is about $50, still above the price. Put the other way, today's price implies growth of only 3 to 7 percent a year. No catalyst is required. Business results will show up in the price in time, and the company's own buybacks add to per-share value while we wait.

We are not putting in the maximum we would allow in one security, for three reasons. We have no 1988 results. Three-quarters of soft-drink profits are earned abroad, so a stronger dollar would reduce reported earnings. And we have not yet judged the managers in person. We would buy faster if the price returned to the $35 to $40 range seen through most of 1988.

Several things would change our view: 1988 results showing growth in local-currency profits well below the recent 8 percent pace; buybacks abandoned or the company's roughly $1.2 billion of unused borrowing capacity spent on acquisitions outside soft drinks; affiliate values well below their carrying amounts; or a meeting with management that leaves us doubting their candor. We intend to hold the position for the long term. We would sell for lasting damage to the franchise, share issuance on unfair terms, or a loss of trust in the reporting, but not because the stock rises.
