# Stage 2: Business analysis (Section 3, in the framework's order)

## 3.1 Return on equity, not EPS growth, is the primary test

The test is "a high earnings rate on equity capital employed (without undue leverage, accounting gimmickry, etc.)" (`brk-letter-1979`). Stage 1 left the leverage question open, so this step takes the reported figure apart.

**Reported ROE.** Return on average equity was 21.8% in 1984, 23.5% in 1985, 28.8% in 1986 and 27.2% in 1987 (`ar-1987`). The 1986 figure is inflated by a one-off gain. The $375M gain on the CCE stock sale added about $262M after tax, and the $180M provisions took off about $128M. Removing both leaves roughly $800M, or about 25% on average equity. The 1987 figure has little distortion, because the $40M TCC gain and the $36M Foods charge roughly offset each other.

**Removing leverage.** At the end of 1987 the company reported net debt of 15.3% of capital. That implies net debt of about $581M: gross debt of $2,702M, less $1,268M excess cash, $545M of CPE notes (paid January 1988) and $308M of temporary investments (sold January 1988). Net capital is therefore about $3.81B. Net interest was $279M − $207M = $72M pretax, or about $47M after tax. Adding that back gives unlevered earnings of about $963M. On average net capital of about $3.87B, that is a **return of about 25%**. Leverage adds only about 2 points to ROE, so it is not "undue."

**History confirms this.** From 1976 to 1980, debt was 3.5–9.3% of capital and ROE was still 19.7–21.2% (`ar-1986`). The business has never needed borrowed money to earn about 20%.

**Core business versus affiliates.** The consolidated figure understates the core business, because about $2.26B of capital sits in equity-method affiliates: CPE $989M, CCE $749M, TCC $84M, and others $435M. These earned $118M of equity income in 1987, about 5% on carrying value. That is well below the 12% cost of capital management itself states (`ar-1987`). CCE contributed $43M on $749M (5.8%). CPE contributed $49M, about 4–5%. After removing the affiliates and their income, the core business earns roughly $845M on about $1.55B of capital, **well above 50%**. This is a rough estimate because the capital allocation between core and affiliates is approximate, but the direction is not in doubt. The core belongs in the same class as the seven Berkshire units that earned about 57% (`brk-letter-1987#1`). It also beats the Fortune benchmark cited in that letter, under which only 6 of 1,000 large companies averaged more than 30% ROE.

**Segment check, pretax operating income on year-end identifiable assets (gross):**

| | 1985 | 1986 (ex-provisions) | 1987 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Soft drink operating margin | 18.3% | 20.9% | 23.0% |
| Soft drinks, OI / identifiable assets | 27% | 36% | 34% |
| International, OI / assets | 39% | 48% | 52% |
| Foods, OI / assets | 24% | 20% | 16% (ex-charge) |

US identifiable assets rose by $623M in 1987, which may include the temporary bottler holdings. That would depress the US return of 15.8%. Foods is the one weak segment. It earns about 7% of operating income and is a near-commodity business exposed to orange solids prices, and it ranks second in chilled juice.

**EPS growth, given secondary weight.** EPS from continuing operations rose from $1.17 in 1981 to $2.43 in 1987, about 13% a year. Average share count was 372M in 1981 and 377M in 1987, so buybacks did not create the per-share growth. They mostly reversed shares issued for the 1982 film studio acquisition and the 1985 Embassy acquisition.

**Incremental returns.** From 1981 to 1987, net income grew by $484M. Equity grew by about $953M and net debt grew by roughly $0.7B over the same period. That is roughly **30% on incremental capital**, achieved while a large share of new capital went into low-return affiliates.

**Accounting quality.** Nothing amounts to gimmickry, but several items are worth recording:
- "One-time" charges appeared in two consecutive years ($180M, then $36M).
- Gains from selling stakes in subsidiaries ($375M, then $40M) were run through earnings.
- Adopting FAS 87 cut pension expense by $13M.
- Interest of $6M was capitalized.
- Contingent guarantees total $394M, plus $325M of CPE receivables sold with recourse.
- The deconsolidations moved leverage off the balance sheet. CCE carries $2.72B of liabilities against $1.53B of net assets.

The core concentrate earnings look clean. The complexity sits around the edges.

## 3.2 Durable competitive advantage: the moat

The framework's model is a moat that management keeps widening, as GEICO does by driving down costs (`brk-letter-1986#16`). The packet's evidence:

- **Scale and share.** The company sells more than 44% of all soft drinks worldwide (excluding the USSR and China), at twice the volume of the nearest competitor (`ar-1987`). In the US it holds 39% share against the primary competitor's roughly 30%. It has 59% of US fountain, where it outsells the nearest rival more than 2 to 1 and took about three-quarters of the segment's growth over four years. US vending share is 49% (`ar-1986`).
- **Cost.** The company describes itself as the "low-cost producer in most of the 155 countries." Concentrate gross margin is about 63%. Capital needs are low: 1987 capex was $300M, or 3.9% of revenue, against $152M of depreciation.
- **Widening the moat, GEICO-style.** In the US, operating income rose 10% on 1% shipment growth. That came from consolidating syrup plants, shifting to concentrate (which cuts freight), and consolidating bottlers. In Germany, bottlers were cut from 102 to 68.
- **Durability under stress.** In 1985 management changed the flagship formula. Consumers forced the original back as "classic," which then became the best-selling US soft drink, and combined sugar-cola volume ended above 1984 levels (`ar-1986`). The trademark survived a serious management error, which is strong evidence that the castle does not depend on the people running it.
- **Runway.** International per-capita consumption was 16% of the US rate in 1986. Even the US is still growing: 203 servings per person in 1980, 274 in 1987, with a target of 315.
- **Pricing power.** Evidence here is moderate. The company has flexible pricing in the CCE contracts, raised CCE prices in October 1986, and states that it can price to offset inflation. Against that, price rises cut volume in Mexico and Germany. This matters under the inflation lens (`fortune-1977-inflation`): a business with low capital needs and some pricing power is the type that holds up.

**Costs and risks of the moat:**
- **Advertising.** Holding the moat requires heavy ongoing spending: $720M of media in 1987 and $2.11B of total selling expense, 27.6% of revenue. This runs through expenses rather than capital, so returns on capital are unaffected, but it is a permanent cost.
- **Currency.** About 74–77% of soft drink operating income is earned abroad. International operating income rose 25% with a 16% currency tailwind, which implies about 8% growth in local currency, in line with 9% volume growth. Reported dollar growth in 1986–87 overstates the underlying rate.
- **Bottlers.** Bottlers are now leveraged, low-return affiliates on which the core business depends.

## 3.3 Owner earnings

The definition is reported earnings, plus D&A and other non-cash charges, minus average capex needed to maintain the business (`brk-letter-1986`).

| 1987, $M | Using total capex | Using depreciation as a maintenance proxy |
|---|---|---|
| Net income | 916 | 916 |
| + D&A | 154 | 154 |
| − Equity income not received in cash | −80 | −80 |
| − Deferred tax (cash taxes above book) | −29 | −29 |
| ± Gain / provision (non-cash, roughly offset) | ~0 | ~0 |
| − Capex | −300 | −152 |
| **Owner earnings** | **≈ 661** | **≈ 809** |
| **Per share (372M shares)** | **≈ $1.78** | **≈ $2.17** |

The same measure on a consistent restated basis (working capital from operations minus capex) gives about $305M in 1985, $464M in 1986 and $659M in 1987. That is a steep rise, though 1985 includes high capex and 1986 includes the provisions. Not counted: about $80M of affiliate earnings retained inside the affiliates. Given what those affiliates earn on Coca-Cola's capital, they should be valued below face. Reported 1987 earnings ($2.43) therefore overstate owner earnings by roughly 10–25%.

## Facts the framework wants that the packet lacks

1. **Results for 1988.** This is the most important gap: the as-of date is 14 months after the last reported data.
2. **Split of capex between maintenance and growth,** and the working capital needed to support volume growth.
3. **Operating income in local currency by region,** and a longer history of real concentrate price per case, to test pricing power directly.
4. **Long-run history of share and margins against the primary competitor,** worldwide, to test whether the moat is widening.
5. **Look-through economics of the affiliates.** We need current market values (the only figure available is for end-1987, when it exceeded carrying value by just $44M) and whether Coca-Cola implicitly stands behind CCE's debt.
6. **Who captured the 1987 US tax cut.** This is the franchise-strength test in `brk-letter-1986#22`. The packet's tax figures mix in deconsolidation effects and cannot answer it.

## Stage 2 conclusion

The core concentrate business passes the framework's central test decisively. It earns about 25% on net capital without leverage, and well above 50% once the low-return affiliates are excluded. It has a durable, widening moat that has been shown to survive management error. It needs little capital and has a long international runway. The weak parts are Foods and the $2.26B of affiliate capital earning about 5%. Those are capital allocation questions for Section 4, not flaws in the franchise. On 1987 figures, owner earnings run at about $1.78–2.17 per share before any 1988 growth. Valuation against $46.56 is the next step.
