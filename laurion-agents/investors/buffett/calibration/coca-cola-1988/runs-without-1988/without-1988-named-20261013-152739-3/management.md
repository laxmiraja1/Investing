# Stage 3: Management and capital allocation (framework §4)

The three rules in §4 are applied in order: honest and able management with candid reporting; buybacks only well below intrinsic value; and each $1 retained must produce at least $1 of market value. Share issuance and acquisitions are judged under the second rule, using the share-issuance passage `brk-letter-1982#17`.

## 4.1 Honest, able management in place; candid reporting (`brk-letter-1979`, `brk-letter-1983#2`)

**Ability, judged by results.** Roberto Goizueta (Chairman and CEO) and Donald Keough (President and COO) have run the company since 1981.

| | 1981 | 1987 |
|---|---|---|
| Net income / avg equity | 19.9% | 27.2% |
| Net income | $482M | $916M |
| Average shares | 372M | 377M (408M peak in 1983) |
| Total return to shareholders, 1981–87 | 25%/yr vs 16%/yr for the S&P 500 (`ar-1987`) | |

Operating decisions in the packet point to competent managers, not just a strong franchise:

- Unit volume grew 10% in 1985 and in 1986, and 6% in 1987. The U.S. share gap over the main competitor is the widest in more than a decade.
- German bottlers were consolidated from 102 to 68.
- U.S. syrup shipments were switched to concentrate, which is cheaper to ship.
- Columbia Pictures' studio management was replaced when results were poor, and so was Foods management.

**Owner orientation in what they say.** The 1987 letter gives "a single objective: increasing shareholder value over time." The 1986 report sets a 12% after-tax cost-of-capital hurdle and commits to "divesting low return assets." These are the right aims, but they are stated in corporate-finance terms ("weighted average cost of capital") rather than in the per-share owner terms Berkshire uses (`brk-letter-1983#0`).

**Candor: better than average, with promotional gloss.**

Where they are candid:
- One-time items are separated out and growth is restated excluding them (for example, "Excluding 35 cents per share of unusual items in 1986").
- Currency help is admitted ("Aided by a weakening dollar"; "aided by a 16 percent strengthening of key currencies").
- 1987 U.S. shipments of +1% are disclosed against retail sales of +6%, because bottlers cut inventory.
- Columbia films: "we were not satisfied with the performance."
- Foods: "obviously in transition."

Where they fall short:
- The 1985 formula change is presented as a megabrand success ("seed firmly the products of megabrand Coca-Cola"), not as a mistake. That fails the test of "tell you the business facts that we would want to know if our positions were reversed" (`brk-letter-1983#2`).
- There is boosterism: international potential "appears limitless," and a "$250 billion" contribution to the world economy is reached by applying a multiplier of five.
- "Net debt" is defined generously.

**Conclusion.** Management is able and talks the right language. Candor is acceptable but stops short of Berkshire's own standard.

## 4.2 Buybacks only well below intrinsic value (`brk-letter-1984`); share issuance (`brk-letter-1982#17`)

**Repurchases:**

| Year | Shares (M) | Cost ($M) | Avg price | P/E on that year's EPS (ex one-time items) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1984 | 18.4 | 335.7 | $18.24 | ~11.6x ($1.57) |
| 1985 | 15.0 | 379.9 | $25.33 | ~14.7x ($1.72) |
| 1986 | 2.9 | 110.5 | $38.37 | ~18.5x ($2.07) |
| 1987 | 14.1 | 605.1 | ~$42.8 | ~17.6x ($2.43) |
| **Total** | **~50M (≈12% of 1983 count)** | **1,431** | **~$28.4** | |

- **1984–85.** Buying at 12–15x earnings a business earning more than 20% on equity, with little capital need and rising volume, is very likely "well below intrinsic value." These purchases fit the rule.
- **1986–87.** The 1987 purchases cost about $42.8, which gives an owner-earnings yield of about 4.3–4.8% (Stage 2: $1.71–2.07/share). They are above the 1987 year-end price of $38.13, though below today's $46.56. Whether they were "well below" value depends on the growth assumption tested in Stage 4. The margin was clearly narrower than in 1984–85.
- **The stated reason is weaker than the framework requires.** The buybacks were "initiated after management determined that... available cash and debt capacity would exceed the funds required," and "Often the best use of excess cash... has been to invest in our own stock." The trigger is surplus cash, not price compared with value. The rule requires the second.
- **Funding.** The 40M-share program announced in 1987 is being paid for largely with short-term borrowing. Loans and notes payable rose from $698M to $1,685M. This ties the buybacks to management's stated aim of raising debt toward a 35% net debt-to-capital ceiling "to lower the weighted average cost of capital." The framework is wary of returns on equity that depend on leverage (`brk-letter-1979`; `fortune-1977-inflation#6`).

**Share issuance was the weaker side of the record.**

- **Columbia Pictures, 1982.** It was a purchase acquisition. Average shares went from 372M (1981) to 390M (1982) to 408M (1983). That implies roughly 36M shares issued, at split-adjusted prices in the $10–18 range. The packet does not give the exact number, so this is inferred.
- **Embassy, 1985.** 7.1M shares were issued, worth about $168.6M from the equity statement (about $23.8/share).
- **The pattern.** Shares in a franchise earning about 50% on its operating capital were given up for film businesses. By 1986 the Entertainment sector earned $235M of operating income on $2.17B of assets (about 11% pre-tax). In 1987 it earned $50.7M net. Most of the 1984–87 buyback money was spent buying back, at $18–43, about as many shares as were issued in 1982–85 at roughly $10–24. This is the "double whammy" described in `brk-letter-1982#17`: business value is lost on the deal, and the market then marks the diluted business lower. It only stopped once the stock-financed deals stopped after 1985.
- **Partial unwinding.** In 1987–88 entertainment was combined into Columbia Pictures Entertainment. About $335M of its stock (carrying value) was paid out to shareholders as a dividend, and Coca-Cola's stake fell to 49%. Plastics (Presto, Winkler), Ronco and Embassy Home Entertainment were sold. Management now says it prefers "investments within our existing lines of business." This is the right direction. It is a correction, not proof that the habit has been broken.

**Other acquisitions and reinvestment:**

- **The bottler program** (CCE initial public offering at $1.12B, TCC, the UK joint venture, and 20–21% stakes in Consolidated and Johnston) moved capital-heavy bottling off Coca-Cola's balance sheet, and parts were sold at a profit ($375M and $40M gains). The cost is about $2.3–2.5B of equity-method stakes earning about 5% pre-tax (Stage 2), below management's own 12% hurdle.
- **Miami and Rainwater** ($536M) were bought and resold to CCE at carrying value. This kept bottlers in strong hands without a loss.

**Compensation.** The only evidence is in the packet:

- About 5.6M options are outstanding (about 1.5% of shares).
- A new 8M-share option plan and a 3M-share restricted-stock plan are in place.
- Performance units on 555,000 shares pay cash based on the stock price above $20.63.

Option exercises dilute by roughly 0.3% a year, small against buybacks of about 3% a year. However, the incentives are tied to the share price, not to returns on capital or per-share business value. The framework has no rule on compensation as of this date, so this is noted without being scored.

## 4.3 Each $1 retained must create at least $1 of market value (`brk-letter-1983`, with `brk-letter-1981#3`)

Period: 1981–1987, the current management's record. Figures are approximate and built from the selected-data tables.

| Item | $M |
|---|---|
| Net income, 1981–87 | ~4,754 |
| Cash dividends, 1981–87 | ~(2,550) |
| In-kind CPE dividend, 1987 | (335) |
| **Earnings retained** | **~1,870** |
| Market value end-1980: $11.13 × ~372M | ~4,140 |
| Market value end-1987: $38.13 × 372.4M | ~14,200 |
| **Gain in market value** | **~10,060** |

- **Result:** about **$5 of market value for every $1 retained**. The ~$1.4B spent on buybacks was also returned to owners, so the earnings retained inside the business were even smaller.
- **Allowing for the change in valuation.** The P/E rose from about 9.8x to about 15.7x. Holding the P/E at the 1980 level, end-1987 market value would be about 9.8 × $2.43 × 372M ≈ $8.9B, a gain of about $4.7B. That is still roughly **$2.5 per $1 retained**.
- **The test is passed clearly**, despite the entertainment detour. The planned cut in the dividend payout to 40% is consistent with this test only if the extra retained cash goes to the core business or to buybacks below value, not to more stakes earning about 5%.

## Information the investor would have relied on that the packet lacks

- **Meeting or knowing the managers.** The framework's permanent holdings rest on managers Berkshire "likes and admires" (`brk-letter-1987#18`), and Capital Cities was judged on long personal acquaintance (`brk-letter-1984#31`). The packet cannot show character, integrity or temperament directly. Any personal relationship with Goizueta or Keough would come from outside the packet and cannot be relied on here.
- **The proxy statement:** salaries, bonuses, the size of option grants to the top two executives, and how much stock management and directors own. That is the "eat our own cooking" test (`brk-letter-1983#0`).
- **How buyback decisions are made:** whether repurchases are priced against an estimate of intrinsic value, how they are timed (for example, any buying after the October 1987 drop to $28.96), and what was done in 1988.
- **How management now judges the Columbia and Embassy share issuances,** and whether they would sell the remaining 49% of CPE.
- **1988 actions:** further acquisitions, debt levels, progress on the 40M-share program.
- **Attitude to a large passive shareholder** and any takeover defenses.

## Conclusion for Stage 3

**Passes, with reservations.**

Strengths:
- Since 1981 management has raised returns on equity, refocused the company on concentrate (more than 95% of operating income), and bought back about 12% of the shares, mostly at sensible prices.
- The company passes the $1-retained test by a factor of 2.5 to 5.
- Its reports are more candid than most.

Concerns to carry into valuation and sizing:
- Shares were issued in 1982 and 1985 to buy lower-quality entertainment businesses, which by the framework's yardstick destroyed value. This has been partly reversed.
- Management prefers more debt as a matter of policy.
- The reason given for buybacks is surplus cash, not a discount to value.
- About $2.3B sits in affiliate stakes earning well below the company's own hurdle rate.

Character and integrity cannot be confirmed without the kind of personal knowledge the framework relies on. On the documents alone, management is able and owner-minded and has been improving its capital allocation, but the record is not spotless.
