**Memo: The Coca-Cola Company, purchase recommendation as of February 27, 1989**

We recommend buying The Coca-Cola Company at about $46.56 a share, building toward roughly 25% of the portfolio and holding it indefinitely. The analysis uses only the company's 1986 and 1987 annual reports and market prices to February 24, 1989. No 1988 results were available.

Coca-Cola's business is simple. It makes syrup and concentrate and sells them to bottlers and fountain customers in more than 155 countries. This activity produces more than 95% of operating income. Company brands account for more than 44% of soft drinks sold worldwide outside the Soviet Union and China, about twice the volume of the nearest competitor. In the United States its share exceeds 39%, and in fountain it outsells its nearest rival by more than two to one. Gross margin on concentrate sold to U.S. bottlers is about 63%. The company also holds 49% stakes in large bottlers and in Columbia Pictures Entertainment, and it owns a juice business built around Minute Maid.

The company passes each of our screens. It is not a turnaround: earnings have grown every year since at least 1976. It is not a technology bet, and the core business needs little capital. In 1987 the soft drink operations spent $170 million on plant and equipment while earning $1.43 billion before tax. The test we weight most heavily is return on equity, measured without the help of debt. Reported return on average equity was 27% in 1987. A cleaner measure strips out cash and the affiliate stakes. On that basis the operating business earned roughly 50% after tax on about $1.7 billion of operating capital. From 1976 to 1981, when debt was under 10% of capital, return on equity was still about 20%. That establishes that the returns come from the franchise, not from borrowing.

The franchise also behaves like one. Management says it can raise prices to offset rising costs, and the new bottler contracts give it discretion over concentrate pricing. When the corporate tax rate fell, the benefit went to the company, which is what happens when a business has pricing power and is not price-regulated. The brand survived the 1985 change to the Coke formula, an error made by the company's own management, and reached record share the following year. International markets drink only about 16% as much per person as the United States, so there is room for many years of volume growth that requires little capital.

Management is good but not flawless. Since 1981 Roberto Goizueta and Donald Keough have raised return on equity from about 20% to 27% and moved the company back toward its core. Over 1981–1987 the company retained about $1.9 billion of earnings, and market value rose by about $10 billion. Even if the price-earnings multiple had not changed, each retained dollar would have produced about $2.50 of market value. They repurchased about 50 million shares between 1984 and 1987, mostly at 12 to 18 times earnings. Three things count against them:

- In 1982 and 1985 they issued roughly 43 million shares to buy film businesses that earned far less than the soft drink franchise.
- About $2 billion of capital sits in bottling and film affiliates that earn roughly 5% before tax.
- They explicitly want more debt. Gross debt reached 45.6% of capital in 1987, and they describe buybacks as a use of surplus cash rather than a purchase made at a discount to value.

None of these is disqualifying, but they are the reason we do not take the largest possible position.

On our old net working capital test the stock is not cheap. Its working capital net of all liabilities is negative, and the shares trade at more than five times book value. That test was designed for mediocre businesses bought cheaply, and it is the wrong tool here. We instead valued owner earnings: reported earnings, plus depreciation, minus capital spending. For 1987 this was about $1.74 a share from the core business. The stakes in bottlers and Columbia add roughly $5.50 a share. The key assumption is that the next decade resembles the last, during which per-share earnings grew 11–13% a year. On that assumption, 12% growth for ten years and 4% thereafter, discounted at 10% (close to what the company itself pays on its long-term debt), the shares are worth about $61. That is a margin of about 23% below value. The current price builds in only about 8% annual growth. If growth slows to that level, or if we discount at management's own 12% cost of capital, value roughly equals the price. In that case we would earn a bond-like return rather than lose capital. Only a near-collapse of growth would produce a serious loss, and nothing in the record suggests one.

This is a fair price for an exceptional business, not a bargain. That is why we are sizing the position at about a quarter of the portfolio rather than the 40% we reserve for situations where we are certain of every fact. We need no event or catalyst. We expect the stock price to follow the business over time. The stock's rise over the past year is not a reason to wait.

We would buy more if 1988 results confirm the recent growth rate or if the price falls toward $35–40. We would reconsider if any of the following occurred:

- Underlying growth falls clearly below 8%.
- Share losses or bottler contract terms weaken concentrate pricing.
- Management again issues stock for unrelated businesses.
- Debt rises well beyond its stated ceiling.

We would sell if we had misjudged the franchise or if management stopped acting for owners. A higher stock price alone would not cause us to sell.
