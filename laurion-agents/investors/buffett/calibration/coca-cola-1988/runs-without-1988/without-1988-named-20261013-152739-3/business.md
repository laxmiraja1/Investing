# Stage 2: Business analysis (framework §3)

The analysis follows the three measures in §3 in the order given: return on equity, then the moat, then owner earnings. All figures are $ millions unless stated, and the 1985–86 comparisons use the restated basis in the 1987 report (`fin-summary`, `ar-1987`).

## 3.1 Return on equity, not EPS growth (`brk-letter-1979#2`)

**Reported EPS is misleading here, which is why the framework puts return on equity first.** EPS went from $2.42 in 1986 to $2.43 in 1987, which looks flat. Two things distort it:
- 1986 included a $375M pre-tax gain on the CCE stock sale (+$262M after tax) and $180M of charges (−$128M after tax). That is a net +$0.35 a share of one-time items.
- Buybacks cut average shares from 393M in 1985 to 377M in 1987, about 4% fewer.

**Return on equity, reported and adjusted:**

| | 1985 | 1986 | 1987 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Net income / avg equity (reported) | 23.5% | 28.8% | 27.2% |
| 1986 without CCE gain and provisions (~$800M net) | | ~24.6% | |
| 1987 one-time items ($40M TCC gain vs $36M foods charge) roughly cancel | | | ~27% |

**Checking for "undue leverage, accounting gimmickry, etc.":**

- **Baseline with almost no debt.** From 1976 to 1981, debt was only 3.5–9% of capital, and the company still earned 19.7–21.2% on equity while holding a lot of cash (`ar-1986`, selected data). So roughly 20% return on equity is what the business earns with essentially no debt.
- **Higher leverage since then.** Equity fell from 67% of total assets in 1981 to 39% in 1987. Gross debt is 45.6% of capital. Two items also shrank the equity base in 1987: $605M of buybacks and the $335M Columbia Pictures Entertainment (CPE) dividend paid in shares. Part of the rise from about 21% to 27% therefore comes from a smaller equity base, not a better business.
- **Return on the core operating assets.** The better test strips out the cash and the affiliate stakes. Starting from total assets of $8,356M, I subtract:
  - cash of $1,468M,
  - CPE notes of $545M (paid January 1988),
  - temporary investments of $308M (sold January 1988),
  - equity-method stakes of $2,259M,
  - non-interest liabilities: payables $1,430M, accrued taxes $454M, deferred taxes $210M.

  That leaves about **$1.7B** of net operating assets. Operating income was $1,324M; adding back the $36M foods charge gives $1,360M, which at the 35% effective tax rate is about **$884M after tax**. That is roughly **50%+ on the capital the operating business actually uses**. This is a rough figure from a balance sheet that has not been split out cleanly.
- **Comparison with Berkshire's own businesses.** That return is in the same range as the ~57% Berkshire reports for its seven best units on their underlying equity (`brk-letter-1987#1`). In Fortune's 1988 survey only six of 1,000 large companies averaged over 30%.
- **The affiliate stakes are the drag.** Equity income was $118.5M in 1987 on about $2.45B of affiliate stakes at the start of the year (CPE $1,437M, CCE $709M, TCC $88M, other $212M). That is **about 5% pre-tax**, against management's own 12% after-tax cost of capital. The market seems to agree: the quoted stakes were worth only $44M more than their carrying value. Bottling and film are poor businesses compared with concentrate. CCE, for example, earned $88M on $3.3B of revenue and carries $2.2B of non-current liabilities.
- **Accounting flags.** None of these suggests fraud, but each needs an adjustment before relying on the numbers:
  - repeated gains from selling subsidiary stock ($375M, $40M);
  - deconsolidations that make revenue look different from year to year;
  - an unconsolidated finance arm (CCFC, about $300M of debt that FASB 94 will bring onto the balance sheet);
  - receivables sold with recourse;
  - $394M of guarantees;
  - the company's flattering definition of "net debt."

  Taken together, the reported figures **understate** the core business and **overstate** how clean the overall structure is.

**Conclusion for 3.1.** The concentrate business clears the test by a wide margin. The reported 27% mixes an exceptional core with low-return affiliate stakes and more debt.

## 3.2 Durable competitive advantage: the moat (`brk-letter-1986#16`)

For GEICO the moat was measured by its cost gap against competitors. For Coca-Cola the packet points to brand, scale, and pricing power:

1. **Share and scale.** The company says Coca-Cola is the "world's best-known trademark." Its brands make up more than 44% of world soft drinks (excluding the USSR and China), and its volume is double the nearest competitor's.
   - U.S. share: over 39% in 1986, 9 points ahead of the main competitor.
   - U.S. fountain: 59% share, more than a 2-to-1 lead, and nearly three-quarters of the segment's growth over four years.
   - U.S. vending: 49% share.

   Brand awareness, image and preference scores were at record highs in 1986 (`ar-1986`).
2. **Pricing power without regulation.**
   - Gross margin on U.S. concentrate is about 63%.
   - The new bottler contracts let the company set prices "at its discretion," and it used that right to raise prices to CCE in October 1986.
   - Management says the company "is able to adjust prices to compensate for increasing costs." This matters for the inflation problem raised in `brk-letter-1979#2` and `fortune-1977-inflation#6`.
   - The LIFO reserve is only $19M, which suggests little exposure to rising costs in inventory.
   - Under the test in `brk-letter-1986#22`, an unregulated, strong franchise keeps the benefit of the drop in tax rates. The statutory rate fell from 46% to 40% to 34%, and the 1987 effective rate was 35%.
3. **Low capital needs.** In 1987 the soft-drink segments spent $170M on capital equipment against $103M of depreciation, while earning $1,432M of operating income. So capital spending is about 12% of operating profit. The capital-heavy bottling has been moved into the 49%-owned affiliates.
4. **Durability under stress.** The 1985 change to the Coke formula and the return of "Coca-Cola classic" was a serious marketing mistake. Even so, return on equity rose in 1985, U.S. share hit a record in 1986, and Coca-Cola classic became the top-selling U.S. soft drink. The brand survived its own management's error.
5. **Room to grow.** Outside the U.S., people drink about 16% as much soft drink per person as Americans do. U.S. consumption itself rose from 203 servings per person in 1980 to 274 in 1987. International volume grew 9–12% a year from 1985 to 1987, and international markets produce 77% of soft-drink operating income.

**Weak points:**
- **Foods.** Operating income fell from $120M to $67M ($103M before the charge). Orange solids are a commodity, and the company is second in chilled juice.
- **Currency.** About 74% of operating income is earned outside the U.S. Key foreign currencies strengthened against the dollar by 27% in 1986 and 16% in 1987, so the recent growth figures include a currency tailwind that could reverse.
- **U.S. operations.** The U.S. operating margin is about 15%, against about 27% internationally. In 1987 U.S. shipments grew only 1% while retail sales grew 6%, because bottlers drew down their inventories.

**Conclusion for 3.2.** The moat is wide and, judging by share gains in fountain and food stores, it is getting wider. It rests on brand and the distribution system, not on technology.

## 3.3 Owner earnings, 1987 (`brk-letter-1986`)

The formula is reported earnings, plus depreciation, amortization and other non-cash charges, minus average capital spending.

| Item | $M |
|---|---|
| Net income | 916.1 |
| + Depreciation | 151.9 |
| + Goodwill amortization | 1.6 |
| − Capital expenditures, 1987 | (299.6) |
| **Owner earnings, before adjustments** | **770.0** |
| − Undistributed equity income (non-cash credit) | (79.7) |
| ± TCC gain (−39.7) against foods provision (+36.4) | (3.3) |
| **Owner earnings, conservative** | **~687** |

- **Using a three-year capex average.** If capex is averaged over 1985–87 ($412M, $346M, $300M, average $352M), the result is about $717M before adjustments and about $635M on the conservative basis. Capex has been falling partly because bottling assets moved off the books, so the 1987 figure is probably the more representative one.
- **Range.** Owner earnings come to roughly **$635–770M, or $1.71–2.07 a share** on the 372M shares outstanding at year-end. Owner earnings are about 75–84% of reported net income.
- **Cross-check by segment.** Working segment by segment gives about $750–780M:
  - soft drinks after tax: ~$931M, plus depreciation $103M, less capex $170M ≈ $863M;
  - minus corporate expense after tax: ~$114M;
  - plus foods: ~$40M;
  - minus net interest after tax: ~$47M;
  - plus dividends received from affiliates: ~$39M.
- **Where the value is.** Almost all of the owner earnings come from the concentrate business.

## Facts the framework wants that the packet lacks

- **1988 results**, which should have been published around February 1989, and 1988 buyback progress and the current share count. I am assuming 1988 continued 1987's underlying trend, but this is not verified.
- **Unit economics over time**: price per gallon of concentrate and real price changes over ten or more years, to confirm pricing power directly rather than from management's statements.
- **Competitor figures** (margins and costs at the main competitor), to measure the width of the moat in the way GEICO's expense-ratio gap measured its moat.
- **Constant-currency growth**: international earnings with the currency effect removed.
- **Split of capex into maintenance and growth spending**, which owner earnings needs. Both the 1987 and the average figures above treat all capex as necessary.
- **Bottler contract terms** (how long they run, formula pricing) and the financial health of bottlers outside CCE and TCC.
- **Market values of the affiliate stakes.** The packet gives only "over $2.3B" and "$44M above carrying value."
- **Management compensation and incentives** beyond option counts, and how the 40M-share buyback is priced compared with value. These belong to Stage 3.

## Summary of Stage 2

The concentrate business shows every economic trait §3 looks for:
- about 50% after-tax return on the operating capital it uses;
- roughly 20% return on equity even with almost no debt (the 1976–81 record);
- capital spending of about 12% of operating profit;
- the ability to raise prices with inflation and to keep tax savings;
- a dominant brand that survived its own management's mistake.

Management has wrapped this core in about $2.3B of affiliate stakes earning around 5% and in deliberately higher borrowing. Owner earnings are about **$1.71–2.07 a share**. The questions about how management allocates capital, and whether $46.56 is a sensible price, go to Stages 3 and 4.
