# Stage 4: Valuation (framework §5) and Catalyst / timing (§6)

## 5.1 The framework's own valuation tool: net working capital (`bpl-letter-1965`)

The only valuation rule in the framework as of this date is the Partnership-era bargain test: buy below net working capital. Applied to the year-end 1987 balance sheet (`ar-1987`):

| Item | $M |
|---|---|
| Current assets | 4,136.2 |
| − Current liabilities | (4,118.5) |
| = Net working capital | 17.7 |
| − Long-term debt | (803.4) |
| − Deferred income taxes | (209.9) |
| **= Working capital net of all liabilities** | **(995.6), or −$2.67 a share** |

At $46.56, against negative net working capital and a book value of $8.66 a share (5.4x), Coca-Cola **fails this test completely**. It is not a "general" bargain bought on quantitative cheapness (`bpl-letter-1963#5`).

That outcome is expected, and it is not decisive. By the as-of date the framework's own evidence has moved the governing test away from this tool for businesses like this one:
- The aim is "an outstanding business at a sensible price, not a mediocre business at a bargain price." Searching for bargains led to "short-line farm implement manufacturers, third-place department stores, and New England textile manufacturers" (`brk-letter-1987#18`).
- "Book value at most companies differs widely from intrinsic business value" (`brk-letter-1986#0`). For a high-return business, price-to-book "does not change… return on equity" (`brk-letter-1987#1`).
- Berkshire buys at "prices that will make sense, even should the future of the acquired enterprise develop much along the lines of its past," and "may very well pay a fairly fancy price for a Category 1 business if we are reasonably confident of what we are getting" (`brk-letter-1981#3`).

So the working test is to **value owner earnings (the §3 measure) and ask whether the price makes sense if the next decade resembles the last**. This is not a generic forecast model. It projects only the demonstrated record, which follows the Berkshire preference for "demonstrated consistent earning power" over "future projections" (`brk-letter-1982`).

## 5.3 Value of owner earnings

Method: core owner earnings of $1.74 grow at rate *g* for 10 years, then at 4% a year after that. The result is discounted and the affiliate stakes are added.

| Case | g (yrs 1–10) | Rate | Core value | + Stakes | **Value/share** | Price ÷ value |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Floor: no growth at all | 0% | 10% | $17.4 | $5.5 | **~$23** | 2.0 |
| Below-record growth | 8% | 10% | $40.9 | $5.5 | **~$46** | 1.00 |
| Record repeats, at management's hurdle rate | 12% | 12% | $40.0 | $5.5 | **~$46** | 1.02 |
| **Record repeats (central)** | **12%** | **10%** | **$55.3** | **$5.5** | **~$61** | **0.77** |
| Recent pace continues | 15% | 10% | $69.4 | $5.5 | **~$75** | 0.62 |

How the central case is built: the 10-year growth stage is worth $1.74 × 11.05 = $19.2. Owner earnings in year 10 are $5.40; capitalized at 4% growth they are worth $93.7, which is $36.1 in today's money. Total core value is $55.3.

**What the price implies.** At a 10% rate, $46.56 assumes core owner earnings grow about **8% a year for ten years**. That is roughly two-thirds of the 11–13% achieved over 1977–87 and 1981–87.

Several facts from Stages 2–3 support the record continuing:
- Volume grew 6–10% a year.
- International consumption per person is only 16% of the U.S. level.
- Pricing power keeps pace with inflation.
- Capital spending is only about 12% of soft-drink operating income, so growth needs little reinvestment.
- Buybacks of about 3% a year raise per-share growth.

Factors that could depress it:
- The currency tailwind of 1986–87 (+27% and +16%) could reverse.
- U.S. operations grow more slowly.
- Retained cash could keep flowing into affiliate stakes earning about 5%.

**Cross-check without a model.** The buyer's starting core owner-earnings yield is $1.74 ÷ ($46.56 − $5.5) = **4.2%**. Adding growth in line with the record (about 12%) and assuming a constant multiple gives roughly a 16% annual return. That compares with about 10% on the company's own bonds. If growth fell to around 6%, the stock would only match the bonds.

**Missing 1988 base.** If 1988 owner earnings grew about 15%, which is close to the 1987 underlying rate, every value above would rise by about 15%. The central case would be about $69 and the margin about 33%. Because 1988 results are not in the packet, this is a sensitivity only, not the base case.

## 5.4 Margin of safety against the hurdle

The framework sets **no numeric hurdle** at this date. It requires a "substantial excess of value" creating "a comfortable margin of safety" (`bpl-letter-1961#3`, `bpl-letter-1962#4`). For a top-tier business it permits "a fairly fancy price… if we are reasonably confident" (`brk-letter-1981#3`), as long as the price "makes sense" if the future resembles the past. I apply it in two parts.

1. **Does the price make sense if the past repeats?** Yes. The central value is about **$61** against a price of **$46.56**, a **margin of about 23%** (price/value 0.77). The range runs from about 0% (8% growth, or a 12% discount rate) to about 38% (15% growth).
2. **What is the downside if the record is not repeated?** If growth falls to 8%, or investors demand 12%, value is about equal to price. The buyer then earns roughly a bond-like return, without a permanent loss (§9, `bpl-letter-1967-oct`). Only the no-growth case (about $23) produces a large loss. That case requires the moat described in Stage 2 to fail, and nothing in the packet points that way.

**Verdict on the hurdle.** This is **not** a bargain by Partnership standards, which demanded deep discounts on quantitatively cheap stocks. It **does** pass the 1980s Berkshire test of a sensible price for an outstanding, well-understood business, with a moderate margin of about 20–25% in the central case and no realistic path to a permanent loss. The margin depends on confidence in the moat (Stage 2), not on cheapness of the assets. That is exactly the trade the framework accepts for a "Category 1" business.

## 6. Catalyst / timing

- **No catalyst is needed.** "The market may ignore business success for a while, but eventually will confirm it" (`brk-letter-1987`). The case rests on owner earnings compounding, not on any event.
- **Not a work-out** (`bpl-letter-1957`). No sale, merger, liquidation or tender is announced. The 40M-share buyback running through 1990 adds to per-share value growth, but it is not a timetabled event that produces the profit.
- **Not a control situation** (`bpl-letter-1963#5`). At about $17B of market value, influence would come from a large, passive, friendly holding, not control. Berkshire would not engage in unfriendly action (`brk-letter-1986#10`).
- **Timing.** The stock is up about 24% from February 1988 ($37.44) and close to its 52-week high of $49.12. It is well above the $28.96 low of October 1987. The framework does not forecast markets (`bpl-letter-1966-mid`; `fortune-1977-inflation`), and Mr. Market "is there to serve you, not to guide you" (`brk-letter-1987`). The past year's rise is therefore not a reason to wait, and waiting for a return to $35 would be market timing. On the "fearful when others are greedy" test (`brk-letter-1986`), a P/E of about 19x on 1987 earnings, and probably about 16–17x on 1988 earnings if they grew as assumed, does not indicate a frenzy around this stock. The packet holds no information on the general market's level or mood.

## Facts the valuation needs that the packet lacks

- **1988 results and the share count.** These are the largest gap. Each 10% of 1988 growth moves value by about 10%.
- **The long-term Treasury yield,** to anchor the discount rate. The 10% used here comes from the company's own borrowing costs.
- **Current (February 1989) market values of the CCE, CPE and TCC stakes.**
- **Constant-currency earnings,** to separate the currency boost from underlying growth.
- **The split of capital spending into maintenance and growth.** The owner-earnings base treats all capital spending as required, which is conservative.

## Conclusion of Stage 4

Coca-Cola **fails** the framework's only formal valuation tool, net working capital, by a wide margin. That tool belongs to the bargain-hunting category the framework has explicitly moved away from for businesses of this quality.

Valued on owner earnings under the "future like the past" standard, intrinsic value is about **$61 a share (range about $46–75)** against a price of **$46.56**. That is a central **margin of safety of about 23%**. The market is pricing in roughly 8% growth, against a record of 11–13%. The downside where the record fades is about break-even, not a permanent loss. No catalyst is needed and none applies.

The valuation therefore supports going on to a buy decision. Sizing (§7) will depend on how confident Stage 2 makes us that "anything could drastically change the underlying value" is a very low-probability event.
