# Stage 3: Management and capital allocation (The Coca-Cola Company, as of 1989-02-27)

The framework's "Management & capital allocation" section has three rules:

1. Honest, able management already in place, with candid reporting (`brk-letter-1979`, `brk-letter-1983#2`).
2. Buybacks only at prices well below per-share intrinsic value (`brk-letter-1984`).
3. Each $1 retained must create at least $1 of market value (`brk-letter-1983`).

The investor's writing on value-diluting share issuance (`brk-letter-1982#17`) and the owner orientation he describes (`brk-letter-1983#0`) are also relevant. I take them in that order and add debt policy and compensation, which the packet lets me examine.

**Bottom line:** management passes, with two reservations. The core is run by able managers who think in returns on capital and who, since 1984, have returned most earnings to owners through dividends and buybacks. The weak spot is the 1982–1986 diversification into films and TV, which earned well below management's own 12% hurdle. In 1987 management reversed course: it deconsolidated the entertainment business, spun part of it out to shareholders, and now says it prefers to invest within existing lines. That reversal reassures me, but it doesn't erase the record.

---

## 1. Management in place, and candor (`brk-letter-1979`, `brk-letter-1983#2`)

> "We don't expect a public relations document when our operating managers tell us what is going on…"

### Who and how long

Roberto Goizueta (Chairman and CEO) and Donald Keough (President and COO) have led the company since 1981. M. Douglas Ivester is CFO. The "Strategy for the 1980s" written in 1981 is still the operating plan seven years later (`ar-1987` letter). The management in place is also the management that built the record. That is the condition the acquisition criteria ask for: "management in place (we can't supply it)" (`brk-letter-1982`).

### Do they think like owners?

Mostly, yes, and in the right terms:
- The stated objective is "to increase shareholder value over time."
- New investment is screened against a stated after-tax cost of capital of about 12% (`ar-1986`, `ar-1987` MD&A). Very few managements publish a hurdle rate.
- The 1987 report says the company prefers "investments within our existing lines of business" and applies "strict discipline in assessing potential investments on the basis of cost of capital, rate of return."

This matches the framework's primary test of return on capital rather than size (`brk-letter-1979`, `brk-letter-1981#3`: "The empire would have been larger, but the citizenry would have been poorer").

### Candor: mixed

The front of each report is a public-relations document:
- "When Coca-Cola is a part of your life, you can't beat the feeling."
- A claimed $250 billion contribution to the world economy, built on "the commonly accepted multiplier factor of five."
- In 1986, entertainment was described as part of a "rich portfolio of opportunities," and management said it was "prepared for whatever direction the consumer takes the industry."

The financial sections are better than average, and in places unusually frank:
- Unusual items are separated, and management compares years excluding them ("Excluding 35 cents per share of unusual items in 1986…").
- They admit weaknesses plainly. On films in 1986: "we were not satisfied with the performance of Columbia Pictures' feature films." On foods in 1987: "Our foods business is obviously in transition."
- They disclose currency movements by country and state that current-cost income "would be less than reported."
- They say inflation-driven purchasing-power gains on debt "do not represent an increase in funds available for distribution to shareholders and do not necessarily imply that incurring more debt would be beneficial." Few managements volunteer a point that cuts against their own leverage policy.

What candor would still require:
- Currency's share of operating-income growth. The percentage moves are given; the dollar effect is not.
- Returns earned on the entertainment and bottling capital, measured against the 12% hurdle they set themselves.

**Verdict:** able management, owner-oriented in its stated framework. The reports pass on substance and fail on tone. The tone is not disqualifying, because the numbers needed to see through it are provided.

## 2. The $1 retained test (`brk-letter-1983`)

> "We test the wisdom of retaining earnings by assessing whether retention, over time, delivers shareholders at least $1 of market value for each $1 retained."

The current managers' record begins at year-end 1981.

| | Year-end 1981 | Now (1989-02-24) |
|---|---|---|
| Share price (split-adjusted) | $11.58 | $46.56 |
| Shares (millions) | ~372 (1981 average) | ~372 (year-end 1987 count) |
| Market value | ~**$4.31B** | ~**$17.34B** |

Market value increased by about **$13.0B**. Shareholders also received 34.1M CPE shares as a dividend in kind, carried at $335M.

**Earnings retained, 1982–1987** (net income less cash dividends):

| Year | Net income ($M) | Cash dividends ($M) |
|---|---|---|
| 1982 | 512 | ~324 (0.83 × 390M shares) |
| 1983 | 559 | ~363 (0.89 × 408M shares) |
| 1984 | 629 | 364 |
| 1985 | 722 | 389 |
| 1986 | 934 | 403 |
| 1987 | 916 | 422 |
| **Total** | **~4,272** | **~2,265** |

Retained earnings come to about **$2.0B**. 1988 retention is not in the packet, since no 1988 results are given. Even a generous assumption of $0.6B for 1988 leaves the ratio near 5:1.

The raw ratio is about **6.5:1**, a clear pass. But much of the gain comes from the market paying a higher multiple, not from management. The P/E went from about 8.9x 1981 earnings ($11.58 / $1.30) to 19.2x 1987 earnings. Two more conservative checks:

- **Hold the 1981 multiple constant.** Income from continuing operations rose from $432M (1981) to $916M (1987), an increase of about $484M. At 8.9x, that is roughly $4.3B of added value against about $2.0B retained, still above 2:1. The share count is about the same at both ends, so the total and per-share comparisons agree.
- **Return on retained capital.** About $484M of added earnings on about $2.0B retained is roughly **24%** after tax. That is double management's own 12% hurdle. It is somewhat flattered by the weaker dollar in 1986–87 and by modest added debt, but the margin is wide.

**Verdict:** passes clearly, even after removing the change in multiple.

## 3. Buybacks only well below intrinsic value (`brk-letter-1984`)

> "major repurchases at prices well below per-share intrinsic business value immediately increase, in a highly significant way, that value."

Average prices paid, from the shareholders' equity statements:

| Year | Shares repurchased | Cost | Average price | Multiple of that year's EPS |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1984 | 18.4M | $335.7M | ~$18.25 | ~11.6x ($1.57) |
| 1985 | 15.0M | $379.9M | ~$25.33 | ~14.7x ($1.72) |
| 1986 | 2.9M | $110.5M | ~$38.37 | ~15.9x reported; ~18.5x underlying (~$2.07) |
| 1987 | 14.2M | $605.1M | ~$42.61 | ~17.5x ($2.43) |
| 1988 | not in packet | — | — | — |

Remaining authorization: up to 40M shares by 1990-12-31. About 6.6M had been bought by year-end 1987, at an average of about $42.12. About 33M shares remain; at today's $46.56 that is roughly $1.5B more.

Assessment:
- **1984–85 purchases** were made at 11–15x earnings, in a business earning 20%+ on equity, needing little capital and growing earnings in the low to mid teens. By any reasonable estimate of owner earnings that is well below intrinsic value. These repurchases fit the rule well.
- **1986–87 purchases** at 16–18x are not obviously "well below" value. Whether they were depends on the Stage 4 valuation. With owner earnings of roughly $2.07–2.46 per share and demonstrated reinvestment returns above 20%, they may still have been below value, but by a narrower margin.
- **Stated rationale.** The 1986 and 1987 MD&A justify repurchases mainly by excess capacity: cash and debt capacity "exceed the funds required." That is the wrong primary test. The rule says buy back because price is below value, not because cash is idle. The 1987 letter comes closer: "Often the best use of excess cash… has been to invest in our own stock." Their actions in 1984–85 were better than their stated reasoning.
- **Funding.** Part of the 1987 buyback was financed with short-term borrowing. Net debt stayed near 15% of capital after the January 1988 cash receipts, so this is not reckless. But it is a deliberate move toward the 35% ceiling, discussed under debt policy below.
- **Net effect on share count.** Roughly 50.5M shares were bought back in 1984–87. That mostly offset about 7.1M issued for Embassy, roughly 1.0–1.3M a year from option exercises, and a rise in average shares from 372M (1981) to 408M (1983). The packet doesn't explain that 1983 rise; it coincides with the Columbia acquisition in 1982 and I infer stock was issued for it. In effect, the buybacks unwound the diversification's dilution.

**Verdict:** the early buybacks pass. The later ones are pending valuation. The stated reasoning is weaker than the record.

## 4. Acquisitions and share issuance (`brk-letter-1982#17`, `brk-letter-1981#3`)

> "we will issue shares only if our owners receive in business value as much as we give."

### Entertainment (1982–1987)

| Deal | Cost (from packet) |
|---|---|
| Columbia Pictures (1982) | Not given. Average shares rose 372M → 408M over 1981–83, suggesting stock was issued. |
| Embassy (1985) | ~$267M, mostly ~7.1M Coke shares plus repayment of debt |
| Tandem (1985) | ~$178M cash |
| Merv Griffin Enterprises (1986) | ~$200M |
| Walter Reade theatres | Small |

**What came of it:**
- Entertainment operating income grew quickly: $121M (1984), $161M (1985), $235M (1986).
- It ended as a 49% stake in CPE carried at $989M, plus about $335M of CPE stock distributed to shareholders.
- Coke's share of CPE's 1987 earnings was about $49M on a $1.44B carrying value at the start of the year, roughly 3–4%. That is far below the company's own 12% hurdle.

Issuing shares of a 20%+ return franchise to buy a hit-driven film and TV business fails the "same yardstick" test in `brk-letter-1982#17`. Owners gave up part of a superior business for an inferior one.

**What counts in management's favor:** the losses were not large. Embassy's investment was mostly recovered through receivables and asset sales, by management's account. And in 1987 management stopped committing Coke capital: it combined the business with Tri-Star, reduced its stake to 49%, and gave shareholders CPE shares directly. Recognizing a mistake and getting out is the behavior `brk-letter-1983#2` values over persisting in "quicksand."

### Foods

Spending here was small (Nutri-Foods for about $30M, plus capex), but returns are deteriorating and senior management was replaced in 1987. Foods has not earned its keep recently. It is small enough not to matter much.

### Bottler investments

Since 1986:
- CCE: Coke took a 49% stake after CCE's $2.25B of bottler acquisitions, which were financed with CCE's own debt and not guaranteed by Coke.
- Rainwater and Miami bottlers: bought and resold at carrying value.
- Coca-Cola Bottling Co. Consolidated (~$63M) and Johnston (~$54M).
- UK joint venture, China, Indonesia, Taiwan.

These stakes earn about 5% on carrying value as equity income. But they exist to strengthen the system that buys Coke's concentrate; CCE alone bought $653M in 1987. Part of their return shows up in concentrate profits rather than equity income. The packet doesn't allow that to be measured.

The structure is rational. It keeps capital-intensive, debt-heavy bottling off Coke's balance sheet while keeping influence over it. The cost is the off-balance-sheet exposure noted in Stage 2, such as the $394M of contingent guarantees.

### Dividend policy

Management plans to reduce the payout ratio toward 40% (46% cash payout in 1987) to retain more. For a core business earning well above 20% on reinvested capital, more retention is right, but only if it goes into soft drinks. The 1987 commitment to existing lines is the safeguard. Whether it holds is something to keep watching.

**Verdict:** a poor 1982–86 diversification episode, sensibly unwound. The core capital-allocation record since 1984, which is dividends, buybacks and system restructuring, is good.

## 5. Debt policy (related to "little or no debt," `brk-letter-1982`)

In late 1986 management raised its net debt-to-capital ceiling to 35% "to reduce the weighted average cost of capital." Actual net debt was 15.3% at year-end 1987, leaving about $1.2B of capacity. This is the language of corporate finance, not of owners who prefer "little or no debt."

At the current level it does no harm. Interest is covered about 4.7 times gross and about 18 times net. If the capacity is used for buybacks below value, it helps owners. If it is used to fund another diversification, it would be the 1982 mistake repeated with borrowed money. This is a watch item, not a disqualifier.

## 6. Compensation and dilution

The framework passages at this date don't set a specific compensation rule. The relevant principles are owner orientation and not issuing shares for less than they're worth (`brk-letter-1983#0`, `brk-letter-1982#17`).

What the packet shows (`ar-1987` Notes 10–11):
- **Options:** 5.6M outstanding at year-end 1987, about 1.5% of shares. About 1.1–1.8M granted a year; about 1.2–1.3M exercised a year, at prices from $10 to $39. A new 1987 plan covers 8M shares.
- **Restricted stock:** 178,500 shares in 1987; 883,500 in 1984.
- **Performance Unit Agreements:** cash awards on 555,000 shares above a $20.63 base. At today's price that is worth roughly $14M to officers.
- **Stock appreciation rights** attached to options.

Annual dilution is about 0.3–0.4% of shares, small next to the buybacks. Option costs aren't charged to earnings under the accounting of the time, so owner earnings are slightly overstated, probably by tens of millions a year against about $916M of net income.

The structure rewards share-price gains rather than return on capital, which is a mild misalignment. The scale is not alarming.

**Not in the packet:** total executive pay, and how much stock directors and officers own. The 1983 letter treats directors with large personal stakes as a mark of owner alignment ("We eat our own cooking"). The proxy statement would show this.

## What the investor would have relied on that I don't have

1. **Personal assessment of Goizueta and Keough.** Their integrity, judgment, and whether "increasing shareholder value" is a conviction or a slogan. The framework puts heavy weight on "high-grade, talented and likeable managers" (`brk-letter-1987#18`). That judgment is formed by meeting and observing people, not from annual reports.
2. **The proxy statement:** executive pay, director and officer ownership, and terms of the option and performance-unit plans.
3. **1988 results and 1988 buyback activity,** including prices paid relative to value.
4. **Management's own post-mortem on Columbia:** what they paid in 1982 and what they think it earned.
5. **How management plans to use the $1.2B of borrowing capacity,** whether buybacks, bottlers or new diversification, and what price discipline governs the 40M-share program.
6. **The economic return on bottler investments including the concentrate profits they generate.** Only management could supply this.

## Stage 3 conclusion

| Rule | Result |
|---|---|
| Able, honest management in place; candid reporting | **Pass.** Proven since 1981; thinks in returns on capital. Reports are promotional in front but candid in the numbers. |
| $1 retained → ≥$1 market value | **Clear pass.** About 6.5:1 raw, about 2:1 even holding the 1981 multiple, roughly 24% on retained capital. |
| Buybacks well below intrinsic value | **Pass for 1984–85; pending Stage 4 for 1986–88.** The stated rationale is weaker than the actions. |
| Share issuance and acquisitions | **Weak 1982–86** (stock issued for entertainment, returns below hurdle). **Corrected in 1987.** |
| Debt | **Acceptable now; watch the 35% ceiling.** |

The packet supports proceeding to valuation. Before committing a large sum, the investor would want the management reservations, especially the risk of another diversification, settled by direct contact with management.
