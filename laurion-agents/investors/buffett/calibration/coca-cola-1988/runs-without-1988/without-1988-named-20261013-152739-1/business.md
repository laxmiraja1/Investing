# Stage 2: Business analysis (The Coca-Cola Company, as of 1989-02-27)

The "Business analysis" section names three tests, in this order: (1) return on equity rather than EPS growth, earned "without undue leverage, accounting gimmickry, etc."; (2) a durable competitive advantage, the moat; (3) owner earnings. Figures come from `ar-1986`, `ar-1987`, `fin-summary` and `prices`.

**Bottom line:** the soft-drink business passes all three tests convincingly. The open questions are how much recent growth came from currency, how the core's capital needs split between maintenance and growth, and what 1988 looked like. None of these is disqualifying, but each limits how precise the valuation in the next stage can be.

---

## 1. Return on equity, not EPS growth (`brk-letter-1979`)

> "The primary test of managerial economic performance is the achievement of a high earnings rate on equity capital employed (without undue leverage, accounting gimmickry, etc.) and not the achievement of consistent gains in earnings per share."

### 1a. The record

Income from continuing operations to average equity, from the 12-year table in `ar-1987`:

| Period | Return on average equity | Total debt / capital |
|---|---|---|
| 1976–1979 | 20.3%, 20.6%, 21.2%, 21.1% | 3.5%–6.3% |
| 1980–1983 | 19.7%, 19.9%, 19.6%, 19.4% | 9.3%–15.1% |
| 1984–1985 | 21.8%, 23.5% | 30.7% (1984), 27.7% (1985 restated) |
| 1986 | 28.8% | 31.4% (restated) |
| 1987 | 27.2% | 45.6% gross; **15.3% net** |

The 1976–1981 rows matter most for this test. Return on equity held at about 20% while debt was 3.5%–9.1% of capital. That is a high return earned with essentially no leverage, before the current management's financial engineering began. The business earned it on its own.

### 1b. Removing one-off items

- **1986:** reported net income of $934M includes a $375M pretax gain on the sale of CCE stock (+$262M after tax, +$0.68 per share). It also includes $180M of restructuring and South Africa provisions (–$128M, –$0.33). Underlying net income was therefore about $800M. Management's own figure agrees: 1987's $916M is "up 14.5%" excluding the 35¢ of 1986 unusual items, which implies about $800M. On average equity of about $3,247M, that is roughly **24.6%**.
- **1987:** the $40M TCC gain and the $36M Foods provision roughly cancel. Net income of $916M on average equity of about $3,370M is **27.2%**, essentially clean.

### 1c. Is leverage producing the return?

Mostly no, with two qualifications.

- **Debt cost is small.** Operating income was $1,324M against interest expense of $279M and interest income of $207M. Net interest is about $72M, roughly 5% of pretax income. Gross debt of $2.70B is offset by $1.47B of cash, $545M of CPE notes (paid in cash on 1988-01-19) and $308M of temporary investments (sold on 1988-01-29).
- **The equity base is being shrunk on purpose.** Equity fell from $3,515M to $3,224M in 1987 because of buybacks ($605M) and the CPE dividend paid in shares ($335M). This raises the ratio mechanically. It is not gimmickry in itself, but it means part of the move from 20% to 27% comes from a smaller denominator rather than better economics.
- **Leverage has moved off the balance sheet.** CCE, 49% owned, carries $2.72B of liabilities against $1.53B of net assets (1987). Coke also has $394M of contingent guarantees (bottlers $93M, CCFC $256M), and CCFC adds about $300M of debt when it is consolidated in 1988. Bottling is capital-hungry, and Coke keeps its own balance sheet light partly by placing that leverage in 49%-owned companies. This is disclosed, not hidden, but it should be noted.

### 1d. Accounting checks

| Item | Assessment |
|---|---|
| Gains on sales of subsidiary stock: $67M (1985), $375M (1986), $40M (1987) | Booked as income. These are real cash or value events, but not recurring earnings. |
| Receivables and contract rights sold with recourse ($618M uncollected at year-end 1986; $325M of CPE receivables guaranteed at year-end 1987) | Entertainment-related and now largely indemnified by CPE. |
| Repeated restatements (CCE 1986; CPE and TCC 1987) | Net income is unchanged each time, but revenue and operating-income trends must be compared on the restated basis. |
| Disclosure | Management separates unusual items in its own comparisons and says current-cost income would be lower than reported. This is reasonably candid. |

No gimmickry large enough to fail the test.

### 1e. The core earns far more than 27%

The consolidated figure blends an exceptional core business with investments that earn little.

**Segment pretax return on identifiable assets, 1987:**

| Segment | Operating income / identifiable assets | Pretax return |
|---|---|---|
| International soft drinks | $1,108.9M / $2,126.7M | **52%** (48% in 1986 excluding the $45M provision) |
| US soft drinks | $323.6M / $2,047.4M | **16%** (about 21% in 1986 excluding the $135M provision) |
| All soft drinks | $1,432.5M / $4,174.1M | **34%** |
| Foods | $66.6M / $627.3M | 11% (about 16% before the $36M provision) |

**Affiliate investments:** about $2.26B carried (CPE $989M, CCE $749M, TCC $84M, other $435M) produced equity income of $118.5M, roughly **5%**. About two-thirds of shareholders' equity sits in these low-return stakes.

A rough illustration: strip out those investments and their income, and the remaining operations earn on the order of $800M after tax on well under $1B of equity. This ignores how debt should be allocated, so it is not precise. Its point is that the concentrate business needs almost no equity.

**On EPS growth**, which the framework treats as secondary: EPS rose from $0.79 (1976) to $2.43 (1987), about 11% a year, and about 13% a year since 1981. Some of that comes from buybacks: average shares fell from 408M (1983) to 377M (1987). The return-on-capital evidence is more persuasive than the EPS record.

**Verdict on Test 1:** passes, and strongly.

## 2. Durable competitive advantage (`brk-letter-1986#16`)

> "The difference between GEICO's costs and those of its competitors is a kind of moat that protects a valuable and much-sought-after business castle."

The question: what keeps competitors from driving soft-drink returns down to normal, and is the gap widening?

### Evidence of a moat

1. **Share and scale lead.** Company brands hold more than 44% of world soft-drink volume excluding the USSR and China, and volume is double the nearest competitor's (`ar-1987`). US share exceeded 39%, nine points ahead of the main competitor (`ar-1986`). The company reports 524 million servings a day in more than 155 countries.
2. **The lead is widening, as GEICO's moat was.**
   - In 1986, Coca-Cola USA's 7% volume gain was more than 60% of total US industry growth. Its sugar-cola volume grew 5% against 1% for all competing sugar colas combined.
   - Over the past four years the fountain business captured nearly three-quarters of segment growth. It outsells its nearest rival more than 2-to-1, and in chain restaurant accounts in 1987 its growth was more than triple the competitor's.
   - In 1987 the food-store cola lead was the widest in more than ten years.
3. **Distribution owned by others but controlled by Coke.** Through the bottler system and fountain accounts, the product is available where people buy drinks. Coke is restructuring the system (CCE, TCC, Germany cut from 102 bottling entities to 68, the UK venture, China) while bottlers carry the capital.
4. **Pricing power over inflation and costs.** Management states it "is able to adjust prices to compensate for increasing costs" (`ar-1986`, `ar-1987` MD&A). The new CCE contracts allow discretionary pricing; prices were raised in October 1986. Gross margin on US concentrate is about 63%. This matches the framework's inflation passages (`fortune-1977-inflation#6`, `brk-letter-1979#2`): a business whose returns survive inflation must raise prices without needing proportionally more capital, and this one can.
5. **The brand survived a serious mistake.** The 1985 change of formula ("new taste of Coke") was a major blunder. By 1986 the company was reporting record US share, and Coca-Cola classic became the top-selling US soft drink. A franchise that can survive a mistake like that is durable.
6. **A long growth runway.** International per-capita consumption was 16% of the US rate in 1986. US per-capita consumption of company products rose from 203 servings in 1980 to 274 in 1987, with a target of 315 by 1990. International volume grew 12% in 1986 and 9% in 1987. Fountain grew 18% in 1987, its 12th straight year of double-digit growth.

### Where the moat is weaker or absent

- **Currency effects.** International soft drinks are about 77% of soft-drink operating income. Key currencies strengthened 27% (1986) and 16% (1987) against the dollar. International operating income rose 25% in 1987, and an unknown part of that is translation, not business performance. The moat is real, but recent growth overstates it.
- **Latin America** has "tremendous unit volume and low profit margins." Hyperinflation and price controls limit pricing power there.
- **Foods (Minute Maid) has no comparable moat.** It is dependent on commodity prices, ranks second in chilled juice, and operating income fell from $120M to $67M ($103M before the provision). It has a brand but not a dominant position.
- **Entertainment (CPE, 49%)** is a hit-driven business that runs counter to the framework's preference for stable earnings (`brk-letter-1987#3`: "Severe change and exceptional returns usually don't mix"). It is now an equity stake earning low single digits on its carrying value. It is not part of the moat, but it does consume capital.
- **Is selling expense the price of the moat?** Selling expenses were $2.11B in 1987, 28% of revenue, including $720M of media advertising. That is the cost of maintaining the brand. It is fully expensed, so reported earnings already bear it, but it is a permanent cost of keeping the moat.

**Verdict on Test 2:** the core soft-drink franchise has a wide and widening moat built on brand, distribution and pricing power. Foods and the entertainment stake do not share it.

## 3. Owner earnings (`brk-letter-1986`)

> "(a) reported earnings plus (b) depreciation, depletion, amortization, and certain other non-cash charges … less (c) the average annual amount of capitalized expenditures for plant and equipment…"

Item (c) in the full definition is the capex needed to maintain long-term competitive position and unit volume. The packet does not split maintenance capex from growth capex, so I bracket the answer.

**1987 inputs:**

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| (a) Reported net income | $916.1M (unusual items roughly cancel) |
| (b) Depreciation | $151.9M |
| (b) Goodwill amortization | $1.6M |
| (c) Capital expenditures, 1985–1987 (restated) | $412M, $346M, $300M; average about $353M |

| Case | Calculation | Owner earnings | Per share (372M shares at year-end 1987) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Conservative: all capex treated as maintenance, using 1987 capex | 916 + 153 – 300 | about **$770M** | about **$2.07** |
| Conservative, using the 3-year average capex | 916 + 153 – 353 | about **$716M** | about **$1.92** |
| Maintenance capex ≈ depreciation | 916 + 153 – 152 | about **$917M** | about **$2.46** |

**Smaller adjustments that roughly cancel:**
- Equity income exceeded dividends received by $79.7M (non-cash).
- The $40M TCC gain was non-cash.
- Deferred taxes were –$28.7M.
- The working-capital release of $272.7M in 1987 reversed uses of $69M (1986) and $77M (1985), so it should not be treated as recurring.

Capex is running at about twice depreciation while revenue grows near 10% a year. Much of the capex is therefore growth (international coolers, fountain equipment, consolidating syrup plants). The real figure is probably closer to the upper end, around **$850–900M, or about $2.30–2.45 per share**. I would value conservatively nearer $2.10 unless the maintenance/growth split can be established.

**A cross-check on how little capital the business needs** (compare `brk-letter-1987#1`: such businesses "can grow while concurrently making almost all of their earnings available"):

| 1985–1987 cumulative | Amount |
|---|---|
| Net income | about $2,572M |
| Cash dividends | about $1,214M |
| Share buybacks | about $1,095M |
| CPE dividend paid in shares | $335M |

Almost all earnings were paid out or used for buybacks, yet underlying earnings rose from about $678M to $916M. Debt rose by about $1.56B. Against that, cash rose about $630M, the $545M CPE notes and the $308M of temporary investments both converted to cash in January 1988. After those, net borrowing barely changed. The payouts were funded from earnings, not debt. This is the clearest sign in the packet of very high returns on reinvested capital.

**Verdict on Test 3:** owner earnings are high relative to reported earnings and close to them, which shows little gap between accounting profit and distributable cash.

## Facts the framework wants that the packet lacks

1. **1988 results, full year or interim.** This is the largest gap. The newest earnings are 14 months old, and the stock has risen from $38 to $46.56 since the end of 1987.
2. **Maintenance versus growth capex.** Owner earnings item (c) can only be bracketed without it.
3. **Currency-neutral growth.** International operating income growth split into volume, local price and translation effects.
4. **Price versus volume breakdown and long-run real price trend for concentrate.** This would confirm pricing power directly rather than from management's statement.
5. **Concentrate business return on its own tangible capital,** separate from bottling, foods and affiliates. Segment assets include some bottling and temporary items, such as the jump in US identifiable assets in 1987.
6. **How system profit is split between Coke and its bottlers,** and Coke's actual exposure if a leveraged bottler such as CCE needed support.
7. **Competitor economics.** Pepsi's share, margins and spending, to measure the moat directly rather than only through Coke's claims.
8. **The size of the current-cost gap.** Management says current-cost income is lower than reported but gives no figure.
9. **The entertainment and foods deployment record.** Returns earned on capital put into Columbia and Minute Maid. This bears on the retained-earnings test, which belongs to the management stage.

---

## Stage 2 conclusion

The soft-drink concentrate business is an exceptional business by the framework's three measures:
- It earned about 20% on equity with almost no debt for a decade, and earns about 27% now. The core segments earn 34% pretax on identifiable assets, and the international business earns about 50%.
- Its moat is wide and widening, built on brand, distribution and pricing power.
- Owner earnings of roughly $770–920M (about $2.07–2.46 per share) are close to reported earnings.

The weaker parts are foods, the entertainment stake, and the affiliates, which earn about 5%. These lower the consolidated return rather than threaten the core. The analysis proceeds to management and capital allocation.
