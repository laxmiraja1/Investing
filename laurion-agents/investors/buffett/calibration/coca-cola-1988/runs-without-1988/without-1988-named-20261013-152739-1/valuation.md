# Stage 4: Valuation (The Coca-Cola Company, price $46.56 on 1989-02-24)

The framework's "Valuation" section contains one rule: buy bargains below net working capital (`bpl-letter-1965`). I apply that rule first because the framework names it. KO fails it completely. I then apply the standard the framework itself puts in its place for outstanding businesses by this date: "an outstanding business at a sensible price, not a mediocre business at a bargain price" (`brk-letter-1987#18`). That standard is judged on owner earnings (`brk-letter-1986`) and on whether the price "will make sense, even should the future of the acquired enterprise develop much along the lines of its past" (`brk-letter-1981#3`). The framework gives no formula for "sensible," so I use the simplest test that matches its terms: the owner-earnings yield plus growth that matches the past record, compared with what a bond pays. I don't build a multi-year discounted cash flow model.

---

## 1. The framework's explicit rule: price below net working capital

> "the company … had net working capital alone (before placing any value on the plants and equipment) of about $19 per share." (`bpl-letter-1965`)

Year-end 1987 balance sheet (`ar-1987`):

| Item | $M |
|---|---|
| Current assets | 4,136.2 |
| Less current liabilities | (4,118.5) |
| **Net working capital** | **17.7** |
| Less long-term debt | (803.4) |
| Less deferred income taxes | (209.9) |
| **Working capital less all long-term liabilities** | **≈ (995.6)** |

Net working capital per share is about **$0.05** on 372.4M shares, against a price of **$46.56**.

**Result:** fails by a margin of nearly 1,000 times. Coca-Cola's value is in its trademark, distribution and pricing power, none of which appear among current assets. On the framework's only explicit valuation rule, this is not a bargain and never would be.

This is the rule the investor says he outgrew: "your Chairman … required only 20 years to recognize how important it was to buy good businesses. In the interim, I searched for 'bargains'" (`brk-letter-1987#18`). The operative test at this date is therefore whether the price is sensible for an outstanding business, the standard behind the three "permanent holdings."

| Measure | Multiple | Yield |

### 2c. Separating the low-return investments

About $2.3B of the market value is equity stakes that earn about 5%: CPE, CCE, TCC and other bottlers. Management puts their market value at "over $2.3 billion" at year-end 1987, and the notes say the publicly traded ones exceeded carrying value by $44M (`ar-1987`). Pricing the core business on its own:

| | $B |
|---|---|
| Market value | 17.34 |
| Plus net debt after the January 1988 cash receipts (2.70 debt − 1.47 cash − 0.545 CPE notes − 0.308 temporary investments) | ≈ 0.38 |
| Less investments at market | (2.30) |
| **Implied price of the core soft-drink and foods operations** | **≈ 15.4** |

- Core after-tax operating earnings: 1987 operating income of $1,324M, plus the $36M one-time provision added back, taxed at 35%, gives about **$884M**.
- Core owner earnings: $884M less $0 to $147M of capex above depreciation gives about **$737–884M**.
- That puts the core at **17.4x–20.9x owner earnings**, a yield of 4.8–5.7%.

The two methods agree. At $46.56 the buyer pays roughly **18–22 times owner earnings** for the core franchise, an owner-earnings yield of about **5%**.

|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

On trailing earnings, today's multiple is the highest of the Goizueta-Keough era. The stock trades near its 52-week high ($49.12) after rising about 22% from the 1988 lows ($35.04).

## 3. "Should the future develop much along the lines of its past" (`brk-letter-1981#3`)

**The past record (Stage 2):**
- Net income from continuing operations grew about 13% a year, from $432M (1981) to $916M (1987), with the share count flat.
- Underlying growth was 14.5% in 1987.
- Most earnings were paid out while that growth continued.

**Adjustments:**
- Part of the 1986–87 growth came from a weaker dollar, with key currencies up 27% and 16%. Unit volume growth was 6–10%.
- A growth range of **8–12% a year** in owner earnings, with no further help from currency, is a fair version of "the past continued."

**The test.** Because the business distributes nearly all its owner earnings and still grows, an owner's annual return is approximately the owner-earnings yield, plus growth, plus or minus any change in the multiple. Starting from the midpoint yield of 4.8% (20.7x):

| Owner-earnings growth | Multiple holds (20.7x) | Multiple falls to 15x in 10 yrs (−3.2%/yr) | Multiple falls to 11x in 10 yrs (−6.1%/yr) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 8% | 12.8% | 9.6% | 6.7% |
| 10% | 14.8% | 11.6% | 8.7% |
| 12% | 16.8% | 13.6% | 10.7% |

**Comparison yardsticks:**
- **Long-term Treasury yield on the as-of date: not in the packet.** I assume about **9%**. Coke's own dollar notes carry coupons of 9⅞% to 12¾% (`ar-1987` Note 7), which supports that level for long-dated high-grade paper.
- **Management's own after-tax cost of capital: about 12%** (`ar-1987` MD&A).

**Reading the table:**
- **Central case** (10% growth, multiple falling to 15x): about **11.6% a year**. That is roughly 2.5 points above the assumed bond yield, and about equal to management's hurdle.
- **Good case** (12% growth, multiple holds): 14–17% a year.
- **Bad case** (8% growth, multiple back to 1983–84 levels of about 11–13x): about 7% a year, below bonds.

### The missing 1988 figures

By 1989-02-27, 1988 results had almost certainly been published, but they are not in the packet. If 1988 simply followed the 1987 pattern, which is an assumption and not a fact, the picture improves by about one year's growth:
- About 14% earnings growth plus a roughly 2% lower share count would give EPS near $2.80 and owner earnings near $2.55.
- The price would then be about **16.6x earnings and 18x owner earnings**, a yield of about 5.5%.
- Every return in the table would rise by about 0.7–1.5 points.

That is the single fact that would most change this valuation.

## 4. Margin of safety against the framework's hurdle

**The framework's explicit hurdle** is price below net working capital, or for "generals," "a substantial excess of value" creating "a comfortable margin of safety" (`bpl-letter-1962#4`). **Not met.** There is no asset cushion, and the price is not a discount to a conservative appraisal.

**The hurdle that governs at this date** for an outstanding business is a sensible price, with safety coming from "a very low probability that anything could drastically change the underlying value" (`bpl-letter-1965`) rather than from the price.

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Is the price sensible on past-like assumptions? | **Yes, but not generously.** The central case returns about 11–12% a year against about 9% bonds. |
| How large is the margin? | About **2–3 points of annual return** over bonds in the central case. **Negative** if growth slows toward 8% and the multiple falls back to its 1983–84 level. |
| What does the margin rest on? | The **durability of the moat** (Stage 2): 44% world share, a widening lead, pricing power, and international consumption at 16% of the US rate. Not on the price. |
| Is Mr. Market offering fear? (`brk-letter-1986`) | **No.** The stock is near its highs at the highest trailing multiple of the decade. The fearful moment in this record was October 1987 ($28.96, about 12x). |

**Conclusion:** at $46.56, KO is a **full but defensible price for an exceptional business**. It is not a bargain. The margin of safety is thin on 1987 figures and moderate if 1988 continued the trend. It depends almost entirely on whether owner earnings keep compounding at roughly 10% or better for a decade.

## 5. Catalyst and timing

| Rule | Application |
|---|---|
| **No catalyst needed** (`brk-letter-1987`: "The market may ignore business success for a while, but eventually will confirm it.") | Applies directly. This is a long-term compounding holding, not a bet on the price correcting. Returns come from the business. |
| **Mr. Market serves, not guides** (`brk-letter-1987`) | The 1988 rise and the high multiple are not reasons to buy. They are a reason to insist the price is justified by owner earnings, which it is in the central case. |
| **Work-outs** (`bpl-letter-1957`) | None. The 40M-share buyback is ongoing capital allocation, not an announced transaction with a timetable such as a merger, tender or liquidation. |
| **Controls** (`bpl-letter-1963`) | Not possible and not relevant. KO is a $17B Dow component held widely by institutions, not a neglected cheap stock. Any stake would be a fractional, passive position in a business with "management in place." |
| **No market forecasting** (`bpl-letter-1966-mid`, `fortune-1977-inflation`) | I make no call on the general market. The question is only whether $46.56 is a sensible price for the earning power, not whether a better entry point is coming. |

## Facts missing that would most change this valuation

1. **1988 earnings and 1988 share repurchases.** These matter most: the trailing figures are 14 months old.
2. **The long-term Treasury yield on the as-of date.** I assumed about 9%.
3. **Maintenance versus growth capex.** This sets where owner earnings fall in the $2.07–2.46 range.
4. **Currency-neutral growth in 1986–88.** This determines whether 10% growth or 8% is the right central case.
5. **Market value of each investee stake (CPE, CCE, TCC) at February 1989.** Only the year-end 1987 aggregate is given.

**Stage 4 result:** KO fails the framework's explicit net-working-capital test. It passes the framework's later "outstanding business at a sensible price" test in the central case, with a thin-to-moderate margin of safety that rests on the franchise's durability rather than on the price. The purchase decision and size go to the next stage on that basis.
