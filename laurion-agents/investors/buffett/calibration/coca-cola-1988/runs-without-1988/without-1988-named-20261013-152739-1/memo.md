**Memo: Purchase of The Coca-Cola Company common stock, as of February 27, 1989**

We recommend buying Coca-Cola common stock at about $46.56 a share, building toward roughly 20% of the portfolio, and holding it indefinitely. The case rests on the quality of the business, not on a low price. Below we explain what the business is, why it meets our standards, what we think it is worth, and what would change our view.

Coca-Cola's main business is selling soft-drink syrup and concentrate, mostly to independent bottlers and to restaurant and other fountain customers. That business produces more than 95% of operating income. The bottlers own the plants, trucks and vending machines and carry most of the debt the system needs. Coca-Cola supplies the brand, the advertising and the flavoring. Company brands account for about 44% of soft drinks sold worldwide outside the Soviet Union and China, twice the volume of the nearest competitor. Around this core sit a citrus and juice business (Minute Maid), 49% stakes in a large US bottler and a Canadian bottler, and a 49% stake in Columbia Pictures Entertainment. In 1987 management moved the film business out of the consolidated company and distributed part of it to shareholders.

The company passes our basic filters easily. It is not a turnaround: income from continuing operations has risen every year we can see, from $278 million in 1976 to $916 million in 1987. The business is simple to understand. Management is already in place and has run the company since 1981. The company is large enough that a meaningful stake would matter to us.

Our main test of a business is the return it earns on the owners' capital without leaning on debt. Coca-Cola earned about 20% on equity from 1976 to 1981, when debt was under 10% of capital. It now earns about 27%, with debt net of excess cash at roughly 15% of capital. The soft-drink business alone does better still: about 34% before tax on the assets it uses, and over 50% outside the United States. Consolidated returns look lower only because about $2.3 billion is tied up in bottling and entertainment stakes earning around 5%.

What protects those returns is a lead that is still widening. In 1986, Coca-Cola's US volume gain accounted for more than 60% of the entire US industry's growth. In restaurant and fountain sales it outsells its nearest rival more than two to one. The brand also came through the 1985 formula change, a serious marketing error, and reached record US share the following year. The room for growth is large: outside the US, people drink about one-sixth as many soft drinks per person as Americans do.

The business also needs little new capital to grow. From 1985 to 1987 the company paid out about $2.3 billion of its $2.57 billion in earnings through dividends and share buybacks, and underlying earnings still rose by more than a third. Owner earnings, meaning reported earnings plus depreciation less capital spending, were between $2.07 and $2.46 a share in 1987, close to reported earnings of $2.43.

Management has done well with the money it kept. Since 1981 the company retained about $2 billion and added about $13 billion of market value. Even with the stock valued at the 1981 multiple, the added earnings justify more than twice the amount retained. Buybacks in 1984 and 1985 were made at 11 to 15 times earnings and plainly added value. The weak spot is the 1982–86 move into films and television, partly paid for with Coca-Cola shares. That move returned far less than the 12% cost of capital management itself sets as its hurdle. We take some comfort that management reversed it in 1987 and now says it prefers to invest within its existing lines of business.

The stock is not a bargain by our older standard. Current assets barely cover current liabilities, so the price has no cushion in working capital. At $46.56 the stock trades at about 19 times 1987 earnings and 21 times owner earnings, the highest multiple of this management's tenure. Our central case is owner-earnings growth of about 10% a year, with the multiple drifting down to 15 times over a decade. On that case, a buyer today earns about 11 to 12% a year. That is a few points above the roughly 9% we assume long-term Treasury bonds pay, and about equal to management's own 12% cost of capital. We put intrinsic value at roughly $45 to $60 a share, with a central figure near $52.

The margin of safety is therefore thin. What makes us willing to accept it is how unlikely it is that anything permanently damages the franchise. That same thin margin is why we are proposing about 20% of the portfolio rather than the 40% we would commit when both certainty and price are strongly in our favor. A meaningfully lower price would justify adding.

Several things would change our view. If growth slowed to around 8% and the market returned to the 11 to 13 times earnings it paid in 1983 and 1984, our return would fall below bonds. Part of the 1986 and 1987 gains came from a weaker dollar. The company also carries some debt outside its own balance sheet through its bottlers and guarantees. Management now has about $1.2 billion of unused borrowing capacity, and spending it on another diversification would concern us. Our materials do not include 1988 results or the 1988 buybacks, and we would want to see both before completing the position. We would also want to meet the two senior executives, Roberto Goizueta and Donald Keough, to judge them in person.

We would sell only if the company's competitive lead clearly eroded, if management began putting retained earnings into businesses that earn less than they cost, or if we found reason to doubt management's candor. A higher stock price alone would not be a reason to sell a business of this quality.
