## Stage 4: Valuation

### 5.1 The framework's valuation rule as written: buy below net working capital (`bpl-letter-1965`)

Year-end 1987 balance sheet (`ar-1987`):

| | $M |
|---|---|
| Current assets | 4,136.2 |
| Current liabilities | 4,118.5 |
| **Net working capital** | **17.7**, about **$0.05 per share** (372.4M shares) |
| Net of long-term debt ($803.4M) and deferred taxes ($209.9M) | about –$996M |

The price is **$46.56**. Book value is $8.66 per share, so the stock trades at 5.4x book. On the rule as written, Coca-Cola is not a bargain by any measure.

### 5.2 Which valuation standard applies on 1989-02-27

The net-working-capital rule dates from 1965. The investor's later writing in the packet explicitly sets it aside for businesses like this one:

- "Our goal is to find an outstanding business at a sensible price, not a mediocre business at a bargain price… In the interim, I searched for 'bargains' – and had the misfortune to find some" (`brk-letter-1987#18`).
- "We may very well pay a fairly fancy price for a Category 1 business if we are reasonably confident of what we are getting," at "prices that will make sense, even should the future of the acquired enterprise develop much along the lines of its past" (`brk-letter-1981#3`).
- The goal is to maximize "intrinsic business value on a per-share basis" (`brk-letter-1983#0`). Owner earnings is the framework's measure of the cash a business produces (`brk-letter-1986`).

So the method I use is per-share owner earnings, extended at rates taken from the company's **past record**, not management's projections ("future projections are of little interest"). The hurdle is the 1981 test: **does the price make sense if the next decade looks like the last one?** The NWC test failing does not disqualify a business Stage 2 judged outstanding.

### 5.3 Owner-earnings base, per share

From Stage 2, I take out all equity income ($118.5M) here and value the investees separately, so they aren't counted twice:

| 1987, $M | Low | Mid | High |
|---|---|---|---|
| Owner earnings (from Stage 2) | 606 (conservative)* | 714 (gross capex) | 820 (net capex) |
| Equity income still included | –39 | –118.5 | –118.5 |
| **Core owner earnings** | 567 | 596 | 702 |
| **Per share (372.4M)** | **$1.52** | **$1.60** | **$1.89** |

*The conservative figure had already deducted the $79.7M of undistributed equity income, leaving $38.8M of investee dividends to remove.

I use **$1.72 per share** as the central core figure. Two cautions:
- 1987 benefited from a 16% move in key currencies. If that reverses, this figure is too high.
- 1988 had a lower US tax rate, worth about 1–2% of net income since only about 25% of pretax income is domestic. This partly offsets the currency risk.

**Investees and other holdings:** carrying value of $2,258.5M (CPE $989M, CCE $749M, TCC $84M, other $435M), about **$6.07 per share**. The packet says publicly traded investees were worth about $44M more than carrying value. Their earnings yield is about 5%, so carrying value is a fair, not conservative, figure. Excess cash and debt are already reflected in net income through net interest.

### 5.4 What $46.56 implies

The core business is priced at $46.56 – $6.07 = **$40.49**. That is 23.5x core owner earnings, an owner-earnings yield of **4.25%**.

At what perpetual growth rate does $40.49 pay a given return? (P = OE × (1+g) / (r – g))

| Required return r | Implied perpetual growth g |
|---|---|
| 9% | 4.6% |
| 10% | 5.5% |
| 12% (management's stated cost of capital) | 7.4% |

**The record for comparison:**
- EPS from continuing operations rose 11.2% a year from 1977 to 1987 ($0.84 to $2.43) and about 13% a year from 1981 to 1987.
- Soft-drink unit volume grew 6–10% a year from 1985 to 1987.
- International per-capita consumption is 16% of the US level.
- The core business can grow on little added capital: revenue +30% on PP&E +8%.

The price assumes roughly half the historical growth rate, continuing forever.

**Discount rate, a figure missing from the packet:** the long-term Treasury yield. As a stand-in I use 9–10%. Coca-Cola's own notes issued in 1985–87 carry coupons of roughly 9⅞–12¾%, its Euroyen issue 5¾%, and management's estimated after-tax cost of capital is 12%. I show 12% as a strict case.

### 5.5 Value if the future resembles the past

Two stages: core owner earnings of $1.72 grow at g₁ for 10 years, then at a lower rate (5%, or 4% in the 7% case), discounted at r. Investees ($6.07) are added.

**Central case (r = 10%, g₁ = 10%, then 5%):**
- Years 1–10: 1.72 × 10 (growth equals discount rate) = **$17.20**
- Year-10 owner earnings: 1.72 × 1.10¹⁰ = $4.46. Terminal value 4.46 × 1.05 / 0.05 = $93.7, present value / 2.594 = **$36.12**
- Core value $53.32 + investees $6.07 = **$59.4 per share**

**Sensitivity, value per share:**

| | g₁ = 7% (then 4%) | g₁ = 10% (then 5%) | g₁ = 12% (then 5%) |
|---|---|---|---|
| r = 9% | $51.4 | $73.6 | $85.3 |
| r = 10% | $43.5 | **$59.4** | $68.3 |
| r = 12% | $33.7 | $43.2 | $49.1 |

Two things to keep in mind:
- 55–70% of each value comes from after year 10. The result depends almost entirely on how durable the franchise is, which is the question Stage 2 addressed.
- The 10–12% columns are roughly what "develop much along the lines of its past" means: 11–13% per-share growth over the last 10 years. Capping it at 10% is a deliberate haircut.

### 5.6 Margin of safety against the hurdle

| Standard | Result at $46.56 |
|---|---|
| NWC bargain rule (`bpl-letter-1965`) | **Fails.** Price is about 900x NWC per share. |
| 1960s "comfortable margin of safety" for generals (`bpl-letter-1962`) | No numerical hurdle is given. On asset or private-owner liquidation value, there is none. |
| **1981 test, "makes sense if the future develops along the lines of its past"** (`brk-letter-1981#3`) | **Passes.** At 10–12% growth and 9–10% discount, value is $59–85. The price is **22–45% below** these figures. At the central $59.4 it is **22% below**. |
| Downside (growth falls to 7%) | Value $43.5 at 10%: price is 7% *above* value. At 12% discount the stock is worth $34, a 27% shortfall. |

The margin of safety does not come from assets. It comes from how far the price's implied growth (4.6–5.5%) falls below the record (11–13%). That gap holds only if the franchise holds. The losses in the downside cases come from growth slowing, not from the business itself being impaired. The currency reversal noted in 5.3 would push 1987's starting figure down, which makes the true margin smaller than the table shows.

### 6. Catalyst / timing (`brk-letter-1987#3`, `bpl-letter-1957`, `bpl-letter-1963`)

- **Work-out?** No. Nothing specific is pending. The CPE spin-off was completed in January 1988. The 40M-share buyback program, running through 1990, will gradually support per-share value, but it is not the type of corporate event a work-out needs.
- **Control?** No. The market value is $17.3B, too large to buy a controlling stake.
- **General / permanent holding: no catalyst needed.** "The market may ignore business success for a while, but eventually will confirm it." The return depends on owner earnings compounding, not on the stock being re-rated.
- **Mr. Market's mood** (no forecasting, `bpl-letter-1966-mid`): the stock is near the top of its 52-week range ($35.04–$49.12), up about 24% from February 1988, and well above the October 1987 low of $28.96. This is not a moment of fear, so price offers no extra cushion. But the framework treats that as a question of valuation, not timing, and on value (5.5) the price is still below the central estimate.

### Figures missing from the packet

1. **1988 results.** The owner-earnings base is 14 months old. If 1988 per-share earnings grew at the 1981–87 rate (~13%, an assumption), the current price would be about 17x rather than 19x, and all values would rise about 13%. If the dollar strengthened, the reverse.
2. **The long-term Treasury yield**, the yardstick for the discount rate. I assumed 9–10%.
3. **Maintenance versus growth capex**, which accounts for the $1.52–$1.89 range in core owner earnings.
4. **Current market value of CCE, CPE and TCC.**
5. **Shares outstanding after 1988 buybacks.** Per-share figures use the 1987 year-end count of 372.4M.

### Stage 4 conclusion

By the literal 1965 rule, Coca-Cola is far from a bargain. By the standard the framework uses for outstanding businesses at this date, a price that makes sense if the future resembles the past, it passes. Intrinsic value is about **$59 per share** on central assumptions, with a range of about $43–85. The price of $46.56 is about **22% below** central value and requires only 4.6–5.5% perpetual growth from a business that has grown 11–13%. The margin depends on the franchise lasting, not on assets, and it disappears if growth falls to about 7%. No catalyst is needed. Stage 5 should set position size on that basis: high confidence in the business and a moderate margin of safety on price.
