## Stage 3: Management and capital allocation

Steps follow the framework's order: (4.1) honest, able management in place with candid reporting; (4.2) buybacks only well below intrinsic value; (4.3) each $1 retained must create at least $1 of market value. Acquisitions and reinvestment are covered under 4.3.

### 4.1 Honest, able management in place; candid reporting (`brk-letter-1979`, `brk-letter-1983#2`)

**In place and able.** Goizueta (Chairman and CEO) and Keough (President and COO) put the current team together in 1981, and they still run it under a stated "Strategy for the 1980s" (`ar-1987` letter). Their results on the packet's numbers:
- Return on equity rose from 19.9% (1981) to 27.2% (1987). Stage 2 showed that much of the recent rise came from leverage; return on total capital is flat at about 20%.
- EPS from continuing operations grew about 13% a year (from $1.17 to $2.43).
- Soft-drink operating margins widened in both the US and international businesses, and world share reached 44%.
- They deliberately narrowed the company back to its franchise. Plastics (Presto, Winkler) and pasta (Ronco) were sold in 1984–85. Bottling moved into 49%-owned affiliates in 1986–87. Entertainment was combined into CPE and half of it spun off in 1987. By 1987 soft drinks were "more than 95 percent of operating income" (`ar-1987`).

Management also states its hurdle in economic terms: investments must earn above an "approximately 12 percent after taxes" cost of capital, and the "primary objective is to increase shareholder value." That is owner-oriented language. Whether they act on it is tested in 4.3.

**Candor: evidence for.**
- The 1986 letter disclosed up front that the year was "enhanced by approximately 35 cents per share" from the CCE gain net of provisions, rather than presenting the $2.42 as clean earnings.
- Bad news is stated plainly: "we were not satisfied with the performance of Columbia Pictures' feature films" and leadership was changed (`ar-1986`); Foods "is obviously in transition" and "experienced a decline in earnings" (`ar-1987`).
- The currency tailwind is quantified each year (27% in 1986, 16% in 1987) rather than folded into growth.
- Management concedes that current-cost income "would be less than reported," and warns that purchasing-power gains on debt "do not necessarily imply that incurring more debt would be beneficial."
- Contingent liabilities are disclosed: $394M of guarantees, about $325M of CPE receivables sold with recourse, and the formula-pricing limit in most US bottler contracts.

**Candor: evidence against.** The framework says "we don't expect a public relations document" (`brk-letter-1979`), and these reports are partly exactly that.
- They contain promotional language: the international "potential appears limitless"; "we are assured of our abilities to quickly seize opportunities"; and a multiplier claiming the system adds "$250 billion to the world economy."
- The 1985 formula change was a serious misstep, yet the 1986 report describes it as "the effort begun in 1985 to revitalize our sugar colas." It never says the company misjudged its customers. Under the framework's standard ("the facts we would want to know if our positions were reversed," `brk-letter-1983#2`), this is the clearest gap.
- The 1987 letter emphasizes total stock return against the S&P, a market-price measure rather than a business measure.

**Accounting quality.** I see no gimmickry, but the accounts are complicated.
- There were three restatements in two years (CCE in 1986; CPE and TCC in 1987). Each is GAAP, each was approved by the auditors, and none changed net income.
- Gains from selling subsidiary stock ($375M and $40M) were booked as income, and the structuring of those transactions created those gains.
- Receivables were sold through an unconsolidated finance subsidiary (CCFC), which FASB 94 forces onto the balance sheet in 1988.

Management discloses all of this, but anyone valuing the company has to adjust the numbers back.

**Compensation and alignment.** The packet shows only part of the picture:
- About 5.6M options outstanding, with 1.1–1.8M granted a year.
- A 3M-share restricted stock plan.
- Performance units on 555,000 shares that pay cash on share-price gains above $20.63.

Dilution from options is modest, roughly 0.3–0.4% of shares a year. The performance units reward the stock price rather than per-share business value, which is a weaker form of alignment. **Not in the packet:** salaries and bonuses, how much stock management owns, and the proxy statement.

### 4.2 Buybacks only well below intrinsic value (`brk-letter-1984`)

From the equity statements:

| Year | Shares bought | Cost ($M) | Average price | Multiple of EPS that year |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1984 | 18.4M | 335.7 | $18.24 | 11.6x ($1.57) |
| 1985 | 15.0M | 379.9 | $25.33 | 14.7x ($1.72) |
| 1986 | 2.9M | 110.5 | $38.37 | about 18.5x ($2.07 ex one-time items) |
| 1987 | 14.2M | 605.1 | $42.61 | 17.5x ($2.43) |
| **Total** | **50.5M** | **1,431** | **$28.34** | |

**Assessment.**
- **1984–85:** Buybacks at 12–15x earnings, for a business earning about 20% on total capital with 35–50% pretax returns in its core, were very likely well below intrinsic value. These meet the rule.
- **1986–87:** About $715M of buybacks were made at about 17.5–18.5x reported earnings, which is roughly 19–26x the owner-earnings range from Stage 2 ($1.65–$2.20). Whether that was "well below" intrinsic value depends on the Stage 4 valuation; it is not obvious. The 40M-share program was announced in July 1987, near the stock's high ($47.68 at month-end; the quarter's range reached $53.13). The packet does not show whether the 6.6M shares bought under it were bought before or after the October crash.
- **Stated rationale.** Management's reason for buying back stock is availability of funds: the company's "available cash and debt capacity exceeded the funds required." Only secondarily do they say that "often the best use of excess cash … has been to invest in our own stock." The framework's test is the price compared with value. Management never states an estimate of intrinsic value, so whether price discipline governs the program cannot be confirmed.
- **Funding.** The 1987 buybacks were financed largely with short-term debt: loans and notes payable rose from $698M to $1,685M. Buying stock at about 17.5x earnings with borrowed money can still create value if the stock is cheap, but it is the kind of leverage-driven ROE improvement Stage 2 identified.

**The round trip.** Average shares were 372M in 1981, rose to 408M by 1983, and fell back to 372M at year-end 1987. The rise coincides with the June 1982 Columbia purchase. Long-term debt also jumped that year, from $132M to $458M. The packet does not state what was paid for Columbia. If, as the share count suggests, roughly 36M shares were issued for it at 1982–83 prices ($9.92–$19.17), then the company later spent about $28 a share buying back stock it had sold for perhaps $13–17. That is the "double whammy" of `brk-letter-1982#17`.

The 1985 Embassy deal is documented: 7.1M shares, valued at about $168.6M (about $23.80 a share, roughly 14x earnings), plus assumed debt. The framework allows paying in stock only "if our owners receive in business value as much as we give." Giving away shares in a 25%-ROE franchise at 14x earnings, for a television producer, almost certainly failed that test.

The net result: buybacks since 1984 have mainly undone earlier issuance rather than shrinking the company's share count. That is a mark against the team's capital-allocation record in 1982–85, even though the later repurchases were sensible.

### 4.3 Each $1 retained must create at least $1 of market value (`brk-letter-1983`)

**Overall test, from the start of 1981 to today:**
- Net income 1981–87: $482M + $512M + $559M + $629M + $722M + $934M + $916M = **$4,754M**.
- Cash dividends (dividends per share × average shares): about **$2,551M**. Retained: **about $2,200M**. If the $335M in-kind CPE distribution is counted as a payout, about $1,870M.
- Market value: 372M shares × $11.13 = **$4.14B** at year-end 1980, against 372M × $46.56 = **$17.34B** now. Shareholders also received 34.1M CPE shares, whose market value the packet does not give, so the increase is understated.
- **Market value rose about $13.2B on about $2.2B retained, roughly $6 for every $1.** The test is passed easily.

Much of that gain comes from a higher valuation multiple: about 10.5x 1980 earnings ($1.06) against 19.2x now. To check, hold the multiple at 10.5x. A price of $2.43 × 10.5 = $25.50 gives a market value of about $9.5B, an increase of $5.3B, still about **$2.40 per $1 retained**. The test is passed without any re-rating.

**By destination, the aggregate hides weak spots.**

| Use of retained capital | Evidence in packet | Grade |
|---|---|---|
| Core concentrate (coolers, vending, fountain, international infrastructure) | Revenue +30% on PP&E +8% (1985–87); international pretax return on assets 52% | Excellent |
| Bottler stakes (CCE, TCC, Britain joint venture, Consolidated, Johnston) | CCE net income $88M on $1.53B net assets (about 5.8%); equity income overall about 5% on about $2.26B carrying value | Below the stated 12% hurdle on their own. Partly defensible as protection of concentrate sales: CCE bought $653M at about 63% gross margin. |
| Entertainment (Columbia 1982; Embassy, Tandem, Merv Griffin 1985–86, about $645M+ plus 7.1M shares) | Entertainment's net income was $109M (1985), $102M (1986) and $51M (1987) on about $1.33B of net assets; CPE took $100M of provisions | Fails management's own hurdle. Management's response was to cut exposure (49% stake, spin-off) rather than call it a mistake. |
| Foods (Canada expansion, new products, Nutri-Foods $30M) | Operating income fell from $123.6M to $66.6M (about $103M before the provision), 1984–87 | Poor. Management is now promising a revival ("reestablish it as a vibrant and growing enterprise"), which the framework treats warily (`brk-letter-1983#2`). |

**Dividend policy going forward.** Management is lowering the payout toward 40% (from 58% in 1984 to 46% in 1987) so it can reinvest more "in opportunities offering returns in excess of the cost of capital." The framework accepts this only if the retained dollars keep earning high returns. Stage 2 showed the core business needs little capital. Higher retention will therefore have to go into buybacks, bottler stakes, or acquisitions, and management's own record shows how acquisitions can go: Columbia, Embassy, Foods. The protection is the stated preference for "investments within our existing lines of business" and the 1987 retreat from entertainment. Judging whether that discipline holds requires knowing the people, and the packet cannot show that.

### What the investor would have relied on that the packet lacks

1. **Meeting Goizueta and Keough.** The framework's management judgments come from personal acquaintance: Cap Cities' managers "tops in both ability and integrity" (`brk-letter-1984#31`), and "the quality of the people we will be joining" (`brk-letter-1986#10`). Whether they would stay away from another Columbia, and how they think about value when buying back stock, cannot be read from annual reports.
2. **The proxy statement:** pay, bonus formulas, insider ownership, and how the board decides on capital allocation.
3. **Columbia acquisition terms:** price, shares issued, and debt. This would confirm or rule out the share-issuance round trip.
4. **Buyback timing and prices after October 1987, and any 1988 repurchases.** The 1988 annual report is not in the packet.
5. **Management's own estimate of intrinsic value or its buyback criteria.**
6. **Market value of the CPE shares distributed,** needed to complete the retention test.
7. **Succession:** the packet says nothing about who follows Goizueta and Keough.

### Stage 3 conclusion

**Management passes the framework's tests, with an imperfect history.**

- **Strengths:**
  - The team is able and has been in place since 1981, and since 1985–87 it has refocused the company on its best business.
  - Each retained dollar has produced about $2.40–$6.00 of market value.
  - The 1984–85 buybacks were bought at prices that were very likely well below value.
  - Disclosure is mostly honest about the numbers, including one-time items, currency effects, and guarantees.
- **Weaknesses:**
  - 1982–86 was a costly detour into entertainment, partly paid for with cheap Coca-Cola stock and later bought back at about twice the price.
  - Some capital still sits in low-return bottler and Foods investments.
  - The buyback rationale rests on available cash rather than price against value.
  - Debt was raised mainly for financial engineering.
  - The glossy reports never acknowledge the 1985 formula mistake.
- **For the decision:** nothing here is a reason to stop. The direction since 1985 is right. Before taking a large, permanent position, the framework would want personal confidence in the managers' judgment, which the packet cannot provide. The weaknesses carry into Stage 4 as reasons to demand a margin of safety in the price.
