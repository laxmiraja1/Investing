**The Coca-Cola Company: Recommendation to Buy, February 27, 1989**

We recommend buying shares of The Coca-Cola Company at around today's price of $46.56. The position should be built toward about 20% of the portfolio, with purchases made only while the shares stay at or below roughly $50. This memo explains the reasoning behind that recommendation and the limits on it.

The core of the company is simple. Coca-Cola makes syrups and concentrates and sells them to bottlers and fountain customers in more than 155 countries. It owns the most recognized trademark in the world and the largest soft-drink distribution system. Its brands account for 44% of world soft-drink volume, double that of its nearest competitor. In the United States it holds over 39% of the market, nine points ahead of its main rival, and 59% of fountain sales. Concentrate carries a gross margin of about 63% and needs little plant. Between 1985 and 1987 revenue rose 30% while net property and equipment rose only 8%. A business that can grow without matching investment holds up well under inflation, and the reported numbers bear that out. Return on equity has been 19% or higher in every year since 1977 and was 27% in 1987. Earnings per share grew from $0.84 to $2.43 over the ten years. The company also passes the screens we apply to any purchase. It is not a turnaround, and its record of earning power is long and consistent. Its management is in place, and it is large enough to matter to us.

Some of these figures need adjusting before they can be trusted. The rise in return on equity since 1985 comes mostly from borrowing and share repurchases. Return on total capital has stayed close to 20%. Debt rose to 45.6% of total capital in 1987, although net of excess cash it is 15%, well inside management's own 35% ceiling. The consolidated figures also understate the core business. They include about $2.3 billion of stakes in bottlers and in Columbia Pictures Entertainment that earn around 5%. Measured on its own assets, the international soft-drink business earned 52% before tax in 1987.

The record of management is good but not flawless. Roberto Goizueta and Donald Keough have run the company since 1981. Over that time each dollar of retained earnings has added roughly $2.40 of market value even if the valuation multiple had stayed at its 1980 level. The 1984–85 buybacks were made at 12 to 15 times earnings. Since then management has sold or deconsolidated plastics, pasta, bottling and entertainment, and soft drinks now provide over 95% of operating income. Against this, the entertainment venture of 1982–86 earned well below the company's own 12% cost of capital. Part of it was paid for in Coca-Cola stock, which the company later repurchased at about twice the price. The annual reports are honest about one-time items, currency effects and guarantees. They never acknowledge that the 1985 formula change was a mistake. The company plans to retain more earnings, so the key question is whether management stays focused on soft drinks. We would want to settle that by meeting management before committing the full position.

We value the company on owner earnings, meaning reported earnings plus depreciation, less the average capital spending the business needs. For 1987 that comes to about $714 million. Excluding income from equity investees, which we value separately at book value of about $6 per share, it is roughly $1.72 per share. Our test is whether the price makes sense if the next decade resembles the last. We assume owner earnings grow 10% a year for ten years, below the 11–13% the company has achieved, and 5% a year after that. Discounting at 10% gives a value of about $59 per share. A plausible range of assumptions gives values from about $43 to $85. The current price implies only 4.6–5.5% perpetual growth, about half the historical rate. Under the old rule of buying below net working capital, the stock is not a bargain. Working capital is negligible and the shares trade at more than five times book value. The margin of safety here rests on how durable the franchise is, not on its assets.

We are not taking the full 40% the framework allows for a single holding, for three reasons. About three-quarters of operating income is earned abroad, and 1987 benefited from a weak dollar. We do not yet have 1988 results. Finally, the margin of safety is gone if long-run growth falls to about 7% or if appropriate discount rates approach 12%. No catalyst is needed. The return should come from compounding inside a holding we expect to keep indefinitely.

Several things would change this view. We would reduce the position or stop buying if 1988 results show weak growth in constant currency, if meetings with management suggest a renewed interest in diversification, or if long-term interest rates rise well above 10%. We would also stop if the price moves above about $50 before the position is built. We would sell if the franchise itself deteriorates, through lasting share losses or lost pricing power with bottlers, or if management issues stock or borrows in ways that hurt owners. A higher share price alone is not a reason to sell. A sharp fall in the price with the business intact would be a reason to buy more.
