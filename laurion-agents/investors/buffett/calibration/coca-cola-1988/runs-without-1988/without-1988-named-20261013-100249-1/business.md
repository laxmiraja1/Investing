## Stage 2: Business analysis

Steps follow the framework's order: (1) return on equity, (2) moat, (3) owner earnings.

### 3.1 Return on equity, not EPS growth (`brk-letter-1979`)

The test is "a high earnings rate on equity capital employed (without undue leverage, accounting gimmickry, etc.)." Three adjustments are needed.

**Reported ROE (income from continuing operations to average equity, `ar-1987` selected data):**

| 1977 | 1979 | 1981 | 1983 | 1984 | 1985 | 1986 | 1987 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 20.6% | 21.1% | 19.9% | 19.4% | 21.8% | 23.5% | 28.8% | 27.2% |

The 10-year average for 1978–87 is about 22%. That is excellent, though below the "over 30%" bar that only six of Fortune's 1,000 cleared (`brk-letter-1987#1`).

**Adjustment 1: one-time items.** 1986 includes a $375M pretax gain on the CCE stock sale (+$262M net) and $180M of provisions (–$128M net), for a net effect of +$134M. Excluding both, 1986 net income is about $800M, or about 24.6% on average equity of $3,247M. In 1987 the $40M TCC gain and the $36M Foods provision roughly cancel, so 27.2% stands. The underlying trend is 23.5%, then about 24.6%, then 27.2%. It is rising, but more slowly than the headline figures suggest.

**Adjustment 2: leverage.** This is where the framework's caveat matters. Equity fell from 64% of assets in 1983 ($2,921M of $4,550M) to 39% in 1987 ($3,224M of $8,356M). Over the same period treasury stock grew to $1,309M and gross debt/capital went from 15.1% to 45.6%. To see what leverage contributes, I computed return on total capital, defined as net income plus after-tax interest expense over average equity plus debt:

- 1985: (678 + 129) / 4,063 ≈ **19.9%**
- 1986 (ex one-time items): (800 + 131) / 4,622 ≈ **20.1%**
- 1987: (916 + 181) / 5,526 ≈ **19.9%**

Return on total capital is flat at about 20%. On a consolidated basis, then, the rise in ROE from 23.5% to 27.2% comes mostly from borrowing and buybacks, not from better business returns. The leverage is not "undue": net debt is 15.3% of capital, operating income covers gross interest 4.7 times, and net interest expense is only $72M because of $207M of interest income. The rising ROE should not be counted as a business improvement.

**Adjustment 3: the core business is hidden inside the consolidated figures.** The $3,224M of equity contains about $2,258M of equity investments: CPE $989M, CCE $749M, TCC $84M and other $435M. These produced $118.5M of equity income, about 5% on carrying value, well below management's stated 12% cost of capital. CCE alone earned $88M on $1.53B of net assets, about 5.8%. Excess cash of about $1.27B also earns money-market rates. Once these are removed, the concentrate business runs on very little capital. Segment pretax operating income on identifiable assets (Note 16):

| | 1985 | 1986 (ex provisions) | 1987 |
|---|---|---|---|
| International soft drinks | 39.1% | 47.7% | **52.1%** |
| USA soft drinks | 14.6% | 20.6% | 15.8%* |
| Combined soft drinks | — | — | **34.3%** |

*USA identifiable assets rose $623M in 1987. This probably reflects temporary bottler holdings such as Miami CCBC, which was sold in January 1988. The packet does not confirm this.

The figures are before tax and on assets rather than equity, since segment liabilities are not disclosed. They still show that the core business earns very high returns on capital, which fits the 1987 letter's description of businesses that "can grow while concurrently making almost all of their earnings available" (`brk-letter-1987#1`).

**Capital intensity, the 1977 inflation test (`fortune-1977-inflation`).** On the restated basis, revenue grew 30% from 1985 to 1987 ($5,879M to $7,658M) while net PP&E grew 8% ($1,482M to $1,598M). The business can grow without a matching increase in capital. That is the trait the inflation article says separates "highly profitable companies" from those that "never can get enough" capital. The borrowing is a choice, not a need. Management says it "is able to adjust prices to compensate for increasing costs" and that its LIFO reserve is small ($19M). It also admits current-cost income "would be less than reported" but does not give the figure.

**EPS growth (secondary).** Continuing EPS rose from $1.17 (1981) to $2.43 (1987), about 13% a year. Net income grew at about the same rate, from $432M to $916M. Buybacks contributed a little: average shares fell from 408M (1983) to 377M.

### 3.2 Durable competitive advantage: the moat (`brk-letter-1986#16`)

GEICO's moat was a measurable cost gap. Coke's, from the packet, is a trademark and distribution franchise, and the packet gives evidence both of its size and of whether it is widening.

**Size of the moat**
- 44% of world soft-drink volume (excluding the USSR and China), double the nearest competitor's volume, sold in 155+ countries at 524M servings a day (`ar-1987`).
- US share above 39%, 9 points ahead of the primary competitor (`ar-1986`). US fountain share is 59%, outselling the nearest rival more than 2 to 1, and Coke took about three-quarters of the segment's growth over four years. Vending share is 49%.
- About 63% gross margin on concentrate sold to US bottlers (`ar-1986`).
- Management says the company is "low-cost producer in most of the 155 countries." This is a claim; the packet has no comparative cost data.

**Is it widening?**
- World share rose a point to 43% in 1986 and to 44% in 1987. US case volume grew 6% in 1987 against about 4% for the industry.
- Soft-drink operating margins rose. International went from 22.9% to 24.5% to **27.0%** (1985 to 1987); USA from 11.6% to 14.5% to **15.3%**. Consolidated gross margin rose from 50.5% to 52.6%.
- **Caveat:** key currencies strengthened 27% (1986) and 16% (1987) against the dollar. International operating income rose 25% in 1987 alongside 9% volume growth, so a large part of the margin gain is currency, and it can reverse. About 74% of operating income comes from outside the US.

**Resilience.** The packet records that management changed the formula in April 1985 and had to bring back the original as "Coca-Cola classic." By 1986 Classic was the best-selling soft drink in the US, and US share reached a record. A franchise that recovers from its own managers' mistake within a year is strong. This also relates to the 1987 letter's point that "severe change and exceptional returns usually don't mix" (`brk-letter-1987#3`): the core product is over a hundred years old and basically unchanged.

**Room to grow.** International per-capita consumption was 16% of the US rate in 1986. US consumption itself rose from 203 to 274 servings per person between 1980 and 1987, so even the most mature market is still growing.

**Pricing power, and where it is limited.**
- The 1986 letter says the strength of a franchise determines whether a tax cut stays with the company (`brk-letter-1986#22`). The test is weak here because US pretax income is only about 25% of the total ($356M of $1,410M) and the effective tax rate actually rose, from 32.2% to 35.0%, as the foreign share grew.
- The direct evidence is mixed. New contracts with CCE allow "flexible pricing" (prices were raised in October 1986). But standard US contracts with most other bottlers fix Coca-Cola syrup at **formula pricing**.
- The bottling system is consolidating. CCE now handles about 45% of US volume, and fewer, larger bottlers will have more bargaining power. Coke offsets this with 49% ownership of the large bottlers, but it also pays cooperative marketing support: a net $53M to CCE in 1987.

**Outside the moat.**
- Foods (orange juice) depends on commodity prices. Operating income fell from $123.6M (1984) to $66.6M (1987, about $103M before the provision), and management calls the segment "in transition."
- Entertainment (now 49% of CPE) recorded $100M of restructuring provisions in 1987, and Coke's share of CPE earnings fell to $48.9M.

Neither belongs to the castle, and together they absorbed a lot of capital. Stage 4 should judge management's capital allocation with this in mind.

### 3.3 Owner earnings (`brk-letter-1986`)

The definition is (a) reported earnings, plus (b) depreciation, amortization and other non-cash charges, minus (c) the average capital spending the business needs. 1987, in $ millions:

| | |
|---|---|
| (a) Net income | 916.1 |
| (b) Depreciation + goodwill amortization | +153.5 |
| Non-cash items: provision +36.4, TCC gain –39.7 | –3.3 |
| (c) Average capex, 1985–87 restated, gross ((411.8 + 345.8 + 299.6) / 3) | –352.4 |
| **Owner earnings, base** | **≈ 714** |
| Less undistributed equity income (79.7), whose value at 5% returns is doubtful | –79.7 |
| Less deferred-tax reversal (taxes paid above book expense) | –28.7 |
| **Owner earnings, conservative** | **≈ 606** |

On 372M year-end shares, owner earnings come to roughly **$1.63–$1.92 per share**, against reported EPS of $2.43.

These figures understate the core business's earning power. The $352M of gross capex includes growth spending (coolers, vending, international production infrastructure) and fixed assets of purchased companies. Net of disposals, average capex is about $249M, which would put owner earnings at about $820M. Capex runs about twice depreciation. The packet does not split capex into maintenance and growth, and the definition asks only for the spending needed "to fully maintain … long-term competitive position and … unit volume." Owner earnings are therefore probably between $1.90 and $2.20 per share, with $1.63 as a floor. Working capital provided cash on average over 1985–87 (+$273M, –$69M, –$77M), so no deduction is needed for it.

### Facts the framework wants that the packet lacks

1. **1988 results.** There are no 1988 filings. Every measure above is a year stale, and nothing shows whether the 1987 currency boost held.
2. **Segment liabilities or equity.** Without them, after-tax return on tangible equity for the concentrate business cannot be calculated; only pretax return on assets is available.
3. **Maintenance versus growth capex.** This is the main uncertainty in owner earnings, roughly a $600M to $820M range.
4. **Current-cost income figure.** Management says it would be lower but gives no number.
5. **Constant-currency operating income by region**, to separate franchise strength from dollar weakness.
6. **Competitor economics**: margins, costs, and the primary competitor's share trend internationally. These are needed to put a GEICO-style number on the moat.
7. **US pricing history** under formula-priced bottler contracts, and price/mix versus volume.
8. **Market value of the equity investees.** The packet gives only "over $2.3B" at end-1987 and a $44M excess over carrying value for the public investees. This is needed to value the non-core assets separately.
9. **Country-level profit concentration.** Japan was the top profit contributor, which concentrates yen exposure; the per-capita table in the 1987 report is illegible in the packet.

### Stage 2 conclusion

- **Core business:** an exceptional franchise. Pretax returns of about 35–50% on the assets it uses, growth that requires little capital, the largest share in a growing world market, and resilience shown in the 1985–86 formula episode.
- **Consolidated ROE:** about 27%, overstated as a measure of improvement. Return on total capital has been flat at about 20% since 1985, so the recent ROE rise comes from leverage and buybacks. It is also understated as a measure of the core, because about $2.3B of low-return investees and $1.3B of excess cash are mixed into the equity base.
- **Owner earnings:** roughly $1.65–$2.20 per share, or about $610–820M.
- **Open risks to carry into valuation:** currency reversal on 74% of operating income, bottler consolidation and formula pricing in the US, and the value of the non-core holdings.
- **Carried to Stage 4:** the poor returns on capital put into entertainment, foods and bottlers.
