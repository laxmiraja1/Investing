# Stage 4: Valuation (Framework §5) and Catalyst / timing (§6)

## 5.1 The rule as written: buy below net working capital

The framework's only valuation rule in force is the Partnership-era bargain test: net working capital per share, before any value is given to plant (`bpl-letter-1965`). Applied to the end-1987 balance sheet (ar-1987):

| Item ($M) | End-1987 |
|---|---|
| Current assets | 4,136.2 |
| Current liabilities | (4,118.5) |
| **Net working capital** | **17.7** |
| Per share (372.4M shares) | **≈ $0.05** |
| Adding back the in-kind dividend payable (settled in stock, not cash) | ≈ $352.7M → **≈ $0.95/share** |
| Current assets less *all* liabilities ($5,131.8M) | **≈ −$996M (negative)** |

Book value is $8.66 per share. At $46.56 the stock trades at about 5.4 times book and roughly 50 to 900 times net working capital. **The stock fails the bargain test completely.** The test also says little about this company. Working capital is thin because the company keeps its cash and its short-term borrowings both in current accounts, and because its value comes from a franchise that earns high returns on very little capital, not from its assets.

**Is this rule the one that governs here?** The investor's own writing before the as-of date says it is not, for a business of this kind:

- "Our goal is to find an outstanding business at a sensible price, not a mediocre business at a bargain price… In the interim, I searched for 'bargains' – and had the misfortune to find some." (`brk-letter-1987#18`)
- "We may very well pay a fairly fancy price for a Category 1 business if we are reasonably confident of what we are getting," with the condition that the price must "make sense, even should the future of the acquired enterprise develop much along the lines of its past." (`brk-letter-1981#3`)
- The goal is per-share **intrinsic business value** (`brk-letter-1983#0`), and §3 supplies the input for it, **owner earnings** (`brk-letter-1986`).

So I record that the net-working-capital test fails. I then value the company the way the framework's later evidence prescribes: capitalize owner earnings, and require that the price still makes sense if the future looks like the past. I do not build a forecast-driven model. Every growth input below is tied to the company's historical record.

## 5.2 Intrinsic value from owner earnings

### Inputs

| Input | Value | Source / basis |
|---|---|---|
| Owner earnings, 1987, cash basis, maintenance capex ≈ depreciation | **$806M ≈ $2.17/share** | Stage 2, §3c |
| Owner earnings, 1987, all capex treated as maintenance | $605M ≈ $1.63/share | Stage 2, §3c (floor) |
| 1988 owner earnings | **Not in packet.** Rolled forward one year at each case's growth rate | Assumption |
| Discount rate | **10%** (sensitivity at 12%) | Packet anchor: the company's own long-term debt costs 9.75–12.25% (notes) and "approximately 10 percent" weighted (other debt); management's after-tax cost of capital is about 12%. The packet has no long Treasury yield. |
| Growth, "future like the past" | **11%** for 10 years | EPS from continuing operations: $0.84 (1977) → $2.43 (1987), ≈11.2% compounded |
| Terminal growth after year 10 | 4–5% | Assumption, below every historical rate |

**Is 11% achievable given the economics (§3)?** On an ex-items basis the company earns about 24.6% on equity (Stage 2). Management is moving the payout toward 40%, and the 1987 cash payout was 46%. Retaining about 54% at about 24.6% supports internal growth of roughly 13% before any buybacks. International per-capita consumption is only 16% of the U.S. rate. An 11% assumption therefore matches the record without relying on management's projections.

### Results (per share; valuation date early 1989)

| Case | Growth, yrs 1–10 | Terminal growth | Discount | PV of 10 yrs | PV of terminal | **Intrinsic value** | Margin vs $46.56 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Recent record (1981–87 ≈ 12–13%) | 12% | 5% | 10% | 26.9 | 61.1 | **$88** | 47% |
| **Future like the past (10-yr record)** | **11%** | **5%** | **10%** | **25.3** | **55.4** | **$81** | **42%** |
| Same, at management's 12% cost of capital | 11% | 5% | 12% | 22.9 | 33.0 | **$56** | 17% |
| Conservative | 8% | 4% | 10% | 21.2 | 33.8 | **$55** | 15% |
| **Break-even (implied by the price)** | **≈6%** | 4% | 10% | 18.9 | 27.5 | **$46** | ≈0% |

How the central case is built ($2.17 base): 1988 owner earnings are $2.41 and 1989 are $2.67. Ten years of growth at 11%, discounted at 10%, are worth $25.32. Owner earnings for 1999 come to $7.18, capitalized at 1/(10% − 5%) to $143.6, which is worth $55.38 today. The total is **$80.70**.

**Sensitivities:**
- **Capex treatment.** If every dollar of capex is treated as maintenance ($1.63 base), each value falls about 25%. The central case becomes about $61 and the conservative case about $41, roughly 11% below the price.
- **Terminal value dependence.** About two-thirds of the central-case value sits beyond year 10. The terminal multiple is about 20 times 1999 owner earnings. The valuation therefore depends on whether the competitive advantage lasts for decades, which Stage 2 judged favourably: a 100-year brand whose share is still widening.
- **Precision.** The investor described his own company's intrinsic value as "a soft number" on which equally informed observers could differ by more than 10% (`brk-letter-1986#0`). The table should be read as a range, roughly **$55–80**, not as a point estimate.

### Items left out that add value

- **Affiliate stakes.** The 49% bottling stake, the 49% Canadian stake, the 49% entertainment stake and other bottler stakes had a market value above $2.3B at end-1987 (ar-1987), or about $6 per share. My cash-basis owner earnings include only the roughly $39M of dividends these stakes paid. Their market value beyond that is an unquantified cushion. Current 1989 market values are not in the packet.
- **Tax rate.** The U.S. statutory rate falls from 40% (1987) to 34% (1988). `brk-letter-1986#22` argues that a strong, unregulated franchise keeps such reductions rather than passing them on. U.S. pretax income is about 25% of the total, so the effect is modest but positive for 1988.

### Cross-check through the primary test

At 5.4 times book, a business earning about 25% on equity gives a buyer an initial return of roughly 4.7%. This is consistent with an owner-earnings yield of 4.7% ($2.17 / $46.56) and a cash dividend yield of 2.6% ($1.20 / $46.56). The return to an owner therefore depends on reinvestment at high rates, through both operations and buybacks below value. That is why the §4 retained-earnings test (passed by a wide margin in Stage 3) carries so much weight here.

## 5.3 Margin of safety against the hurdle

The framework's in-force sections give **no numerical hurdle**. The Partnership "generals" asked for a "substantial excess of value" (`bpl-letter-1962#4`). By the as-of date the standard for a business of this quality is a price that "make[s] sense, even should the future… develop much along the lines of its past" (`brk-letter-1981#3`), together with the avoidance of permanent loss of capital (§9). I apply that as a two-part test:

1. **On the "future like the past" case, the price should sit well below value.** It does: **≈42% below** $81 at a 10% discount rate, and ≈17% below $56 at 12%.
2. **On a pessimistic case, an owner should not suffer a permanent loss.** The price implies only about **6% growth** for a decade, roughly half the company's 10-year record and below its current internal growth capacity. If that is all the business delivers, the buyer still earns about the 10% discount rate. The capital is not lost. The protection comes mostly from the durability of the franchise, not from assets on the balance sheet.

**Result:** the price is a **sensible price for an outstanding business**. It passes the in-force hurdle, though not at the "toad-like" level of the investor's best fractional purchases (`brk-letter-1981#3`). The margin is wide on the record-based case and thin on conservative assumptions.

## 6. Catalyst / timing

- **Category.** This is not a work-out (`bpl-letter-1957`) and not a control situation (`bpl-letter-1963`). It is a long-term holding in an undervalued business, so **no catalyst is needed**: "The market may ignore business success for a while, but eventually will confirm it" (`brk-letter-1987`).
- **Price as servant, not guide** (`brk-letter-1987`):
  - The stock fell to $28.96 in October 1987 and traded between $35 and $39 through most of 1988, with a 52-week low of $35.04. At those prices the central-case margin would have been about 55%.
  - It now stands at $46.56, near its 52-week high of $49.12, still below the August 1987 peak of $53.12, and up about 33% from the low.
  - The best entry point has passed. Under §9, however, the job is to judge value, not to time the market (`bpl-letter-1966-mid`). The current price still sits well inside the value range.
- **Sentiment** (`brk-letter-1986`). Nothing in the packet suggests a speculative mania in this stock. A trailing P/E of 19.2 on stale 1987 EPS likely falls to the mid-to-high teens on 1988 earnings, but those earnings are not in the packet. This is neither a moment of widespread fear nor of excess.
- **Implication for buying.** The company's own 40M-share buyback program competes for the same stock and supports per-share value. The size of any purchase is left to Stage 5.

## Missing data the valuation needs

1. **1988 results.** The 1988 report would be out around the as-of date. Every value above rolls 1987 forward by assumption, and actual 1988 owner earnings could move the range by ±10%.
2. **Maintenance capex** (base $2.17 vs. floor $1.63 per share).
3. **Long-term Treasury yield** on the as-of date, the reference rate for owner earnings. I used the company's own borrowing cost of about 10% as a proxy.
4. **Shares outstanding after 1988 buybacks.** The packet has only the end-1987 count.
5. **Current market values of the affiliate stakes.**
6. **Currency-neutral earnings.** Part of 1986–87 growth came from a weaker dollar, and a reversal would lower the base.

---

## Stage 4 verdict

- **§5 rule as written (net working capital):** fails. The investor had explicitly replaced this test for high-quality businesses by the as-of date.
- **In-force method (owner earnings, future like the past):** intrinsic value of **about $55–81 per share** (central case **$81** at 10%, **$56** at 12%), against a price of **$46.56**. The margin of safety is about **42%** on the record-based case. The price implies roughly 6% growth, about half the historical rate.
- **§6:** no catalyst needed. The price is above its 1988 lows but still well below value. The conclusion is a sensible price for an outstanding business, which supports a buy subject to sizing in the next stage.
