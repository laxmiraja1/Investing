# Memo: Purchase of Company A common stock, as of February 27, 1989

We recommend buying Company A common stock up to about 25% of the portfolio at prices near the last close of $46.56. The reasoning follows the order in which we judge any business: what it is, whether it passes our tests, what it is worth, and what would make us reconsider.

The core business is easy to describe. The company makes soft-drink syrups and concentrates and sells them to a worldwide network of mostly independent bottlers, who carry its brands into more than 155 countries. Soft drinks produce more than 95% of operating income. The company's brands account for over 44% of soft drinks sold worldwide outside the Soviet Union and China, and its 1987 volume was double that of its nearest competitor. In the United States its share reached a record of more than 39% in 1986, nine points ahead of the main rival. Its fountain share is 59%, and the 1987 report says it outsold its nearest rival in fountain by more than two to one. Over the past two years the company has moved its capital-heavy activities, including U.S. and Canadian bottling and the film business, into separately financed affiliates in which it keeps 49% stakes. What remains with the parent is a trademark business that needs very little capital. The foods unit, mainly orange juice, is a small and mediocre exception.

The business passes our tests on its long record, not just its recent one. The test we care about most is return on equity earned without heavy borrowing. From 1976 through 1983 the company earned 19% to 21% on equity while debt was only 4% to 15% of capital, including during the inflation of the late 1970s, when most American companies did much worse. More recently, reported returns of 27% to 29% are partly the product of share buybacks and added debt, so we discount them. Even after removing one-time items, 1986 earned about 25%. The soft-drink segment alone earned roughly 34% pretax on its assets in 1987. Earnings are also close to cash. Depreciation of $152 million is small next to operating income of $1.32 billion. After deducting normal reinvestment, we estimate owner earnings at about $806 million, or $2.17 per share, against reported earnings of $2.43.

The company's position also looks secure and is getting stronger. The brand is a hundred years old and survived the company's own 1985 change to its flagship formula, regaining record share within a year. The company is the low-cost producer in most of its markets and has shown it can raise prices to bottlers. Consumption per person outside the United States is only about one-sixth of the U.S. level, so there is a long runway for growth.

Management has handled capital well since the current team took over in 1981. It sold its pasta and plastics businesses and refocused on soft drinks. It set an explicit 12% hurdle rate for new investment. Between 1984 and 1987 it bought back about 50 million shares, and the 1984–85 purchases were made at 11 to 15 times earnings. Our simplest test is whether retained earnings have produced at least a dollar of market value for each dollar kept. Since 1980, about $1.8 billion of retained earnings has been followed by roughly $13 billion of added market value. Some of that reflects a higher price-to-earnings multiple. Even so, the earnings increase alone shows retained capital earning about 20% after tax.

We have reservations, and partners should weigh them:
- The company spent most of the decade in the film business and issued stock to acquire part of it.
- Management openly favors more borrowing, with a target ceiling of 35% net debt to capital.
- The annual reports read more like promotion than plain business accounts.
- Executive incentive pay is tied to the stock price, not to returns on capital.
- We have not met management.

The old test of buying below net working capital does not apply here. Net working capital is close to zero and the stock trades at about 5.4 times book value. What matters is whether the price makes sense if the next decade resembles the last one. Earnings per share grew about 11% a year over the past ten years. Projecting that rate for ten more years, slowing to 5% afterward, and discounting at about 10%, gives a value of roughly $81 per share. Discounting at 12% instead gives about $56, and a cautious case with 8% growth gives about $55. The current price assumes only about 6% growth, roughly half the company's record and less than it can fund internally. That cushion is our margin of safety. It rests on how durable the franchise is, not on any assets we could sell. If growth disappointed down to the level the price assumes, we would still expect a fair return rather than a loss.

On sizing: the business is about as unlikely to suffer a sudden collapse in value as anything we could own, which would justify a large position. We are holding at about 25% rather than our 40% maximum for four reasons:
- Our figures stop at 1987, because 1988 results were not in the information we had.
- About three-quarters of operating income is earned abroad, so a stronger dollar would reduce reported earnings.
- On conservative assumptions the margin of safety is thin.
- We have not met management.

We would add more on prices back in the 1988 range of $35 to $39. Our view would also change with:
- weak 1988 results after removing currency effects;
- evidence that ongoing reinvestment needs are much larger than depreciation;
- a large acquisition outside soft drinks;
- borrowing above the 35% ceiling.

We intend to hold indefinitely. We would sell only if the competitive position wears away, if returns earned without heavy borrowing fall below about 18% to 20% for several years, if retained earnings stop creating value, or if we find we misjudged the business. A high stock price alone is not a reason to sell.
