# Stage 2: Business analysis (Framework §3)

I apply the three rules of §3 in order: return on equity, then durable competitive advantage, then owner earnings. Figures come from ar-1986, ar-1987 and fin-summary.

---

## 3a. Return on equity, not EPS growth, is the primary test

The rule asks for "a high earnings rate on equity capital employed (without undue leverage, accounting gimmickry, etc.)" rather than consistent EPS gains (`brk-letter-1979`). The test therefore has three parts: the level of ROE, whether leverage produced it, and whether one-off accounting items produced it.

**Long record.** Income from continuing operations to average equity, from the selected-data tables:

| Year | ROE | Total debt / capital |
|---|---|---|
| 1976 | 20.3% | 3.5% |
| 1977 | 20.6% | 3.5% |
| 1978 | 21.2% | 3.8% |
| 1979 | 21.1% | 6.3% |
| 1980 | 19.7% | 9.3% |
| 1981 | 19.9% | 9.1% |
| 1982 | 19.6% | 15.1% |
| 1983 | 19.4% | 15.1% |
| 1984 | 21.8% | 30.7% |
| 1985 | 23.5% | 27.7% |
| 1986 | 28.8% | 31.4% |
| 1987 | 27.2% | 45.6% (gross) / 15.3% (net, company basis) |

*(1982 onward on the 1987 report's restated basis.)*

The years 1976–1981 are the cleanest evidence. The company earned about 20–21% on equity with almost no debt, through the high-inflation period in which, by `fortune-1977-inflation`, most American businesses were stuck near 12% and had to borrow to hold even that. A business that earned 20% unlevered in those conditions passes the primary test on its own merits.

**The rise since 1984 needs adjusting.** ROE moved from about 20% to 27–29% at the same time that debt to capital rose from about 15% to 30–45%. Over the same years the company bought back about 50M shares; treasury stock went from $12M in 1983 to $1,309M in 1987, which shrinks the equity denominator. The 1987 equity figure is also reduced by the $335M in-kind dividend of entertainment-affiliate stock. Part of the recent improvement is therefore balance-sheet engineering, which is exactly what the rule tells us to strip out. Two cross-checks:

- *1986, excluding one-offs.* Reported net income of $934M includes a $262M after-tax gain on the bottler IPO and a $128M after-tax restructuring charge, a net +$134M. Excluding these gives about $800M on average equity of $3,247M, or **about 24.6%**.
- *1987, return on all capital.* Net interest expense was $72M ($279M expense less $207M income), about $47M after a 35% tax rate. Unlevered earnings of about $963M over average total capital (debt plus equity) of about $5,526M gives **about 17%**. This measure is too harsh, because roughly $2.1B of the debt was offset by cash, notes due from the entertainment affiliate, and temporary investments that were all converted to cash in January 1988. Using the company's net-debt basis, unlevered return on capital falls between 17% and the low 20s.

**The core business on its own.** The segment notes show the concentrate business is the source of the returns:

| Segment, 1987 | Operating income | Identifiable assets | Pretax return on assets |
|---|---|---|---|
| Soft drinks, U.S. | $323.6M | $2,047M | 16% |
| Soft drinks, International | $1,108.9M | $2,127M | 52% |
| **Soft drinks, total** | **$1,432.5M** | **$4,174M** | **≈34%** |
| Foods | $66.6M ($103M before $36M charge) | $627M | 11% (16% before charge) |

In 1986, before the $180M of provisions, soft drinks earned about $1,181M on $3,287M of assets, about 36%. These returns are on total assets, before any benefit from payables or debt. After tax at about 35%, that is roughly 22–24% on all assets employed. The return on the net tangible capital that owners actually have to supply is higher still.

The businesses that were spun out or deconsolidated earn much less. The company's share of the bottling affiliate's 1987 net income was $43M on a $749M carrying value (about 6%). The entertainment affiliate earned about $49M on roughly $1B. Moving these to the equity method makes the company's own statements show the concentrate economics more clearly, and also shows that the low-return parts of the system are capital-hungry and highly leveraged. The bottling affiliate had $2.72B of liabilities against $1.53B of net assets.

**EPS growth, given as a secondary figure.** EPS from continuing operations went from $0.84 in 1977 to $2.43 in 1987, about 11% compounded. Management says that excluding unusual items, 1987 EPS rose 17.4% and operating income 23%. However, key currencies strengthened 16% against the dollar in 1987 and 27% in 1986, and about 74% of operating income is earned outside the U.S. Part of the recent growth is therefore translation, and the packet does not let me separate it out.

**Conclusion on 3a: passes clearly.** The long-run unlevered record of about 20% ROE, the core segment's return of roughly 34–36% pretax on assets, and earnings that are not inflated by one-offs together meet the standard. The recent jump to 27–29% should be valued at something closer to the low-to-mid 20s, because leverage and buybacks contributed part of it.

## 3b. Durable competitive advantage

`brk-letter-1986#16` describes the advantage as something that protects a valuable business from competitors and that a good manager keeps widening. The question here is what the advantage is, whether it is getting wider, and what could breach it.

**What protects the business, according to the packet:**
- **Brand and share.** The brand is described as "the world's best-known trademark." The company's brands account for more than 44% of soft drinks sold worldwide outside the USSR and China, and 1987 volume was double the nearest competitor's. U.S. share was above 39% in 1986, nine points ahead of the main rival.
- **Channels where the lead is strongest.** U.S. fountain share is 59%, outselling the nearest rival more than 2 to 1. The company captured about three-quarters of fountain-segment growth over four years, and its lead is wider still in chain and fast-food accounts. U.S. vending share is 49%.
- **System scale and cost.** The system operates in more than 155 countries through bottlers, and the company describes itself as "low-cost producer in most of the 155 countries." That cost position is the same kind of advantage `brk-letter-1986#16` credits to GEICO.
- **Pricing power.** The 1986 bottler contracts include flexible pricing, and prices were raised in October 1986. Average U.S. concentrate gross margin is about 63%. Management states it "is able to adjust prices to compensate for increasing costs." In 1987 soft-drink operating income rose 21% on 6% volume growth, helped by price increases, lower sweetener costs and currency.
- **Durability.** The brand is 100 years old, dividends have been paid every year since 1920, and the 1985 reformulation episode (the "new taste" launch, followed within months by bringing back the original formula as "classic") ended with record share in 1986. A franchise that survives its owner's own mistake is strong evidence that consumers, not the company, hold the loyalty.
- **Low capital need.** Management describes the core business as having "low capital requirements and high return on investment." Soft-drink capex in 1987 was $170M, against roughly $1.4B of segment operating income.

**Is it widening?** Yes, on the evidence here:
- U.S. share reached a record in 1986, and the lead in food-store colas is the widest in more than ten years.
- Fountain grew by 14,000 net new accounts in 1987.
- International volume has grown at double digits or close to it (12% in 1986, 9% in 1987).
- International per-capita consumption was only 16% of the U.S. rate in 1986, while U.S. per-capita consumption itself rose from 203 servings in 1980 to 274 in 1987. The long runway is outside the U.S.

The tax argument in `brk-letter-1986#22` points the same way. A strong, unregulated franchise keeps the benefit of a lower corporate tax rate instead of passing it on in prices. The rise in 1987 operating margins is consistent with that, although the packet cannot isolate the tax effect.

**Possible breaches to watch:**
- **Advertising dependence.** Selling expenses were $2.11B on $7.66B of revenue in 1987, and media advertising $720M. The franchise has to be maintained with heavy spending, and the 1985 heavy marketing year shows how much these costs can swing.
- **Bottler health.** The largest U.S. bottler, a 49% affiliate handling about 45% of U.S. volume, is heavily leveraged. The company guarantees $93M of independent bottlers' debt and $256M owed by its finance subsidiary.
- **Currency and country risk.** About three-quarters of operating income is earned abroad, Latin America is described as "tremendous unit volume and low profit margins," and earlier in the decade the company pulled out of South Africa.
- **Capital allocation outside the core.** Foods earned only 11–16% on its assets in 1987 and is described as "in transition," and the company spent most of the 1980s in film. The core is a strong franchise, and the open question is what management does with the cash it produces. That belongs in §4.

**Conclusion on 3b: a wide and widening advantage**, based on brand, distribution scale and cost position, with pricing power the packet documents directly.

## 3c. Owner earnings

The rule from `brk-letter-1986` is reported earnings, plus depreciation, amortization and other non-cash charges, minus the average annual capitalized expenditure needed to maintain the business.

**1987 calculation ($M):**

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Net income | 916.1 |
| + Depreciation | 151.9 |
| + Goodwill amortization | 1.6 |
| + Non-cash restructuring provision | 36.4 |
| − Non-cash gain on Canadian bottler IPO | (39.7) |
| − Deferred tax benefit (non-cash credit) | (28.7) |
| **Subtotal before capex** | **≈1,037.6** |
| − Equity income not received in dividends | (79.7) |
| **Cash-basis subtotal** | **≈957.9** |

**Maintenance capex is the figure the packet does not give.** Two bounds:
- **Conservative,** treating all capex as maintenance. Average restated capex for 1985–87 was ($412M + $346M + $300M) / 3 ≈ $353M. Owner earnings ≈ $958M − $353M ≈ **$605M**, or about **$1.63 per share** on 372M shares.
- **Realistic,** treating maintenance as roughly equal to depreciation ($152M). Owner earnings ≈ **$806M**, or about **$2.17 per share**, on a cash basis. If the retained earnings of the affiliates are counted on a look-through basis, the figure is about **$886M**, or **$2.38 per share**.

A large share of capex is clearly for growth, including international bottling infrastructure, fountain equipment and the new office building. The 1985 figure also includes bottling assets that were later deconsolidated. My working estimate is **about $800M, or roughly $2.15–2.20 per share**, a little below reported EPS of $2.43.

I exclude the 1987 cash-flow benefit of a $273M decrease in working capital, mostly from higher payables and accrued taxes. It is not a recurring source of owner earnings.

**Quality of earnings.** Reported earnings and owner earnings are close together, and depreciation is a small fraction of operating income. Both point to a business that converts accounting profit into distributable cash, the opposite of the capital-hungry case in `brk-letter-1985#9`. Management also states that earnings measured at current cost "would be less than reported" but does not give the number. Two off-balance-sheet items reduce the clarity of the figures somewhat: the unconsolidated finance subsidiary (which will add about $300M of debt to the balance sheet when consolidated in 1988) and entertainment receivables sold with recourse ($325M, indemnified by the entertainment affiliate).

## Facts the framework wants that the packet lacks

1. **1988 results.** The latest figures are 14 months old, and the 1988 report would be due around the as-of date. ROE, owner earnings and the trend in share all need updating.
2. **Maintenance versus growth capex**, which owner earnings cannot be pinned down without.
3. **Currency-neutral growth.** I would want volume, price/mix and translation effects shown separately for international operating income.
4. **Margins by geography for concentrate alone,** separating out the roughly 4% of international volume the company bottles itself.
5. **Current-cost earnings,** which management says exist but does not quantify.
6. **Economics of the main competitor,** to measure the cost and share gap directly, as `brk-letter-1986#16` does for GEICO.
7. **Bottler system finances** beyond the main affiliate's summary: leverage, profitability, and the risk of being pressed to support bottlers.
8. **Current market value of the affiliate stakes.** The packet gives about $2.3B at end-1987, and in 1986 the bottling stake was 68.6M shares at $14.25 ≈ $978M. Values as of February 1989 are not given.
9. **Foods sector returns over a longer period**, to judge whether it destroys value or is merely mediocre.

---

## Stage 2 verdict

Applied in order, the §3 tests describe an exceptional core business:
- **3a:** about 20% unlevered ROE for more than a decade, with the soft-drink segment earning roughly 34–36% pretax on assets.
- **3b:** an advantage based on brand, distribution and cost that the packet shows is widening.
- **3c:** owner earnings of about $800M (≈$2.15–2.20 per share), close to reported profit, because the business needs little capital.

The caveats for §4 (management and capital allocation) and §5 (valuation) are these. Recent ROE is partly the product of leverage and buybacks, recent growth is partly currency, and the record of using cash outside the core is mixed.
