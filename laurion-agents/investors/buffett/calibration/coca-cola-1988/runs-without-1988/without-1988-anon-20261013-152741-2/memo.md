**Memo: Purchase of Company A common stock, as of February 27, 1989**

We recommend buying Company A, building toward a holding of about 20% of the portfolio. Most of it would be bought at or below today's price of $46.56, and more aggressively if the price fell to about $40.

Company A is, at bottom, a seller of soft-drink syrup and concentrate. Independent and partly owned bottlers turn that concentrate into finished drinks and sell them in more than 155 countries. This core business produced more than 95% of 1987 operating income.

The company also has three other pieces. It owns 49% stakes in large bottlers in the United States, Canada and Britain, and a 49% stake in an entertainment company it spun off in 1987. It runs a juice business, which is small and struggling. And it holds smaller bottler investments.

The 1987 annual report is the latest full year we have. No 1988 results were available to us.

The company passes each of our screens:
- **It is not a turnaround.** Net income rose every year from $331 million in 1977 to $916 million in 1987.
- **It is simple to understand.** There is no technology to judge.
- **It is large enough to matter.**
- **Its debt is a choice, not a need.** The core business does not require borrowing. Gross debt has risen to about 46% of capital, mostly to fund share repurchases. Net of excess cash and receivables collected in January 1988, it is about 15%.

Return on equity is the test we weigh most heavily, and here the record is unusually clean. From 1977 to 1981 the company earned 20–21% on equity while holding more cash than debt. It did so even though that equity also carried bottling plants, a food business and idle cash, all of which earned less.

Return on equity has since risen to about 27%. About three or four points of that come from leverage and from shrinking equity through buybacks. The rest is real.

The concentrate business itself earns roughly 34–37% before tax on gross identifiable assets, and about 52% internationally. After tax and on the capital actually used, we estimate 40–50%. Very few large companies earn returns like that for long.

The returns last because the advantage is structural:
- **Share and scale.** The company sells about 44% of the world's soft drinks, outside the Soviet Union and China, and has twice the volume of its nearest competitor. In U.S. fountain sales it outsells its main rival more than two to one.
- **Cost and pricing.** It is the low-cost producer in most of its markets, and its gross margin on U.S. concentrate is about 63%.
- **Proof of durability.** In 1985 the company replaced its flagship formula and had to bring the original back. Within a year the original was the best-selling soft drink in the country and company share was at a record. A franchise that survives its owner's own blunder that well is durable.
- **Room to grow.** Consumption per person outside the U.S. is about one-sixth of the U.S. level.
- **Growth costs little.** Soft-drink capital spending was only about $170 million in 1987, against $1.43 billion of operating income.

Management deserves a mixed verdict. The team in place since 1981 has run the core business well. Return on equity has gone up, margins have widened, and the dividend payout has been cut so that more cash can go to buybacks. About 50 million shares were repurchased from 1984 to 1987, and another 40 million are authorized through 1990. Each dollar of earnings retained since 1981 has been matched by roughly six dollars of market value.

Against that:
- **Entertainment.** Management spent the mid-1980s moving capital from a business earning 40–50% into entertainment, where returns fell below its own 12% cost of capital.
- **Share issuance.** In 1985 it issued 7.1 million shares at about $24 to buy a television producer, while buying back stock at about $25.
- **Low-return stakes.** Roughly $1.9 billion now sits in affiliate stakes earning 3–6%.
- **Promotional reports.** The annual reports are candid with the numbers but promotional in tone.
- **What we don't know.** We do not know how much stock the directors and officers own, and we have not met them. That matters here.

Our valuation does not use the working-capital test of our earlier bargain-hunting years. By that test Company A would fail badly, as would every business we now hold permanently. Instead we ask what the business is worth if the next decade resembles the last.

In 1987 the core business produced about $760 million of owner earnings. That is reported earnings plus depreciation, less capital spending, with the affiliates and debt stripped out. It grew at roughly 11% or better over the past decade. We extend that growth for ten years, then assume 4% after, and discount at 10%. That rate is close to what the company pays on its own bonds.

On those assumptions the core business is worth about $22.4 billion. Adding the affiliates at roughly market value and subtracting net debt gives about $64 a share. If 1988 grew in line with the past, the figure is about $70. At $46.56 that is a discount of 27–33%.

The cushion is thinner on harsher assumptions. If growth halves, or if we demand 12% instead of 10%, the value comes out at about $47–52, close to the price. So our protection lies mainly in how durable the franchise is, not in a large statistical discount.

The stock is also not cheap against its own history. It trades at 19 times 1987 earnings, close to its 52-week high, and it sold in the high $30s for much of 1988. We do not count on the market to correct anything. The business compounding its earnings will do the work.

We are therefore sizing the holding at about half the 40% ceiling we reserve for situations where both the facts and the price leave almost no room for error. The business clears that bar; the price and the management record do not quite.

Several things would change our view:
- **Growth.** 1988 results showing underlying growth well below 10% once currency effects are removed. The weak dollar has flattered recent figures.
- **Owner earnings.** Evidence that maintenance spending is close to total capital spending.
- **Management.** Signs that management is drifting back toward diversification, or issuing stock on poor terms.
- **Interest rates.** Long-term rates well above 10%.

We would sell if share losses or eroding concentrate margins showed the franchise weakening, if a large acquisition fell outside soft drinks, or if we found we had misread the economics. A rising share price alone would not be a reason to sell. A business of this quality, competently run, is one we would expect to hold indefinitely.
