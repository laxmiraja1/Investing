# Stage 3: Management and capital allocation

**Conclusion.** Management passes, with clear reservations.

- **What earns the pass:** Since 1981 this team has steered the company back toward the concentrate franchise. They put return on equity at the center of their stated goals and cut the dividend payout so more cash could go to buybacks. They have bought back about 50 million shares in four years and committed to 40 million more.
- **What holds it back:**
  - The team spent several years moving capital out of a business earning roughly 50% into entertainment, where it earned far less.
  - In 1985 it issued shares for an acquisition at about the same price it was paying to buy shares back.
  - Its 1986–87 buybacks were made at prices that do not look *well* below value.
  - Its annual reports often read as promotion.
- **What would decide it:** The judgment that matters most is one the packet cannot support, namely the character of the two senior executives and how much of the stock they own. The framework would rely on meeting them for that.

## 1. Honest, able management in place; candid reporting (`brk-letter-1979`, `brk-letter-1983#2`)

### Ability: the operating record since the team was assembled in 1981

- Return on equity rose from 19.9% in 1981 to 27.2% in 1987 (`ar-1987` selected data).
- Income per share from continuing operations rose from $1.17 to $2.43.
- Soft-drink margins widened. International operating margin went from 22.9% to 27.0%, and U.S. operating margin from 11.6% to 15.3% (1985–87).
- Gross margin rose from 49% in 1983 to 52.6% in 1987.
- Management describes the core business as "rapid volume growth, high margins, strong cash flow, low capital requirements." That is an accurate description, and they have run it well.

### Candor (`brk-letter-1979`: "we don't expect a public relations document")

Points in favor:
- The reports state a cost of capital and a debt ceiling, and set return-on-equity targets.
- Growth figures are shown excluding unusual items. The 1987 letter strips out 35¢ of 1986 one-time items.
- The reports disclose how much exchange rates helped: key currencies rose 27% in 1986 and 16% in 1987.
- An inflation note says current-cost income would be lower than reported, and that purchasing-power gains "do not represent an increase in funds available."
- The 1986 letter admits it was "not satisfied" with the film studio, and the 1987 letter calls Foods "obviously in transition."

Points against:
- The reports lean on promotional lines: international potential that "appears limitless," a "sensational year," and "$250 billion to the world economy" derived from a "multiplier factor of five."
- The 1986 headline of 41% EPS growth includes the CCE gain, although the gain is disclosed.
- The reports repeatedly highlight total return on the stock, and one says the stock split put the price "in a range attractive to individual and institutional investors." That attention to the share price is the reverse of the framework's focus on the business.
- The 1986 report predicted "healthy growth levels" for Foods, and Foods shrank the next year (`fortune-1977-inflation`: forecasts tell more about the forecaster).
- "Increasing shareholder value" appears throughout. `brk-letter-1984#4` warns that such phrases count for nothing unless they are backed by what management has actually done.

Overall the reports are candid on the numbers and promotional in tone.

### Board and alignment

The board consists of bankers, an investment banker, industrial CEOs, a university president and a law partner. The packet gives **no figures on how many shares directors or officers own**. The framework's "eat our own cooking" test (`brk-letter-1983#0`) cannot be applied.

### Compensation (from the packet only)

- Plans: 3M shares of restricted stock, a 1983 option plan of 6M shares, a 1987 option plan of 8M shares with stock appreciation rights, and cash performance units on 555,000 shares measured against a $20.63 base price.
- Outstanding options are 5.6M, about 1.5% of shares. Annual grants are 1.1–1.8M shares, about 0.3–0.5% a year, and buybacks more than offset the dilution.
- All of these awards are tied to the share price, not to returns on capital or per-share value.
- Individual pay amounts are not in the packet.

## 2. Buybacks only well below intrinsic value (`brk-letter-1984#4`)

*EPS is from continuing operations.

**What has been demonstrated.** The key word in `brk-letter-1984#4` is "demonstrated." Management has cut average shares from 408M (1983) to 377M (1987), about 7.5%. It has also announced a 40M-share program, more than 10% of the company. The 1987 report says plainly: "Often the best use of excess cash generated by our businesses has been to invest in our own stock." That fits the pattern the framework praised at GEICO and the Washington Post.

**Price discipline.**
- The 1984–85 purchases, at 12–15 times earnings for a franchise earning more than 20% on equity, were very likely well below value.
- The 1986–87 purchases were at about 17–18 times earnings. At the 1987 average price, owner earnings of $1.77–2.17 per share (Stage 2) give a yield of only about 4–5%.
- To beat management's own 12% hurdle at that price, owner earnings would need to grow about 7–8% a year for a long time. That is plausible given underlying growth of about 17%, but it is not obviously "well below" value. Stage 4 will decide this.
- Management's stated reason for buying back stock is that cash and borrowing capacity exceeded the company's needs. It does not say the stock was cheap relative to its value.
- The 1987 purchases were made both before and after the October crash, at an average close to the year-end price. There is no evidence of buying more heavily when the price fell.
- Purchases made in 1988, when the stock traded at $35–40, are not in the packet.

**Funding.** In 1987 short-term debt rose by about $1.2B, partly to pay for buybacks. This is a deliberate choice to borrow more, with the ceiling raised to 35% net debt to capital. The framework prefers little or no debt, so it would accept this only while net leverage stays modest. It was 15.3% at the end of 1987.

**The share-issuance problem (`brk-letter-1982#17`).** In 1985 the company bought back 15.0M shares at an average of $25.33. In the same year it issued 7.1M treasury shares, worth about $168.6M or about $23.83 each, to pay for Embassy, a TV production company. If the stock was cheap enough to buy back at $25, it was too cheap to hand over at $24 for a lower-return business. The framework's rule is to issue shares "only if our owners receive in business value as much as we give." On the packet's evidence this deal fails that test. This is the clearest single mark against management's thinking about per-share value. It is now four years old.

## 3. Each $1 retained must create at least $1 of market value (`brk-letter-1983`)

All figures are per share, from year-end 1981 (when the team was in place) to year-end 1987.

| | Per share |
|---|---|
| Net income 1982–87 (1.32 + 1.37 + 1.59 + 1.84 + 2.42 + 2.43) | $10.97 |
| Cash dividends | ($5.79) |
| CPE shares paid out as a dividend | ($0.90) |
| **Earnings retained** | **about $4.28** |
| Share price change, $11.58 → $38.13 | **+$26.55** |
| Same, to $46.56 at 1989-02-24 | +$34.98 |

On this measure each retained dollar was matched by about $6 of market value through 1987, and about $8 through February 1989. The test is passed by a wide margin.

Two caveats:
- **Part of the gain is a higher valuation multiple.** The P/E went from about 8.9x (end 1981) to 15.7x (1987) to 19.2x now. Even at a constant 8.9x, the $1.26 rise in EPS would be worth about $11, or about $2.6 per retained dollar. The test still passes without any rerating.
- **The company-wide result hides the misallocated pieces.** Entertainment and the bottler stakes, about $1.8–2.3B of carrying value earning 3–6%, have probably not created a dollar of value for each dollar retained, since their market values are close to carrying value. The overall result is carried by the concentrate business, which needs little capital, and by buybacks.

**Looking forward.** Management plans to cut the payout ratio toward 40% (it was 65% in 1983 and 46% in 1987) and to retain more "in opportunities offering returns in excess of the cost of capital." Soft-drink capex is only about $170M a year, so most of the extra retained cash will go to buybacks, bottler stakes or acquisitions. The buybacks pass the test if they are made at sensible prices. On the packet's record, the bottler stakes and acquisitions have not. This is the main capital-allocation risk to monitor.

## Information the framework would have relied on that the packet lacks

1. **Meeting the chairman and the president.** Their integrity, how they think about intrinsic value, whether they understand that the concentrate franchise is the business, and whether they are cured of diversifying. The framework judged its other holdings largely on people it "likes and admires" (`brk-letter-1987#18`).
2. **Insider and director ownership.** The proxy statement is not in the packet.
3. **Pay levels and performance measures.** Salaries, bonus formulas, and what triggers the performance units.
4. **Buyback activity and prices in 1988.** In particular, whether management bought heavily during the $35–40 period after the crash.
5. **The 1982 purchase price of the film studio.** Without it, the full loss on the entertainment detour cannot be measured.
6. **Look-through economics of CCE and the other 49% stakes.** Their returns, their debt, and how price and marketing terms between Company A and CCE are set.
7. **Succession and management depth** below the top two executives.
