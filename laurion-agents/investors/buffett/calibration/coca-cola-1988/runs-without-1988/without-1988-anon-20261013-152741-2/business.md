# Stage 2: Business analysis

**Conclusion.** The soft-drink concentrate business inside Company A passes all three business-analysis tests clearly.
- **Returns:** it earned about 20% on equity for years while holding net cash. Today it earns around 40–50% after tax on the capital it actually uses.
- **Moat:** it is protected by brand, share, distribution and pricing, and it held up under a severe self-inflicted test.
- **Owner earnings:** it turns most of its reported profit into cash for owners.

The consolidated figures are less clean than the core business. Part of the rise in ROE comes from borrowing to buy back stock and from paying out a business as a dividend. A large share of the balance sheet now sits in affiliates that earn about 5%. And roughly a third of reported earnings are not owner earnings. Those points go into the valuation and the Stage 3 questions on management.

All figures below are on the restated basis in `ar-1987`, which reports entertainment and the Canadian bottler as equity-method affiliates.

## 1. Return on equity, not EPS growth (`brk-letter-1979`)

The test is a high return on equity "without undue leverage, accounting gimmickry, etc." I take the three parts of that sentence in order.

**a) Reported ROE (income from continuing operations ÷ average equity, `ar-1987` selected data)**

| 1977 | 1978 | 1979 | 1980 | 1981 | 1982 | 1983 | 1984 | 1985 | 1986 | 1987 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 20.6% | 21.2 | 21.1 | 19.7 | 19.9 | 19.6 | 19.4 | 21.8 | 23.5 | 28.8 | 27.2 |

**Adjusting for one-time items.**
- 1986 includes a $262M after-tax gain from the CCE share sale and $128M of after-tax provisions, a net boost of about $134M.
- Underlying 1986 net income is therefore about $800M. Average equity was $3,247M, so underlying ROE was about **24.6%**, not 28.8%.
- In 1987 the $40M TCC gain and the $36M Foods provision roughly cancel out. The 27.2% stands.
- On this basis underlying EPS grew about 17%, from $2.07 to $2.43. Management states the same figure.

**b) Leverage**

*Long-run test.* From 1977 to 1981, debt was 3.5–9.3% of capital. In 1977 the company held $351M of cash against $57M of debt, so it had net cash. It still earned about 20% on equity. Over that period equity also carried excess cash, bottling plants, foods and, from 1982, film. A 20% return with no net leverage, on a diluted asset base, is the key fact here. Only a handful of the 1,000 largest companies averaged above 30% (`brk-letter-1987#1`).

*Current test, 1987.*
- Gross debt is $2,702M, or 45.6% of capital, which looks heavy.
- Netting out $1,468M of cash and the $545M of CPE notes collected in January 1988 leaves net debt of about $690M.
- Net interest expense was $279M − $207M = $72M pretax, about $47M after tax.
- If all net debt were replaced with equity, 1987 return on equity plus net debt would be about **23.6%** ($963M ÷ about $4.08B average capital).
- So leverage adds about 3–4 points. That is noticeable but not undue.

*Where the rise from about 20% to 27% came from.*
1. **Shrinking the denominator.** Treasury stock grew from about $12M in 1984 to $1,309M in 1987, much of it debt-financed. The in-kind dividend of CPE shares removed another $335M of equity.
2. **Shedding lower-return businesses.** Bottling went to CCE and TCC, and entertainment went to CPE.
3. **Genuinely better soft-drink margins.** See below.

Items 2 and 3 are real improvements in the economics. Item 1 is partly financial engineering.

*Leverage that has been moved off the parent's books.*
- CCE has about $2.7B of liabilities, up from $2.36B. Company A owns 49%.
- The parent guarantees $394M of others' debt.
- It also guarantees $325M of CPE receivables sold with recourse.
- CCFC, the finance subsidiary, will bring about $300M onto the balance sheet in 1988.

Looked at through to the bottlers, the whole system carries considerably more debt than the parent alone.

**c) Accounting quality**
- **Recurring "one-time" items.** There were gains of $67M (1985), $375M (1986) and $40M (1987) from selling shares in bottling subsidiaries, and provisions of $180M (1986) and $36M (1987). Reported earnings need cleaning every year.
- **Restatements.** Revenue was $8,669M as originally reported for 1986 and $6,977M as restated. Each deconsolidation rebases the history.
- **Equity income is mostly not cash.** Of $118.5M of 1987 equity income, $79.7M was not distributed.
- **Pension changes.** Changes to pension accounting reduced expense by $13M in 1987 and $7M in 1986.
- **Low goodwill ($74M).** Equity is almost entirely tangible, so ROE is not inflated by purchase accounting.

**d) EPS growth, which the framework treats as a secondary test.** EPS from continuing operations grew from $0.84 in 1977 to $2.43 in 1987, about 11% a year. Share count changed little until 1984. The growth was earned from a high and rising return on capital rather than simply by retaining more capital, which is what the framework wants to see.

**Return on the core business itself.** Following `brk-letter-1987#1`, managers should be judged on the assets they actually use. The table shows soft-drink operating income over gross identifiable assets, excluding provisions.

| Soft drinks | 1985 | 1986 | 1987 |
|---|---|---|---|
| International: operating income ÷ assets | 39% | 48% | 52% |
| U.S.: operating income ÷ assets | 15% | 21% | 16% (about 19% excluding the $308M temporary bottler investment) |
| Combined | 27% | 36% | 34–37% |
| International operating margin | 22.9% | 24.5% | 27.0% |
| U.S. operating margin | 11.6% | 14.5% | 15.3% |

These are pretax returns on gross assets, before deducting payables. As a rough estimate, the after-tax return on net capital in the core business is about 40–50%.

The rest of the balance sheet earns much less:
- About $2.3B of equity-method investments earn about 5%. CCE earned $43M on $749M carrying value, and CPE earned $49M on $1,437M.
- The publicly traded investees are worth only about $44M above carrying value.
- Foods earned $103M before its provision on $627M of assets, a 7% margin, in what management calls a "difficult business climate."

The consolidated ROE understates the concentrate franchise and overstates everything else.

## 2. Durable competitive advantage (`brk-letter-1986#16`)

The question is whether there is a durable structural advantage that competitors cannot close, and whether management is widening it. The packet offers strong evidence on several fronts.

- **Scale and share.**
  - The company sells 44% of soft drinks worldwide, excluding the USSR and China.
  - Its 1987 volume was twice the nearest competitor's.
  - U.S. share was above 39% in 1986, 9 points ahead of the main competitor.
  - Fountain share was 59%, outselling the nearest rival more than 2-to-1. The company captured about three-quarters of fountain growth over four years, and its lead is wider still in chain restaurants.
- **Low-cost producer.** The company says it is "low-cost producer in most of the 155 countries" (`ar-1987`). This is the same kind of advantage the framework found at GEICO. The packet gives no competitor cost figures to check it against.
- **Durability under stress.** In April 1985 the company replaced its flagship formula. It reintroduced the original as "Brand A classic." By 1986 the original was the best-selling U.S. soft drink, and company share reached an all-time high (`ar-1986`). The franchise absorbed a severe self-inflicted mistake and came out stronger. That is direct evidence of a durable moat.
- **Little change in what is sold.** The product line is a century old. `brk-letter-1987#3` notes that "severe change and exceptional returns usually don't mix." This is the steady kind of business.
- **Pricing power and inflation.**
  - Gross margin on U.S. concentrate was about 63% (`ar-1986`).
  - Management believes it "is able to adjust prices to compensate for increasing costs."
  - It raised prices to CCE under the new flexible contracts.
  - Gross margin rose from 44% in 1977 to 52.6% in 1987, partly because of the switch to concentrate.
  - A business with high returns, low capital needs and pricing power is the best defense against inflation (`fortune-1977-inflation#6`).
- **Tax.** A strong, unregulated franchise keeps the benefit of a corporate tax cut (`brk-letter-1986#22`). But 75% of 1987 pretax income ($1,054M of $1,410M) is foreign, so the effective rate actually rose from 32.2% to 35.0%. The U.S. rate cut matters less here than it does for the Berkshire investees named in that letter.
- **Room to grow.**
  - Consumption per person outside the U.S. was 16% of the U.S. level (1986).
  - U.S. consumption per person rose from 203 to 274 servings between 1980 and 1987.
  - International volume grew 9–12% a year, and international fountain volume has grown at double digits for 12 years.
- **Widening the moat.** Management is consolidating bottlers (Germany went from 102 bottlers to 68), switching to concentrate, adding fountain outlets (14,000 net new in 1987) and pushing coolers and vending machines. All of this deepens distribution. This matches the GEICO pattern.

Limits and weak spots:
- **Contract pricing in the U.S.** Contracts with most U.S. bottlers other than CCE use formula pricing for Brand A syrup (`ar-1986`). Pricing power in the U.S. is limited by contract, and U.S. returns are clearly below international.
- **Upkeep costs.** Media advertising was $720M and selling expense $2.11B in 1987. Cooperative advertising runs at 19–21% of related revenue. The moat requires heavy annual spending, although it is all expensed.
- **Currency.** International operating income rose 25% in 1987, but key currencies strengthened 16% (27% in 1986). Underlying local-currency growth is meaningfully lower.
- **Price sensitivity.** Volume in Germany fell 4% after price increases. Latin America has high volume but low margins.
- **Businesses without a moat.** Foods (second in chilled juice, exposed to commodity costs) and film (hit-driven) show no durable advantage. Both are now small or moved off the books.

## 3. Owner earnings (`brk-letter-1986`)

Owner earnings are reported earnings plus depreciation, amortization and other non-cash charges, minus average capital spending needed to maintain the business. I start from "working capital provided by operations," which already makes the non-cash adjustments: it adds back depreciation and provisions, and removes gains and undistributed equity income.

| $M | 1985 | 1986 | 1987 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Working capital provided by operations | 771 | 810 | 959 |
| Less capex, treating all of it as maintenance (low case) | (412) | (346) | (300) |
| **Owner earnings, low case** | **359** | **464** | **659** |
| Less depreciation as a stand-in for maintenance (high case) | (130) | (151) | (152) |
| **Owner earnings, high case** | **641** | **659** | **807** |
| Reported net income | 722 | 934 | 916 |

For 1987, owner earnings were about **$660–810M**, or **$1.77–2.17 per share** on 372M shares, against reported EPS of $2.43.

The gap with reported earnings has three causes:
- About $80M of undistributed affiliate income that has not been received in cash.
- A $40M non-cash gain.
- Capital spending running about twice depreciation.

The core soft-drink business needs very little capital. U.S. and international capex was $170M against $103M of depreciation, to produce $1.43B of operating income. Most of the remaining capex was for foods ($55M) and corporate buildings and computers ($74M). Capex is falling, from $412M to $346M to $300M, as bottling moves into affiliates. True maintenance spending is probably near the high case.

One exclusion: the 1987 cash flow also included a $273M working-capital release. I leave it out because it is not repeatable.

## Facts the framework wants that the packet does not have

1. **1988 results.** No 1988 filings are in the packet. This is the largest gap, since the price is quoted 14 months after the last report.
2. **Maintenance versus growth capex.** Owner earnings swing by about $150M depending on this split.
3. **Local-currency operating income by region.** Without it, franchise growth cannot be separated from currency movements.
4. **Competitor cost and margin data.** This would test the "low-cost producer" claim the way GEICO's expense ratio was tested.
5. **U.S. economics.** Concentrate price per case, bottler margins, the remaining term of formula-priced contracts, and why U.S. returns on assets (15–20%) trail international (about 50%).
6. **Look-through financials of the 49% affiliates.** Their debt, returns on capital, and retained earnings, to judge whether the about $2.3B parked in them earns its keep.
7. **A consistent ten-year history for the soft-drink segment alone.** The packet only gives segment data from 1985. Earlier consolidated figures mix in bottling, foods and film.
8. **Share and profitability by country outside the U.S.** Japan is described as the top profit contributor but no figures are given.
