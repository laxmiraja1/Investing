# Stage 4: Valuation

**Conclusion.**
- **The framework's only stated valuation rule fails badly.** That rule is to buy below net working capital. Company A trades at roughly 50 times its net working capital and about 5.4 times book value.
- **That rule comes from the Partnership's "bargain" period, and the framework's own 1987 evidence rejects it for businesses like this one.** So I apply the test the framework uses for outstanding businesses: owner earnings, valued on the assumption that the future looks broadly like the past (`brk-letter-1981#3`).
- **On that test the stock is worth about $64–70 a share, against a price of $46.56.** That is a margin of safety of about 27–33%. The margin disappears if growth halves or the required return is 12%.
- **The price is sensible for a great business, but it is not a bargain.** The protection comes mostly from how durable the franchise is, not from a discount to a conservative valuation.
- **No catalyst is needed.**

## 5.1 The "Valuation" section rule: buy below net working capital (`bpl-letter-1965`)

Figures are from the 1987 balance sheet in `ar-1987`.

| $M | |
|---|---|
| Current assets | 4,136.2 |
| Current liabilities | (4,118.5) |
| **Net working capital** | **17.7**, about $0.05 a share |
| Add back the CPE share dividend owed, extinguished in January 1988 | +335.0 |
| **Adjusted net working capital** | **about 353**, about $0.95 a share |
| Less long-term debt (803) and deferred taxes (210) | Net-net figure is **negative** |

At $46.56 the stock trades at about 49 times adjusted net working capital per share. On this rule the answer is **fail**.

That failure does not settle the decision, for three reasons drawn from the framework's own evidence:
1. **The rule belongs to the Partnership's bargain-hunting era.** In 1987 the investor wrote that it took "20 years to recognize how important it was to buy good businesses. In the interim, I searched for 'bargains' – and had the misfortune to find some" (`brk-letter-1987#18`).
2. **The goal by 1989 is different.** It is "an outstanding business at a sensible price, not a mediocre business at a bargain price" (same passage).
3. **None of the permanent holdings would pass it.** For example, Capital Cities was bought at $172.50 a share (`brk-letter-1984#31`), far above working capital.

Stage 2 found an outstanding business. The working-capital test is therefore the wrong tool here. The framework's own instruments for this case are owner earnings (Business analysis) and the 1981 acquisition test.

## 5.2 Owner earnings, on the assumption the future follows the past (`brk-letter-1986`; `brk-letter-1981#3`)

The 1981 test is to pay only prices "that will make sense, even should the future of the acquired enterprise develop much along the lines of its past." The letter also allows "a fairly fancy price for a Category 1 business if we are reasonably confident of what we are getting."

### Step 1: Split the company into its parts

Figures are at year-end 1987, the latest balance sheet in the packet.

- **Stakes in affiliates (CCE, CPE, TCC and others)**
  - Carrying value: $2,257M, less the $335M of CPE shares paid out as a dividend, gives **about $1,922M**, or $5.16 a share.
  - The packet says the market value of the public stakes is only about $44M above carrying value, so I count them at carrying value.
  - These stakes earn about 3–6%, so valuing them at market is generous rather than conservative.
- **Net debt: $581M**, or $1.56 a share.
  - This is total debt of $2,702M less $1,268M of excess cash, the $545M of CPE notes collected in January 1988, and $308M of temporary investments sold in January 1988.
  - The figure matches management's stated 15.3% net debt to capital ratio.
  - Off-balance-sheet guarantees are not included. CCFC's debt, about $300M, will come onto the books in 1988, but it is offset by CCFC's receivables.
- **What the market is paying for the core business**
  - Market value is $17,337M (372.36M shares × $46.56).
  - Subtracting the affiliates and adding back net debt gives a core business value of **about $15,996M**, or $42.96 a share.

### Step 2: Core owner earnings for 1987

I start from the Stage 2 owner-earnings range of $659M to $807M. Two adjustments make it a measure of the core business alone:
- Remove the $39M of dividends received from affiliates, because the affiliates are valued separately.
- Add back about $47M of after-tax net interest, because net debt is subtracted separately.

That gives **$667M to $815M, with a central estimate of $760M.** Stage 2 judged true maintenance capex to be near depreciation, so the central figure sits toward the high end of the range.

At the current price, the core business is valued at about **21 times owner earnings**, an owner-earnings yield of about **4.75%**.

### Step 3: Assumptions, because the packet lacks some inputs

- **Discount rate: 10%.** The packet gives no Treasury yield. The company's own dollar notes due 1992 pay 9⅞%, so I round that up. I also test 12%, which is management's own stated after-tax cost of capital.
- **"Past continues" growth: 11% a year for 10 years.**
  - Per-share earnings from continuing operations grew about 11% a year over 1977–87 and about 13% over 1981–87.
  - Underlying per-share earnings grew about 17% in 1987.
  - So 11% is the conservative end of the company's own record.
- **Growth after year 10: 4%.**
- **Starting year: 1987 actuals.** No 1988 results are in the packet. I show separately what happens if 1988 grew in line with the past, at +10%.

**A quicker cross-check.** Add the 4.75% owner-earnings yield to the 11% historical growth rate. That gives an expected return of about **15–16% a year** if the valuation multiple stays the same, compared with 9⅞% on the company's own bonds. If growth halves, the expected return drops to about 10%, roughly the same as the bonds.

Three things are worth noting about this growth. It needs almost no new capital: soft-drink capex is about $170M a year. Consumption per person abroad is only 16% of the U.S. level, which leaves room to grow. And the franchise came through the 1985 formula episode stronger. All three make the "past continues" case reasonable rather than optimistic. They also mean a buyer is relying on the franchise lasting, not on today's earnings being cheap.

## 5.3 Margin of safety against the hurdle

**The framework sets no number for the hurdle.** For bargain stocks in the Partnership era it demanded a "comfortable margin of safety" and a price "substantially below" what a private owner would pay (`bpl-letter-1962#4`, `bpl-letter-1963#5`). For outstanding businesses it accepts a "fairly fancy price" if it is "reasonably confident of what we are getting" (`brk-letter-1981#3`).

I translate that into a two-part hurdle:
1. Price should be comfortably below value when the past is assumed to continue. I take "comfortably" to mean at least 25%.
2. Price should not be above value when growth is materially worse than the past.

**Result: both parts are met.**
- On part 1, the margin is 27% on the 1987 base and 33% if 1988 grew in line with the past.
- On part 2, the price roughly equals the value in Scenario B and Scenario C.

The cushion is thin if the pessimistic cases come true. The downside protection rests on the moat's durability (Stage 2), not on a statistical discount.

**What weakens the margin.**
- The stock is at about 19 times reported earnings and 21–26 times owner earnings. That is above every multiple in the packet's history: 8.9 times at end-1981, and 11.6–17.6 times at the 1984–87 buyback prices.
- It is near its 52-week high of $49.12.
- It traded at $35–38 for much of 1988.

**What strengthens it.**
- Underlying earnings have grown about 17% a year and are compounding at core returns of 40–50% after tax.
- If 1988 matched the past, today's price is on earnings already 14 months stale.

## 6. Catalyst and timing

- **No catalyst is needed** (`brk-letter-1987`: "The market may ignore business success for a while, but eventually will confirm it"). The case rests on owner earnings compounding, not on a re-rating.
- **Work-outs.** There are none. The CCE, TCC and CPE transactions are already complete. The 40M-share buyback program running through 1990 supports per-share value but is not a work-out with a timetable (`bpl-letter-1957`).
- **Controls.** Not applicable. At a $17B market value, the stock is neither neglected nor cheap enough to buy a controlling stake (`bpl-letter-1963`).
- **Timing.** The framework does not forecast the market (`bpl-letter-1966-mid`). Prices were lower after the October 1987 crash, when others were fearful. That is in the past, and waiting for a repeat would be market timing. Nothing in the packet shows unusual greed about this stock now. Its multiple is high relative to its own history but not extravagant for the core business's returns. "Mr. Market is there to serve you, not to guide you" (`brk-letter-1987`). Today's price is acceptable, and a lower price would simply be a better one.

## Inputs the valuation would want that the packet does not contain

1. **1988 results and the 1988 share count.** These alone move the value by about $6 a share.
2. **The long-term Treasury yield on the as-of date.** I used the company's 9⅞% note coupon as a stand-in.
3. **Maintenance capex versus growth capex.** This swings core owner earnings by about $150M, roughly ±$7 a share.
4. **Current market values of CCE, CPE and TCC.** The latest available is year-end 1987.
5. **Currency-neutral growth.** Without it, the past growth rate overstates the "past continues" case to the extent that the weak dollar flattered 1986–87.
