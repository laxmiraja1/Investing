# Stage 4: Valuation (framework Section 5) and Catalyst / timing (Section 6)

## 5.1 The framework's stated valuation rule: buy below net working capital

The only valuation rule in Section 5 is the Graham test from the partnership years. The 1965 example was a company whose "net working capital alone (before placing any value on the plants and equipment)" was about $19 a share (`bpl-letter-1965`).

**Applied to the end-1987 balance sheet ($M):**

| Measure | Amount | Per share (372.4M shares) |
|---|---|---|
| Current assets | 4,136.2 | |
| − Current liabilities | (4,118.5) | |
| **Net working capital** | **17.7** | **$0.05** |
| NWC adding back the $335M CPE dividend payable (paid in stock, not cash) | 352.7 | $0.95 |
| Net-net: current assets − all liabilities ($5,131.8M) | (995.6) | **−$2.67** |
| Book value | 3,223.8 | $8.66 |

The price is **$46.56**. Even on the generous figure, it is about 49 times net working capital. On the strict net-net basis there is no working-capital cushion at all.

**Result: under the literal Section 5 rule, Company A fails completely.** It has never been close to a Graham bargain. It traded at 3.6 to 4.4 times book from 1985 to 1987 and is about 5.4 times end-1987 book today.

## 5.2 Why the NWC rule does not decide the case at this date

The investor's own writings from 1981 to 1988 show that the NWC test had been demoted to one tool among several. It applies to "generals" and mediocre businesses, and the investor no longer treats it as the test for great businesses:

- "Our goal is to find an outstanding business at a sensible price, not a mediocre business at a bargain price… In the interim, I searched for 'bargains' – and had the misfortune to find some. My punishment was an education in the economics of short-line farm implement manufacturers, third-place department stores, and New England textile manufacturers" (`brk-letter-1987#18`). Those were exactly the NWC-type bargains.
- Price is set so purchases "will make sense, even should the future of the acquired enterprise develop much along the lines of its past"; "we may very well pay a fairly fancy price for a Category 1 business" (`brk-letter-1981#3`).
- Paying "six times book value" for a high-return business "does not change [its] return on equity" (`brk-letter-1987#1`).
- The yardstick is "per-share intrinsic business value" (`brk-letter-1983#0`, `brk-letter-1984#4`). The input to it is the owner-earnings measure from Section 3 (`brk-letter-1986`).

So the operative method at this date is to **capitalize owner earnings, assuming the past record continues and without counting on improvement**. That is what I do below. The framework does not specify a formal discounted-cash-flow model or a discount rate. The calculation is therefore kept to two plain stages, and every assumption is stated.

## 5.3 Owner-earnings valuation

**Base owner earnings, from Stage 2.** 1987 owner earnings were about **$2.30 a share**, within a range of $2.05 to $2.46. The low end allows for the currency tailwind reversing.

**Gap: 1988 results are not in the packet.** The 1988 dividend was raised to $1.20, about 7% above 1987's $1.12. Under management's policy of raising dividends more slowly than earnings, that suggests 1988 EPS grew faster than 7%, but this is an inference, not a figure. I start the projection from 1987 and grow it forward.

**Past record, which supplies the growth assumption (no management projections used):**

| Period | Measure | Compound annual rate |
|---|---|---|
| 1977–1987 | EPS, continuing operations, $0.84 → $2.43 | ~11.2% |
| 1977–1987 | Net income, $310M → $916M | ~11.4% |
| 1981–1987 | EPS, $1.17 → $2.43 | ~12.9% (includes buybacks and the 1985–87 currency boost) |

Internal capacity check: ROE is about 25% and the company retains about 55–60% of earnings, which would support roughly 14–15% growth without outside capital. A 10% assumption for the next decade is therefore *below* the record, as the 1981 test requires.

**Discount rate. Gap: the packet gives no Treasury yield.** It gives only management's own after-tax cost of capital of about 12% (ar-1986, ar-1987) and the company's note coupons of 9⅞% to 12¾%. I use:
- **9% as the base case.** This is my assumption for the long-term government bond yield in early 1989. It is the risk-free alternative for Berkshire's capital.
- **12% as a stress case.** This is management's own hurdle.

**Model.** Owner earnings grow at *g* for 10 years from the $2.30 base, then 5% a year indefinitely. All values are per share.

| Growth for 10 yrs | Discount 9% | Discount 12% |
|---|---|---|
| 8% (well below record) | **$77** | $43 |
| 10% (slightly below record) | **$90** | $50 |
| 12% (at the 1981–87 record) | $106 | $58 |

Worked example, 9% discount and 10% growth:
- Years 1–10: earnings start at $2.53. The present value of that growing stream is about $24.2.
- Year-10 owner earnings are $5.97. Growing at 5% gives $6.26 in year 11.
- Terminal value is $6.26 ÷ (0.09 − 0.05) = $156.6. Discounted 10 years, that is $66.1.
- Total intrinsic value is about **$90**.

**Simple check without a model.** Owner-earnings yield on today's price:
- About 4.9% on 1987 owner earnings.
- About 5.4% if 1988 grew at the 10-year rate (my assumption, not a packet figure).

If owner earnings grow 10% a year, they reach about $6.53 a share by 1999, roughly a 14% yield on a $46.56 cost. At 8% growth, about $4.97, or 10.7%. Either outcome beats a 9% bond held for ten years, *because* the business keeps compounding.

**Items already inside owner earnings (not added separately):**
- The CCE, CPE and TCC stakes, carried at about $1.8B. Their earnings are in equity income.
- Net debt of about $581M, or $1.56 a share. Its cost is in interest expense.

The equity stakes are probably worth somewhat more than they contribute to earnings. At end-1987 their market value was $44M above carrying value. They add a small cushion, not a major one.

## 5.4 Margin of safety against the hurdle

**The framework's hurdles:**
- **Explicit Section 5 hurdle: price below NWC. Failed by about 49 times.** Under the literal rule, the answer is no.
- **Partnership-era standard for "generals": a "substantial excess of value" over price creates "a comfortable margin of safety" (`bpl-letter-1961#3`, `bpl-letter-1962#4`).** Measured against owner-earnings value:

| Case | Intrinsic value | Price / value | Margin of safety |
|---|---|---|---|
| 9%, growth 10% (base) | $90 | 52% | **~48%** |
| 9%, growth 8% (conservative) | $77 | 60% | **~40%** |
| 12%, growth 10% (stress) | $50 | 93% | ~7% |
| 12%, growth 8% (severe) | $43 | 108% | none (overvalued ~8%) |

**Reading the table.**
- At a bond-rate opportunity cost, the price is about 50–60% of value, even when growth is assumed to slow below the record. That is a substantial margin by the framework's partnership-era standard.
- The margin disappears only if both conditions hold: Berkshire's opportunity cost is 12% *and* growth falls to about 8%.
- Stage 2 found a moat that is widening and a long runway outside the U.S. That makes a growth collapse the less likely risk. The real swing factor is the discount rate. A better-informed analyst on the as-of date would check it against actual bond yields, which the packet does not provide.

**Where the margin of safety comes from.** For this company the cushion is less in the price than in the predictability of the business. A franchise earning about 25% unlevered, with 44% world share and low capital needs, makes the "future develops along the lines of its past" assumption unusually reliable. The 1987 letter makes this trade-off explicitly. A low price on a sow's ear failed; a sensible price on silk works (`brk-letter-1987#18`).

**Not "toad-like" pricing.** The 1981 letter prizes "fractional interests in easily-identifiable princes at toad-like prices" (`brk-letter-1981#3`). $46.56 is not such a price. It is about 20 times 1987 owner earnings, near the 52-week high of $49.12, and 33% above the 1988 low of $35.04. The October 1987 low of $28.96 would have been close to toad-like, at about 12.6 times owner earnings. Today's price is *sensible*, not cheap.

## 6. Catalyst / timing

- **No catalyst needed** (`brk-letter-1987`): "The market may ignore business success for a while, but eventually will confirm it." The 1987–88 record supports this. The stock went sideways, roughly $36–44, for about 15 months while earnings rose. The multiple fell from about 21 times (August 1987, at $50.56) to about 15–16 times (mid-1988). It is now catching up. A long-term owner does not need anything else to happen.
- **Work-outs** (`bpl-letter-1957`): **not applicable.** No sale, merger, tender or liquidation has been announced. The bottler share sales and the CPE spin-off are completed events, not pending ones.
- **Controls** (`bpl-letter-1963`): **not applicable.** At $17.3B of market value, Berkshire cannot buy influence the way the partnership did. Influence would come through a relationship with management, which Stage 3 noted cannot be judged from the packet.
- **"Mr. Market is there to serve you, not to guide you"** (`brk-letter-1987`). The flat period in 1988 offered better prices than today. That is history, though, and the question now is whether *today's* price is sensible.

**Related temperament rules (Section 9):**
- *"Be fearful when others are greedy"* (`brk-letter-1986`). The stock is up 30% from its lows and near its highs, so the crowd is not fearful. The rule argues for discipline on price: build the position patiently and don't chase. It does not argue for staying out of a wonderful business.
- *No market forecasting* (`bpl-letter-1966-mid`, `fortune-1977-inflation`). The decision rests on value against price, not on where the market goes next.

## What the packet lacks for valuation

1. **1988 earnings, owner earnings and share count.** This is the largest gap. The valuation is about 14 months stale. If 1988 grew at the record rate, every value above rises by about 10%.
2. **Long-term interest rates on the as-of date.** These are the discount rate and opportunity cost that decide whether the margin of safety is about 45% or about 7%.
3. **A split of capex into maintenance and growth,** to narrow owner earnings within $2.05–2.46.
4. **Local-currency earnings,** to judge how much of the 1985–87 jump could reverse if the dollar strengthens.
5. **Current market values of the CCE and CPE stakes.**

## Stage 4 conclusion

- **Under the framework's literal valuation rule (buy below NWC), Company A fails**, by a factor of about 49.
- **Under the method the investor's 1981–88 writings actually apply to outstanding businesses** (owner earnings, past record assumed to continue, opportunity cost near the bond rate), intrinsic value is about **$77–90 a share against a $46.56 price**. That is a **margin of safety of roughly 40–48%**, and it holds even with growth assumed below the decade's record. Only at management's own 12% hurdle rate, combined with slower growth, does the margin shrink to nothing.
- **No catalyst is required,** and work-outs and controls do not apply.
- The price is **sensible, not a bargain,** which fits "an outstanding business at a sensible price." Sizing should reflect that the main uncertainty is the opportunity-cost rate and the missing 1988 figures, not the quality of the business.
