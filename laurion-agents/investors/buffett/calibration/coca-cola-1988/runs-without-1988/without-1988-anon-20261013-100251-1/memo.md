# Company A (AAA): Recommendation to Buy, February 27, 1989

Company A sells soft-drink syrup and concentrate under the most widely recognized trademark in the world. Independent and partly owned bottlers turn that concentrate into finished drinks in more than 155 countries. The company's brands hold about 44 percent of world soft-drink volume, twice the share of the nearest competitor. In the United States it leads its main rival by nine share points overall, and by more than two to one in fountain sales. After moving its film business into a 49-percent-owned affiliate in 1987, the company gets more than 95 percent of its operating income from soft drinks. The foods division is a smaller, weaker business built mostly on orange juice. It lost ground in 1987 and does not affect the case for or against the company.

We judge a business first by what it earns on the capital it employs, not by how fast its per-share earnings grow. On that test Company A is exceptional. From 1976 to 1981, debt was under ten percent of capital, and return on equity was still around 20 percent every year. That shows the high returns come from the business, not from leverage. In 1987 return on equity was 27 percent. Net of excess cash, debt was about 15 percent of capital, and the after-tax return on all capital, debt included, was close to 25 percent. The soft-drink segments earned roughly 34 percent pre-tax on their identifiable assets, and the international operations earned over 50 percent. U.S. concentrate carries a gross margin of about 63 percent.

The reason such returns persist is the moat around the business. Consumers ask for the product by name. When the company changed its flagship formula in 1985, it had to bring the original back, and that product became the best-selling soft drink in the country. The bottler and fountain network is the largest in the world, and the company is consolidating it into fewer, lower-cost operators. New contracts give the company flexibility to raise prices to its largest bottler, and management says it can pass rising costs through. There is also a long growth runway, because international consumption per person is still about a sixth of the U.S. level.

The business also needs little capital. In 1987 the soft-drink segments spent $170 million on capital expenditures against $1.43 billion of operating income. Total company capex has fallen for two years while earnings rose. Owner earnings, meaning reported earnings plus depreciation minus the spending needed to keep the business competitive, come to about $2.30 a share. That is roughly equal to reported earnings, so the earnings are close to fully available to owners.

Management passes, with reservations. The team in place since 1981 sold low-return businesses and refocused on concentrate. Between 1984 and 1987 it bought back about 50 million shares, roughly 12 percent of the company, at an average near $28, which is well below what the business was worth. Measured per share, each dollar of earnings retained from 1982 through 1987 added more than six dollars of market value, and about $2.30 even if the stock's valuation multiple had not risen. Against that, the move into entertainment earned less than management's own 12 percent hurdle rate. In 1985 the company issued about seven million shares at roughly 14 times earnings to buy a television producer, in the same year it was buying its own shares back at a similar price. Management has also raised its ceiling on net debt to 35 percent of capital. Its reports carry more promotional language than we would like. None of this disqualifies the company, but all of it bears watching.

On value, the old test of buying below net working capital does not apply here. The company has essentially no net working capital, and the stock has never been a balance-sheet bargain. That test was built for mediocre businesses bought cheaply. Our experience with textiles and similar bargains taught us that a fair price for an outstanding business is the better trade. We therefore value the owner earnings directly, assuming the future resembles the past rather than improves on it. Earnings per share compounded at about 11 percent a year from 1977 to 1987. If owner earnings grow 8 to 10 percent a year for a decade and 5 percent after that, and are discounted at about 9 percent (our assumption for the long government bond yield, which the packet does not give), the shares are worth roughly $77 to $90. At today's price of $46.56, that is a margin of safety of about 40 to 48 percent. The margin depends heavily on the discount rate. At management's 12 percent cost of capital combined with 8 percent growth, the stock is about fully priced. The price is sensible but not cheap: it is close to its 52-week high, and the late-1987 lows were far better entry points.

We recommend building a position of about a quarter of the portfolio over time, buying at or below current prices and adding on weakness. The facts about the business are unusually clear, and the risk of a drastic change in its value is unusually low, which supports a large position. It stays below our maximum concentration because we do not have the 1988 results, the opportunity-cost rate is uncertain, and management's appetite for debt is a concern. No catalyst is needed. Through 1988 the stock moved sideways while the business compounded, and over time the market tends to recognize results like these.

We would reconsider under any of the following conditions:

- The 1988 results, or later years, show local-currency volume or operating income stalling.
- Concentrate margins or market share erode on a sustained basis.
- Long-term interest rates rise well above 10 percent, which would remove the margin of safety.
- Management uses its new borrowing capacity for diversifying acquisitions, or issues stock below its value.
- A conversation with management shows that buybacks are driven by available borrowing capacity rather than by price relative to value.

If none of these occurs, we intend to hold the shares indefinitely and not sell merely because the price has risen.
