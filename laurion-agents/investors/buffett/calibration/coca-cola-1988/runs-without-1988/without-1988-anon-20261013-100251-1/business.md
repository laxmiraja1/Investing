# Stage 2: Business analysis (framework Section 3)

The section names three measures, applied in order: (1) return on equity, not EPS growth; (2) a durable competitive advantage (the moat); (3) owner earnings.

---

## 3.1 Return on equity is the primary test

The framework's test is "a high earnings rate on equity capital employed (without undue leverage, accounting gimmickry, etc.)" (`brk-letter-1979`). Each of the three qualifiers needs checking.

**Reported ROE (income from continuing operations to average equity):**

| 1976 | 1978 | 1980 | 1982 | 1984 | 1985 | 1986 | 1987 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 20.3% | 21.2% | 19.7% | 19.6% | 21.8% | 23.5% | 28.8% | 27.2% |

Sources: ar-1986 and ar-1987 selected data.

**Gimmickry check: strip out the one-time items.**
- **1986:** the $375M CCE stock-sale gain added $262M after tax. The $180M of provisions cost $128M after tax (ar-1986 MD&A). Adjusted income is about $800M, roughly $2.07 per share, and adjusted ROE is about 24.6%.
- **1987:** the $40M TCC gain and the $36M foods provision roughly offset each other. Reported 27.2% is close to the underlying rate.

**Leverage check.** This is the important one. From 1976 to 1981, debt was only 3.5–9% of capital, and ROE was still 20–21%. So the business earned about 20% on equity with essentially no leverage. Since then ROE has risen to 27%, and two financial levers explain part of that rise:
- Buybacks put $1.31B into treasury stock by the end of 1987, which shrinks the equity base.
- Gross debt rose to 45.6% of capital.

To remove the financing effect, I compute an unlevered return:
- 1987 operating income plus equity income is about $1,442M pre-tax. At the 35% effective rate, that is about $937M after tax.
- Capital is equity of $3,224M plus net debt of about $581M. Net debt is total debt of $2,702M less excess cash of $1,268M, the CPE notes of $545M (paid in January 1988), and temporary investments of $308M. Total capital is about $3,805M.
- After-tax return on total capital is therefore **about 24.6%**.

The high return comes from the business itself, not from the balance sheet.

**The core business on its own capital (1987 segment data, pre-tax):**

| | Operating income | Identifiable assets | Return |
|---|---|---|---|
| Soft drinks, international | $1,108.9M | $2,126.7M | ~52% |
| Soft drinks, USA | $323.6M | $2,047.4M | ~16% |
| Soft drinks, total | $1,432.5M | $4,174.1M | ~34% |
| Foods | $66.6M ($103M before provision) | $627.3M | ~11–16% |

The U.S. asset base rose from $1,425M to $2,047M in one year. I suspect bottler holdings awaiting resale, such as Miami CCBC, inflate it, but the packet does not break this out. The U.S. return is probably understated.

**Benchmark.** The 1987 letter notes that only six of Fortune's 1,000 largest companies averaged over 30% ROE across a decade (`brk-letter-1987#1`). This company sits just below that group. It earns about 27% on equity and about 34% pre-tax on the soft-drink assets, at a scale of $916M of net income.

**EPS growth is a secondary measure and is flattered.** EPS rose from $1.72 to $2.43 between 1985 and 1987. Part of that came from share count falling from 393M to 377M average shares, and part from a weak dollar (see 3.2). The framework says to judge on the return rate, which holds up.

**Verdict on 3.1:** this passes. The business earned about 20% on equity unlevered for a decade, and earns about 25% unlevered today.

## 3.2 Durable competitive advantage (the moat)

The framework's model is GEICO. Its cost advantage over competitors "is a kind of moat that protects a valuable and much-sought-after business castle," and good management keeps widening it (`brk-letter-1986#16`). For this company the moat is the brand plus the distribution system, not costs alone.

**Evidence the moat is wide:**
- **Share.** Company brands are "more than 44 percent of all soft drinks sold worldwide," with volume "double that of our nearest competitor" (ar-1987).
  - U.S. share is over 39%, 9 points ahead of the main competitor.
  - U.S. fountain share is 59%, more than 2-to-1 over the nearest rival, and the company took about three-quarters of fountain growth over four years.
  - U.S. vending share is 49%.
- **The trademark is owned by consumers, not the company.** In 1985 the company changed the taste of its flagship. It reintroduced the original formula as Brand A classic, and that product became "the best-selling U.S. soft drink" by 1986 (ar-1986). A brand that can survive its owner's own mistake is a strong asset.
- **Economics of selling concentrate.**
  - Gross margin on U.S. concentrate was about 63% in 1986.
  - Soft-drink capex in 1987 was $170M against $1,432M of pre-tax soft-drink operating income, about 12%.
  - Management describes the core as "high margins, strong cash flow, low capital requirements."
  - This matches what the 1987 letter values: businesses that "can grow while concurrently making almost all of their earnings available for deployment" (`brk-letter-1987#1`).
- **Pricing power.**
  - In October 1986 the company used new flexible-pricing contracts to raise concentrate prices to CCE.
  - Management states it "is able to adjust prices to compensate for increasing costs."
  - The 1986 letter says the strength of the franchise decides whether a company keeps the benefit of lower corporate taxes or passes it to customers (`brk-letter-1986#22`). The U.S. statutory rate fell from 46% to 40% in 1987, and the effective rate was 35%. The benefit appears to have stayed with the company: margins rose.
  - The 1977 inflation article says the winners under inflation are businesses that need little added capital per dollar of sales (`fortune-1977-inflation#6`). Concentrate fits that description.
- **Long growth runway.** International per-capita consumption was 16% of the U.S. rate in 1986. Even the U.S. rate is still rising, from 203 servings in 1980 to 274 in 1987, with a target of 315 by 1990. International markets supply 65% of volume and about 77% of soft-drink operating income.
- **The moat is being widened.** Management is consolidating and recapitalizing bottlers so they become "low-cost producers in their markets": 102 German bottlers became 68, plus CCE, TCC, and the British joint venture. This mirrors the GEICO description of a manager who "continually widens the moat."

**Contrast case.** Burlington spent $3B on a commodity business and left shareholders with a third of their 1964 purchasing power (`brk-letter-1985#9`). This company's core needs little capital, and its customers pick the product by name.

**Weaker parts and risks:**
- **Foods is not a moat business.** It is exposed to orange-solids prices, ranks second in chilled juice, and its operating income fell from $120M to $67M. It is under 5% of operating income.
- **Currency inflates recent growth.** Key foreign currencies rose 27% in 1986 and 16% in 1987 against the dollar. International operating income went from $613M in 1985 to $1,109M in 1987. With 1987 volume up 9% and international operating income up 25% excluding unusual items, roughly half of the reported jump came from translation. The underlying local-currency growth I estimate at about 8–12% a year, which is still very good. Currency gains can reverse.
- **Bottler economics are thin and leveraged.** CCE earned $88M on $3.3B of revenue and carries $2.7B of liabilities against $1.5B of net assets. The concentrate company's high returns partly reflect pushing the capital-heavy bottling layer into 49%-owned affiliates. Those stakes, about $2.26B of carrying value, already sit inside the equity base used in 3.1, so the ROE figure is honest. Still, the health of the bottler system is part of the moat.
- **The main competitor spends heavily,** and Latin American markets are "high volume, low margin."

**On change.** The 1987 letter says "Severe change and exceptional returns usually don't mix" (`brk-letter-1987#3`). The product is a hundred years old and the economics are unchanged. The changes here are in corporate structure, not in the product.

**Verdict on 3.2:** this passes strongly. It is the widest moat seen in the framework's examples, and its future economics are readable 10 or more years out. The 1987 letter cautions that "Charlie and I may misread the fundamental economics of a business" (`brk-letter-1987#18`), but here the economics are simple.

## 3.3 Owner earnings

The definition is reported earnings, plus depreciation, depletion, amortization and similar non-cash charges, less the average capex needed to maintain competitive position and unit volume (`brk-letter-1986`).

**1987 calculation ($M):**

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Income from continuing operations | 916.1 |
| + Depreciation | 151.9 |
| + Goodwill amortization | 1.6 |
| + Non-cash restructuring provision (foods) | 36.4 |
| − Non-cash gain on TCC stock sale | (39.7) |
| **Subtotal before capex** | **1,066.3** |
| − Capex: if all $300M is treated as maintenance (floor) | (299.6) → **766.7** |
| − Capex: if maintenance ≈ depreciation (about $152M) | → **914.4** |

Per share on 372M shares, that is **$2.06 to $2.46**. My central estimate is **about $2.30**.

Supporting points:
- Soft-drink capex of $170M ran about 1.7 times soft-drink depreciation of $103M, which means much of it funded growth (coolers, vending machines, fountain equipment, production lines) rather than maintenance. That argues for the upper half of the range.
- Working capital released $273M in 1987 rather than absorbing cash, so no deduction is needed for it.
- **Equity earnings caveat.** About $80M of 1987 equity income was not received in cash. If an owner valued the affiliates' retained earnings at zero, the range drops to $1.85–2.24 per share. The framework's look-through approach credits retained earnings that create value, so I keep them but treat them as lower quality than concentrate earnings.

**Cross-check over three years (restated):**

| Year | Cash from operations | Capex | Depreciation | Underlying net income |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1985 | $694M | $412M | $130M | $678M |
| 1986 | $741M | $346M | $151M | ~$800M |
| 1987 | $1,231M | $300M | $152M | $916M |

Capex is falling while earnings rise, which is consistent with a business whose owner earnings are close to, or above, its reported earnings.

**Verdict on 3.3:** owner earnings are about equal to reported earnings, roughly $850M a year or about $2.30 per share for 1987, with a defensible range of $2.05–2.45. This figure is the input to valuation in Section 5.

## Facts the framework wants that the packet lacks

1. **1988 results.** This is the largest gap. By 1989-02-27 the 1988 results would be known. I am working from 1987 figures that are more than a year old. The 1988 dividend was $1.20 and the stock rose from $38 to $46, but earnings are unknown.
2. **A split of capex into maintenance and growth,** company-wide and in the soft-drink segments. This is needed to narrow the owner-earnings range.
3. **Local-currency operating income by region,** to separate real growth from currency translation.
4. **Bottler franchise terms and capital needs abroad:** how long the contracts run, whether pricing is formula-based or flexible outside CCE, and how much capital the company must keep putting into bottler stakes to sustain volume.
5. **Competitor economics:** the main rival's margins and international share, to test how wide the gap is outside the U.S.
6. **What sits in the $2,047M of U.S. identifiable assets,** to confirm the true return on U.S. soft-drink capital.
7. **The value of the equity stakes at market:** CCE, CPE and TCC together are about $1.8B of carrying value, and are noted only as exceeding carrying value by $44M at the end of 1987.

**Stage 2 conclusion:** the business clears every Section 3 test with room to spare. It earns about 25% after tax on total capital without depending on leverage, has a widening brand-and-distribution moat with a long international runway, and its owner earnings are close to reported earnings at about $2.30 per share. The open questions are management's use of debt and acquisitions (Section 4) and whether $46.56, about 20 times owner earnings, is a sensible price (Section 5).
