# Stage 2: Business analysis

I take the three rules in the order the framework lists them: return on equity, the moat, then owner earnings.

## 3a. Return on equity, not EPS growth (`brk-letter-1979`)

The rule asks for a high return on equity "without undue leverage, accounting gimmickry, etc." So I look at the return first, then strip out leverage and one-time items.

**Reported return on average equity** (from the selected-data tables in ar-1986 and ar-1987):

| Period | ROE | Total debt / capital |
|---|---|---|
| 1977–1981 | 20.6%, 21.2%, 21.1%, 19.7%, 19.9% | 3.5% – 9.3% |
| 1982–1984 | 19.6%, 19.4%, 21.8% | 15.1% – 30.7% |
| 1985–1987 | 23.5%, 28.8%, 27.2% | 27.7% – 45.6% (gross); 15.3% net at end-1987 |

**Leverage test.** In 1977–81, with almost no debt, ROE was about 20%. That is the business's own earning power. Since then debt has risen and ROE has risen with it. Part of the move from 20% to 27–29% therefore comes from leverage and a shrinking equity base, not better economics. Two things shrank equity in 1987:
- $605M of buybacks, taking treasury stock from $705M to $1,309M.
- A $335M in-kind dividend of shares in the entertainment business, Columbia Pictures Entertainment (CPE).

Equity fell from $3,515M to $3,224M while net income was roughly flat. I treat about 20% unlevered as the reliable floor and the late-1980s figures as partly financial.

**One-time items.**
- 1986: a $375M pretax gain from Coca-Cola Enterprises (CCE), the 49%-owned US bottler, selling stock, offset by $180M of provisions. Net effect about +$0.35 per share.
- 1987: a $40M gain from T.C.C. Beverages (TCC), the 49%-owned Canadian bottler, offset by a $36M Foods provision. These roughly cancel.

Underlying, management reports net income per share up 17.4% in 1987.

**The core business on its own capital.** Soft-drink segment results from ar-1987 note 16, pretax:

| Year | Soft-drink operating income | Identifiable assets | Return |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1987 | $1,432.5M (USA $323.6M + Int'l $1,108.9M) | $4,174M | about 34% |
| 1986, before $180M provisions | about $1,182M | $3,287M | about 36% |

This is measured on total assets, not equity. The return on the equity actually needed to run the syrup business is higher still.

**Benchmark.** The 1988 Fortune data show only six of the 1,000 largest companies averaged above 30% ROE over the previous decade (`brk-letter-1987#1`). Coca-Cola's soft-drink core earns at about that level before tax, and the whole company has earned about 20% after tax without leverage.

**EPS growth, for the record.** EPS rose from $0.84 in 1977 to $2.43 in 1987, about 11% compounded, with no down year. The framework treats this as secondary to the return figures.

**Drags on the consolidated return:**
- **Foods.** 1987 operating income was $66.6M on $627M of assets, after the $36M provision. Even before the provision that is $103M, about 16% pretax. The business depends on commodity orange-solids prices and is second in chilled juice.
- **Equity investees.** CCE earned $88.4M on $1,526M of net assets in 1987, about 5.8%. Coca-Cola's share was $43.3M. The bottling stakes and CPE ($989M carrying value) earn far less than the concentrate business. The 1987 cash-flow statement shows $79.7M of equity income retained in investees rather than received as dividends. Those retained dollars probably do not each create a dollar of value. I come back to this in the management stage.

## 3b. Durable competitive advantage (`brk-letter-1986#16`)

GEICO's moat was a cost gap. Coca-Cola's is mainly a trademark-and-distribution franchise, with a claimed cost advantage underneath it. Evidence from the packet:

**Share and scale**
- 44% of world soft-drink volume, excluding the USSR and China. 1987 volume was twice the nearest competitor's.
- US share above 39% in 1986, 9 points ahead of the main competitor.
- US fountain share 59%, more than 2-to-1. The company took about three-fourths of fountain growth over four years.
- US vending share 49%.
- Coca-Cola classic is the top US soft drink, and diet Coke is third.

**Margins**
- Average gross margin on concentrate sold to US bottlers was about 63% (ar-1986).
- Company gross margin was 52.6% in 1987.
- Capital needs are small: soft-drink capex was $170M against $1.43B of segment operating income.

**Pricing power**
- New contracts with CCE allow flexible pricing, which the company used in October 1986.
- Contracts with international bottlers "generally do not contain restrictions."
- Management "believes that the Company is able to adjust prices to compensate for increasing costs."

One limit: standard contracts with most *other* US bottlers set Coca-Cola syrup prices by formula. Domestic pricing power is therefore partly bound by contract. This matters under the inflation analysis (`fortune-1977-inflation#6`). It also matters under the tax pass-through test (`brk-letter-1986#22`), which says a strong, unregulated franchise keeps the benefit of a tax cut.

**Durability under stress.** In 1985 management changed the product itself and had to bring back the original formula as "classic." By 1986 classic was the best-selling US soft drink, and total sugar-cola volume grew 5% against 1% for competitors. Consumers stayed with the trademark through a major management error. That is strong evidence the moat belongs to the brand, not the managers. It also fits the observation that "severe change and exceptional returns usually don't mix" (`brk-letter-1987#3`). The product that earns the returns is the one that did not change.

**Room to grow inside the moat**
- International per-capita consumption was about 16% of the US rate in 1986.
- US per-capita consumption of Company drinks rose from 203 servings in 1980 to 274 in 1987.
- Japan grew from almost nothing to the top profit contributor in thirty years.
- About 65% of volume and 77% of soft-drink operating income are already outside the US.

**Moat risks visible in the packet**
- The US is a two-company advertising contest. US soft-drink operating margin was only 15% in 1987 ($323.6M on $2,120M), against 27% internationally.
- Recent growth is partly a currency effect. Key foreign currencies strengthened 27% in 1986 and 16% in 1987 against the dollar. International operating income rose 25% in 1987 excluding unusual items, so local-currency growth was well below the reported figure.
- The "low-cost producer" claim is management's own assertion, not something the packet shows.

**Verdict.** The concentrate business has a wide and durable moat. Foods has none.

## 3c. Owner earnings (`brk-letter-1986`)

Owner earnings = (a) reported earnings + (b) depreciation, amortization and similar non-cash charges − (c) average capital spending needed to maintain the business.

| 1987, $M | Low case | High case |
|---|---|---|
| Net income | 916.1 | 916.1 |
| + Depreciation | 151.9 | 151.9 |
| + Goodwill amortization | 1.6 | 1.6 |
| − Capital expenditures | (299.6): all capex treated as maintenance | (151.9): maintenance capex set equal to depreciation |
| **Owner earnings** | **≈770** | **≈918** |
| Per share (372M shares) | ≈$2.07 | ≈$2.47 |

**Adjustments and judgments behind the table:**
- The TCC gain (+$40M pretax) and the Foods provision (−$36M) roughly offset, so I leave them out.
- Capex over three years is falling: $412M, $346M, $300M (restated), against depreciation of about $144M a year. Much of the spending is expansion, such as international bottling and fountain equipment. True maintenance spending is probably nearer the high case.
- The deferred tax reversal (−$28.7M) and the $272.7M working-capital release in 1987 look like timing items. I exclude them rather than count them as recurring cash.
- Equity income ($118.5M) is included in reported earnings, but only about $39M came in as cash dividends. If those retained investee earnings are worth less than face, as CCE's 5.8% return suggests, owner earnings sit toward the lower half of the range.

**Working estimate.** About $2.10–2.40 per share, near $2.25, with the soft-drink core producing almost all of it.

## Facts the framework would want that the packet lacks

1. **1988 results.** There are no 1988 interim or annual figures. The latest owner-earnings base is fourteen months old.
2. **Split of capex** into maintenance and growth spending, by segment.
3. **Local-currency results.** Profit growth by region with the currency effect removed.
4. **Unit pricing history.** Concentrate price per unit over time, and how much US volume is under formula-priced contracts versus flexible ones.
5. **Ten-year return figures for the soft-drink segment alone.** The packet gives only three years of segment data.
6. **Competitor economics.** The packet gives US share against the main rival but little on its international position or profitability.
7. **Bottler health beyond CCE and TCC.** The health of independent bottlers determines how much concentrate the system can push through.
8. **Investee market values.** Market value versus carrying value for CPE and the other investees. Only a combined $44M excess over carrying value is given for end-1987.
9. **Tax on foreign earnings.** Tax that would fall due if the $280M of unremitted foreign earnings were brought home, and the effective benefit to Coca-Cola from the US rate falling to 34%.

## Stage conclusion

The soft-drink concentrate business passes every test in this section:
- It earned about 20% on equity with almost no debt in 1977–81, and earns about 35% pretax on its own assets.
- It has a trademark moat that survived a self-inflicted product failure.
- It needs little capital to grow.

The drags are Foods, the low-return bottling and entertainment stakes, the recent rise in leverage, and growth that is partly currency translation. Owner earnings are roughly $2.10–2.40 per share. That is the figure to carry into the valuation stage against the $46.56 price, about 19–22 times owner earnings.
