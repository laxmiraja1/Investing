# Memo: Purchase of The Coca-Cola Company (KO), as of February 27, 1989

We recommend buying Coca-Cola common stock at the current price of about $46.56. We aim to build the position over time to roughly a quarter of the portfolio. This memo explains what the business is, why it passes our tests, what we think it is worth, and what would change our view. It relies only on the company's 1986 and 1987 annual reports and recent prices. No 1988 results were available to us.

Coca-Cola is, at its core, a seller of syrup and concentrate. It sells to bottlers around the world and to fountain customers such as restaurant chains. In 1987 soft drinks produced more than 95% of operating income.

Coca-Cola is not purely a syrup company. It owns 49% of its largest US bottler, Coca-Cola Enterprises, and 49% of its Canadian bottler. It holds smaller stakes in other bottlers, and 49% of Columbia Pictures Entertainment, the former entertainment division. It also runs a juice business under the Minute Maid name. These businesses earn far less than the concentrate operation and are the weakest parts of the company. The investment case rests on the concentrate business.

The business passes our screens. It is not a turnaround: earnings per share from continuing operations rose every year from 1977 to 1987, from $0.84 to $2.43. Its high returns do not depend on borrowing. Between 1977 and 1981, with debt at under 10% of capital, return on equity ran steadily at 19% to 21%. The soft-drink segment alone earned about 34% pretax in 1987 on the assets it uses.

It needs little capital. Soft-drink capital spending in 1987 was $170 million, against $1.4 billion of segment operating income. And it is simple to understand: a branded product sold through an established system, with no technology risk.

What protects those returns is the trademark and the distribution system behind it. The company sells about 44% of the world's soft drinks outside the Soviet Union and China, twice its nearest competitor. In the United States it holds 59% of fountain sales, more than double the next rival.

The best evidence of durability comes from management's own mistake. In 1985 the company changed the formula of its flagship product. Consumers rejected the change and the original was restored as Coca-Cola classic. By 1986 classic was the best-selling soft drink in the country. A franchise that survives its owners tampering with the product is unusually sturdy. There is also room to grow: consumption per person outside the United States is about one-sixth of the US level.

Management under Roberto Goizueta and Donald Keough has been in place since 1981. On the whole they have used shareholders' money well. They have pulled back from diversification: plastics and pasta were sold, entertainment was spun into a minority-owned company, and soft drinks now dominate the business. They have repurchased large amounts of stock, including about 33 million shares in 1984 and 1985 at $18 to $25, well below today's price. They are reducing the dividend payout so more earnings stay in the business.

Retained earnings of roughly $2 billion over 1982 to 1987 were accompanied by an increase in market value of about $13 billion. That comfortably exceeds our requirement that each retained dollar produce at least a dollar of value.

We note three reservations about management. The company issued stock in 1985 to buy an entertainment business that earned modest returns. Its reports lean toward promotion. And it has deliberately raised its borrowing limit, partly to finance buybacks.

On value, the company fails the strict test of trading below net working capital; that figure is close to zero. We do not think that test fits a business whose suppliers and bottlers effectively finance its working capital. We valued it instead on owner earnings: reported profit plus depreciation, less the capital spending needed to keep the business competitive. For 1987 we put this at about $2.07 to $2.47 per share, centered near $2.25.

If those earnings grow 8% to 10% a year for a decade, broadly in line with the past, and money is worth about 10%, the shares are worth roughly $60 to $70. At $46.56 that is a discount of 22% to 33%.

The margin is not large and it is not unconditional. At 8% growth and a 12% required return, value falls to about $42 to $49, and the discount disappears. The stock is also near its 52-week high and traded at $35 to $40 for most of 1988. These are the reasons we stop well short of a maximum position.

Some of the recent growth came from a weaker dollar and from more debt, and should not be extrapolated. The missing 1988 results are the largest gap in our information.

We intend to hold the shares indefinitely, as we do our other permanent holdings, and would not sell because the price rises. We would reconsider for any of the following reasons:
- A lasting loss of market share or pricing power.
- Return on equity falling toward ordinary levels without the help of debt.
- A large acquisition outside soft drinks, especially one paid for with stock.
- Repurchases made at prices above intrinsic value.
- Accounting that obscures weakening core results.

A drop back toward the 1988 price range would lead us to add. A price near $65 to $70 would leave no margin for further purchases.
