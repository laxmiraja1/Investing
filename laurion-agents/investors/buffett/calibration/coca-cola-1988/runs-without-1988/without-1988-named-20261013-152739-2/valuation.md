# Stage 4: Valuation

## 5. The framework's stated valuation rule: buy below net working capital (`bpl-letter-1965`)

The only valuation rule listed in the framework is the Partnership-era bargain test. Applied to the end-1987 balance sheet (ar-1987):

| $M | End-1987 |
|---|---|
| Current assets | 4,136.2 |
| Current liabilities | (4,118.5) |
| **Net working capital** | **17.7**, about **$0.05 per share** |
| Current assets less *all* liabilities (5,131.8) | **–995.6**, about –$2.67 per share |

Against a price of **$46.56**, Coca-Cola fails this test completely. Net working capital is close to zero, and nothing is left after all liabilities. **On the letter of the framework's valuation rule, this is not a bargain and there is no margin of safety.**

That result says little about this business. Working capital near zero partly reflects a franchise whose suppliers and bottlers effectively finance it: payables and accruals were $1.43B. The as-of-date writings also say the net-working-capital method is no longer the test applied to a business like this:
- "Our goal is to find an outstanding business at a sensible price, not a mediocre business at a bargain price … I searched for 'bargains' – and had the misfortune to find some" (`brk-letter-1987#18`).
- "We may very well pay a fairly fancy price for a Category 1 business if we are reasonably confident of what we are getting" (`brk-letter-1981#3`).
- The standard is a price "that will make sense, even should the future of the acquired enterprise develop much along the lines of its past" (`brk-letter-1981#3`).

So I report the net-working-capital failure as the literal result. I then apply the test those later writings set out: value the owner earnings from Stage 2 on the assumption that the future resembles the past. This is not a generic multi-stage forecast. It is the owner-earnings figure, grown at the historical rate, compared with what the money could earn elsewhere.

## The "future like its past" test on owner earnings

**Inputs, all from the packet unless marked as an assumption:**
- **Owner earnings:** $2.25 per share (Stage 2 range $2.07–2.47, based on 1987).
- **Past per-share growth:** continuing EPS rose from $1.17 in 1981 to $2.43 in 1987, about 12.9% a year, and from 1977 to 1987 about 11.2% a year. Part of the recent rate came from buybacks, more debt and a weaker dollar. I therefore use **10% as the central case and 8% as the conservative case** for the next ten years.
- **Growth after year 10:** an assumed 5% a year. The packet supports a long runway: international consumption per head is 16% of the US level.
- **Opportunity cost:** the packet has no Treasury yield. The nearest market rates in the packet are Coca-Cola's own notes, with coupons of 9⅞%–12½% and a weighted average of about 10% on other debt, and management's 12% after-tax cost of capital. I use **10% as the central rate and 12% as the stress case.**

**Value per share** (present value of years 1–10, plus the value at year 10 capitalized at r – 5% and discounted back):

| Case | Growth, yrs 1–10 | Rate | PV yrs 1–10 | PV of year-10 value | **Value/share** | Price as % of value |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Past repeats fully | 12% | 10% | 24.9 | 56.6 | **≈$81** | 57% |
| Central | 10% | 10% | 22.5 | 47.3 | **≈$70** | 67% |
| Slower growth | 8% | 10% | 20.4 | 39.3 | **≈$60** | 78% |
| Management's hurdle rate | 10% | 12% | 20.4 | 28.2 | **≈$49** | 96% |
| Conservative on both | 8% | 12% | 18.5 | 23.5 | **≈$42** | 111% |

The value after year 10 is 55–70% of each figure, so the result depends heavily on how long the moat lasts. Stage 2 judged the moat durable, and the 1985 formula episode is the strongest evidence for that, which is why I accept the long tail. The estimate is still a range, not a single number.

**Check without a terminal value.** At $46.56 the owner-earnings yield is about 4.8% (4.4–5.3% across the Stage 2 range), well below the roughly 10% Coca-Cola pays on its own debt. Growing at 10%, owner earnings reach 10% of today's price ($4.66) in about **year 8**; at 8%, in about **year 9–10**. Over ten years the business would earn $35–39 per share, 75–85% of today's price, and would still own a franchise earning $4.90–5.80 a share. On these figures the price looks sensible. It is not cheap: this is not a "prince at a toad-like price."

**Items not added:**
- Excess cash ($1.27B at end-1987) roughly offsets debt; net debt was 15.3% of capital.
- Bottling and CPE equity income is already inside owner earnings. The combined market value of the publicly traded investees was only $44M above their carrying value at end-1987.
- No 1988 figures are available. If 1988 owner earnings grew about 10–15%, every value above rises by roughly the same percentage. A central value nearer $75–80 is plausible but **not shown in the packet.**

## Margin of safety against the hurdle

The framework sets no numerical hurdle. The Partnership letters require a "substantial excess of value" (`bpl-letter-1962#4`, `bpl-letter-1961#3`). The 1981 and 1987 letters accept a "fairly fancy price" when the business is outstanding and the investor is "reasonably confident." For this kind of business, the safety is meant to come largely from the durability and predictability of the earnings, not only from a price discount.

| Measure | Margin of safety |
|---|---|
| Net working capital (the literal rule) | **None.** Price is about 900x net working capital per share. |
| Central owner-earnings value (~$70) | **≈33%** below value |
| Slower-growth case (~$60) | **≈22%** |
| Stress cases at a 12% rate ($42–49) | **About –11% to +5%**: none |

On the central and slower-growth cases, the price clears a "substantial" margin. On the stress cases it does not. The verdict rests on confidence in 8–10% per-share growth sustained for a decade. Stage 2 found that supported by:
- about 20% unlevered returns on equity since 1977,
- a moat that survived a self-inflicted product failure, and
- international consumption per head at 16% of the US level.

## 6. Catalyst / timing

- **No catalyst needed** (`brk-letter-1987`): "The market may ignore business success for a while, but eventually will confirm it." The case is a long-term holding of a growing franchise, so no event is required.
- **Work-out** (`bpl-letter-1957`): **not applicable.** There is no announced merger, liquidation or tender. The ongoing 40M-share buyback helps per-share value but is not a work-out.
- **Control** (`bpl-letter-1963`): **not applicable.** At about $17B of market value, Berkshire could own a significant minority stake but not control. Influence would come only through a relationship with management.
- **Mr. Market** (`brk-letter-1987`: "there to serve you, not to guide you"): the price trend gives no reason to buy or to wait.
  - The stock fell from $53 (August 1987) to $29 (October 1987) and traded at $35–40 for most of 1988, about 15–25 times owner earnings. That was the more generous offer, and it is gone.
  - Today's $46.56 is near the top of the 52-week range ($35.04–49.12) and about 22% above mid-1988.
  - This is not a moment of fear. But the framework says to judge price against value, not to time the market (`bpl-letter-1966-mid`). On the central case, today's price is still below value.

## Missing data that would change the numbers

1. **1988 owner earnings and share count.** This is the single largest gap. It moves the whole range.
2. **Long-term government bond yield.** Needed to set the opportunity cost. I used Coca-Cola's own borrowing cost instead.
3. **Local-currency growth.** Needed to separate real growth from currency effects in the 10–13% history.
4. **Maintenance versus growth capex.** This sets where in the $2.07–2.47 range owner earnings sit.
5. **Market values of the CCE, CPE and TCC stakes**, and end-1988 net debt after the buyback program.

## Stage conclusion

- **Literal valuation rule (net working capital): fails.** There is no bargain margin.
- **The as-of-date test (outstanding business at a sensible price, priced as if the future resembles the past): passes.** Central value is about **$60–70 per share** against a price of **$46.56**, a margin of about **22–33%**. That disappears only if growth slows to about 8% *and* money is valued at 12%.
- **No catalyst is needed.** Mr. Market is not offering a panic price but is not asking a full one either.

This supports a buy on valuation grounds, with the size of the position left to Stage 5. Sizing should reflect that the margin rests on confidence in the franchise's durability, not on balance-sheet protection.
