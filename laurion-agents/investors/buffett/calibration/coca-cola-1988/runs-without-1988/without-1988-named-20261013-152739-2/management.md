# Stage 3: Management and capital allocation

I apply the three rules in section 4 of the framework, in order. I also bring in the share-issuance principle from `brk-letter-1982#17`, because the packet shows Coca-Cola issuing stock for an acquisition.

## 4a. Honest, able management already in place; candid reporting (`brk-letter-1979`, `brk-letter-1983#2`)

**Already in place.** Roberto Goizueta (Chairman and CEO) and Donald Keough (President and COO) built the "Strategy for the 1980s" in 1981 and still run the company. Nothing needs to be supplied from outside, which satisfies criterion 4 in `brk-letter-1986#10`.

**Record of ability under this team, 1981–1987:**
- Return on equity rose from about 20% to about 27%. Stage 2 showed that part of the rise came from more debt and a smaller equity base.
- Income from continuing operations rose from $432M to $916M.
- Total return to shareholders compounded at about 25% a year, against 16% for the S&P 500 (ar-1987).
- The company now gets more than 95% of its operating income from soft drinks, its best business.

**Mistakes this team has made, and how it handled them:**
- **The 1985 formula change.** The 1986 report describes the outcome as a "resurgence" and "revitalization" of sugar colas. It never calls the change an error. The *recovery* was handled well: Coca-Cola classic became the top-selling US soft drink in 1986.
- **The entertainment diversification.** The team bought Columbia Pictures in 1982 (price not given in the packet), then Embassy and Tandem in 1985 and Merv Griffin in 1986. In 1987 it combined the whole sector into Columbia Pictures Entertainment (CPE), gave shareholders part of it as a dividend, and kept 49%. The stake carries at $989M. CPE took a $100M restructuring charge in 1987, and the entertainment business earned roughly $50–100M a year on well over $1B of capital. That is a mediocre return on money diverted from the core, but management did pull back.
- **Plastics and pasta.** Presto, Winkler and Ronco were sold in 1984–85.
- **Foods.** The 1987 report says the business is "obviously in transition." Senior management there was changed, and a $36M charge was taken.

The pattern: management strays outside the core but recognizes poor results and retreats.

**Candor.**

| What the reports do well | What reads as promotion |
|---|---|
| Report unusual items separately and restate results without them (for example, 1987 EPS up 17.4% before the 1986 items) | Claims of "$250 billion to the world economy" from a multiplier of five, and international potential that "appears limitless" |
| Quantify how much of growth came from currency moves (+27% in 1986, +16% in 1987) | Comparisons shift every year because of deconsolidations: 1986 revenue was $8.67B as first reported and $6.98B as restated |
| Admit that income on a current-cost basis would be lower | Gains from selling stakes in subsidiaries recur and flow into earnings: $67M in 1985, $375M in 1986, $40M in 1987 |
| Disclose $394M of guarantees and $325M of CPE receivables with recourse | |
| Admit poor film results in 1986 and the difficulties at Foods | |

The facts are disclosed fully. The tone is more of a "public relations document" than the framework likes (`brk-letter-1979`). The recurring subsidiary-sale gains do not affect value, but they need watching: "the CEO who misleads others in public may eventually mislead himself in private" (`brk-letter-1983#2`). On balance this is acceptable but not exemplary.

## 4b. Buybacks only well below intrinsic value (`brk-letter-1984`)

Average prices paid, from the shareholders'-equity statements:

| Year | Shares bought | Cost | Avg price | Multiple of that year's EPS |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1984 | 18.4M | $335.7M | ≈$18.24 | ≈11.6x ($1.57) |
| 1985 | 15.0M | $379.9M | ≈$25.33 | ≈14.7x ($1.72) |
| 1986 | 2.9M | $110.5M | ≈$38.37 | ≈15.9x ($2.42 reported; higher excluding the net one-time gain) |
| 1987 | 14.2M | $605.1M | ≈$42.61 | ≈17.5x ($2.43) |

A 40M-share program was authorized in July 1987 to run through 1990. Shares outstanding fell from 408M (1983 average) to 372M at end-1987. Purchases in 1988 are not in the packet.

**Assessment:**
- **1984–85.** These purchases at about 12–15 times earnings were almost certainly well below what the business was worth. Even the current price of $46.56 is more than double what was paid.
- **1986–87.** These were made at about 17–20 times my owner-earnings estimate of $2.07–2.47 per share. Whether that was "well below" intrinsic value depends on the Stage 4 valuation, so I leave it open here.
- **How management frames buybacks.** It says it buys back stock when "available cash and debt capacity exceeded the funds required," and calls repurchases "often the best use of excess cash." That is a leftover-cash rationale, not an explicit comparison of price with per-share value. It still leads in the right direction for owners.
- **Financing.** The 1987 buybacks were financed largely with short-term debt: loans and notes rose from $698M to $1,685M. Under the framework's preference for "little or no debt," borrowing to buy back stock is a yellow flag.

## 4c. Each $1 retained must create at least $1 of market value (`brk-letter-1983`)

**Market test, 1982–1987.**

*Earnings retained:*
- Net income 1982–87 totalled about $4.27B ($512M, $559M, $629M, $722M, $934M, $916M).
- Cash dividends totalled about $2.25B. The 1984–87 figures come from the cash-flow statements. For 1982–83 I estimated dividends from the reported payout ratios (about $310M and $361M), because the packet does not give the amounts.
- Retained earnings were therefore about $2.0B, or about $1.7B after the $335M in-kind dividend of CPE shares, which I count as a distribution.

*Market value created:*
- End-1981 market value was about $4.3B ($11.58 × about 372M shares).
- End-1987 market value was about $14.2B ($38.13 × 372M), even after $1.43B of buybacks and the CPE spin-off.
- At $46.56 today it is about $17.3B.

On these figures, about $1 retained produced about $5 or more of market value. **The test passes by a wide margin.**

**Correcting for a re-rating.** Much of that gain came from the market paying more for each dollar of earnings: the P/E rose from about 10x in 1981 to about 16x at end-1987 and about 19x now. A business-level check is more conservative. Continuing income rose by about $484M a year on roughly $2.0B of retained earnings, about 24% after tax. That figure is overstated, because added debt also funded part of the growth. Total debt went from $227M to $2.7B, against $1.1B more cash. Even allowing for that, the incremental return on retained capital is clearly above the 12% after-tax cost of capital management cites.

**Where future retentions will go.** Management is cutting the payout toward 40% so it can keep more. But soft-drink capex was only $170M in 1987, so the core business cannot absorb most of the retained earnings. The rest goes to:
- **Bottler stakes.** CCE returned about 5.8% on its equity in 1987. The company also bought 20% of Consolidated for $63M and 21% of Johnston for $54M. These may pay off across the system through higher concentrate volume, but that cannot be measured from the packet.
- **Buybacks**, assessed under 4b.
- **Debt-funded "investment opportunities"** under the higher 35% net-debt ceiling.

So the test passes on history. Whether it keeps passing depends on management's discipline with excess cash, and the entertainment record is the main caution.

**Share issuance (`brk-letter-1982#17`).** In 1985 the company issued about 7.1M treasury shares, worth about $169M (about $24 per share), to buy Embassy. That is stock that now trades near $47, given for an entertainment asset that earned low returns: a value-diluting issuance of the kind the framework criticizes. Since then the company has been a net repurchaser, and option-related dilution is small, about 1.1–1.3M shares a year on 372M.

**Compensation, as far as the packet shows:**
- 5.6M options are outstanding, and a new plan covering 8M shares was adopted in 1987.
- A restricted-stock plan covers 3M shares.
- Performance units pay cash based on the stock price above $20.63 on 555,000 shares.

Pay is tied to the share price, not to return on equity, which the framework treats as the primary test. Salary levels and the proxy statement are not in the packet.

## Information the investor would have relied on that I lack

- **Direct conversation with management.** In particular, how Goizueta and Keough think about the price paid in buybacks against per-share value, and their appetite for further acquisitions outside soft drinks.
- **How much stock management and directors own.** The framework values "we eat our own cooking" (`brk-letter-1983#0`).
- **The proxy statement:** salaries, bonus formulas, option terms.
- **1988 results and buyback activity**, including the average price paid under the 40M-share program.
- **The prices paid for Columbia Pictures in 1982**, and the full return on the entertainment venture once CPE is valued at market.
- **Bottler-system economics**, to judge whether the bottler stakes earn their keep through concentrate volume.

## Stage conclusion

Management passes.
- **Positives:** the team is able and already in place; it now concentrates on the best business it owns; it has pruned weak diversifications; it is returning large amounts of cash; and retained earnings have far exceeded the $1-for-$1 test.
- **Reservations:** an entertainment detour partly paid for with stock; a deliberate rise in leverage; buybacks justified as a use of leftover cash rather than by explicit comparison with value; promotional reporting; and pay linked to the stock price rather than returns.

None of these is disqualifying. They argue for watching how the company uses excess cash, and for checking in Stage 4 whether the current price, and the price recent buybacks were made at, is below intrinsic value.
