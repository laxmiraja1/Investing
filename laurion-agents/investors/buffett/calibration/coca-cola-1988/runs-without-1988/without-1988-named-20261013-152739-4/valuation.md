# Stage 4: Valuation (§5) and Catalyst / timing (§6)

Price on the as-of date: **$46.56** (close 1989-02-24). Shares: **372.4M** (end-1987; 1988 buybacks are unknown). Market value: **$17.34B**. All business figures are 1987, because the packet has no 1988 results.

---

## Step 1: The framework's stated valuation rule, buy below net working capital (`bpl-letter-1965`)

| Item ($M, end-1987) | Amount |
|---|---|
| Current assets | 4,136.2 |
| – Current liabilities | (4,118.5) |
| **Net working capital** | **17.7 → about $0.05/share** |
| – Long-term debt and deferred taxes | (1,013.2) |
| **Net current assets after all liabilities** | **–995.5 (negative)** |

The price is $46.56 against $0.05 of working capital, and 5.4x book value of $8.66. **Coca-Cola fails the stated §5 test completely.** It is nothing like a net-working-capital bargain.

### Why that result does not end the analysis

By 1989 the framework has said in its own words that it no longer looks for this kind of bargain:
- "Our goal is to find an outstanding business at a sensible price, not a mediocre business at a bargain price… I searched for 'bargains' – and had the misfortune to find some" (`brk-letter-1987#18`).
- "We may very well pay a fairly fancy price for a Category 1 business if we are reasonably confident of what we are getting" (`brk-letter-1981#3`).
- Book value is beside the point: paying six times book "does not change CCH's return on equity" (`brk-letter-1987#1`).

So the NWC rule applies to the 1960s "generals". For a franchise business in 1989, the test the framework actually supplies is a different one. The price must make sense "even should the future of the acquired enterprise develop much along the lines of its past" (`brk-letter-1981#3`). And it should be judged on **owner earnings** (`brk-letter-1986`, §3), not on projections ("future projections are of little interest to us," `brk-letter-1982`). That is the method used below.

## Step 3: The comparison yardstick

The framework compares an equity's earnings return with what a bond pays (`fortune-1977-inflation`). It gives no discount rate.

**Missing from the packet:** the long-term US Treasury yield on the as-of date.

**Proxies the packet does provide:**
- Coca-Cola's own notes carry coupons of about 9.9–12.75%, and its "other" debt averages about 10%. I use **10%**, a bond-like alternative return.
- Management's stated after-tax cost of capital is **12%**. I use this as a stricter second case.

**Breakeven growth.** At a 4.0% starting yield, owner earnings must grow about **6% a year indefinitely** to match a 10% bond, and about **8%** to match 12%.

## Step 4: Intrinsic value if the future follows the past

The framework asks what the business is worth if it keeps doing roughly what it has done.

**Growth "along the lines of the past"**
- Income from continuing operations per share grew **11.2% a year** from 1977 to 1987 ($0.84 → $2.43), and 13.0% from 1981 to 1987.
- That record includes currency gains in 1986–87 and share issuance and buybacks.
- Worldwide soft drink volume grew 10%, 10% and 6% in 1985–87.

**Method.** This is two-stage, and it simply carries the record forward rather than forecasting:
- Base owner earnings of $1.67 grow at g₁ for 10 years.
- After that, growth slows to a long-run g₂. This is my assumption, deliberately set below g₁.
- Discount at r.
- Add the affiliates at $5.37 per share.

Worked example (r = 10%, g₁ = 11%, g₂ = 5%):
- Present value of years 1–10: $1.67 × 10.51 = **$17.52**.
- Year-11 owner earnings: $1.67 × 1.11¹⁰ × 1.05 = $4.97.
- Terminal value at year 10: $4.97 ÷ (10% – 5%) = $99.3. Present value: $99.3 ÷ 1.10¹⁰ = **$38.30**.
- Core value: $55.82. Plus affiliates $5.37 = **about $61.20**.

### Value grid (base owner earnings $1.67)

| | g₁ = 8%, g₂ = 4% (below the record) | g₁ = 11%, g₂ = 5% (the record continues) |
|---|---|---|
| **r = 10%** | $44.50 (price is **4.6% above** value) | **$61.20 (price is 24% below value)** |
| **r = 12%** | $34.10 (price is 37% above value) | $44.10 (price is 6% above value) |

### Sensitivity to the capex assumption

If maintenance capex is close to depreciation (owner earnings $2.06), core values rise by about 24%:
- r = 10%, slower growth: about **$53.70** (13% margin of safety).
- r = 10%, record continues: about **$75** (38% margin of safety).

### Sensitivity to the missing 1988 results

The base is 1987, more than a year old on the as-of date. If 1988 owner earnings grew at anything like the record, every figure in the grid is understated by roughly that growth (about 10%). I do not add this, because the packet does not show it.

## Step 5: Margin of safety against the framework's hurdle

**The hurdle.** The framework gives no number. It asks for "a substantial excess of value" creating "a comfortable margin of safety" (`bpl-letter-1961`, `bpl-letter-1962`), and for a price that makes sense if the future resembles the past (`brk-letter-1981#3`).

**The result:**
- **If the record continues and money is worth about 10%, value is about $61–75.** The margin of safety is **about 24–38%** on base-to-generous owner earnings. That would meet the hurdle.
- **If growth slows to about 8%, or the correct rate is 12%, value is about $34–54.** The margin is roughly zero, or the price is above value.
- The deciding question is therefore **not the price itself**. It is how confident one can be that the franchise compounds at close to its historical rate for a decade or more.

**What supports that confidence (Stages 2 and 3):**
- about 20% after tax on all capital for over a decade;
- share leadership that held through a major management error;
- per-capita consumption outside the US only 16% of the US level;
- the business needs little reinvestment.

This is the "Category 1 business" for which the framework permits "a fairly fancy price … if we are reasonably confident of what we are getting." On the packet's evidence, that confidence is reasonable.

**What weakens it:**
- part of the record comes from currency;
- 1988 is unseen;
- management has used capital for low-return entertainment and bottler stakes.

**Conclusion:** the price clears the framework's hurdle **only on the record-continues case**, and there it clears it with a real but not extraordinary margin (about 24%, more if maintenance capex is near depreciation). It is a sensible price for an outstanding business, not a bargain. That is exactly the kind of purchase `brk-letter-1987#18` describes.

### Expected return check
A buyer's return is roughly the owner-earnings yield plus growth:
- Starting yield: about 4.0–4.3% (including the look-through share of affiliate earnings).
- Growth: 8–11%.
- Result: **about 12–15% a year** with no change in the market multiple, against the roughly 10% bond-like proxy.

Buybacks made below value would add to growth per share.

### Tie-back to Stage 3 (buybacks)
At $42–47, the 40M-share program is buying **below** value in the record-continues case, by about 24% at the current price. It is **not** buying below value in the slower-growth case. So the repurchases are reasonable but not clearly "well below" intrinsic value (`brk-letter-1984`).

## §6 Catalyst / timing

| Rule | Application |
|---|---|
| **No catalyst needed**: "The market may ignore business success for a while, but eventually will confirm it" (`brk-letter-1987`) | This is the relevant category. The return depends on the franchise compounding, not on an event. No catalyst is required. |
| **Work-outs**: profit from a specific announced corporate action (`bpl-letter-1957`) | Not applicable. The CPE spin-off and the CCE and TCC offerings are complete. The buyback is an ongoing policy, not a dated event with a known payoff. |
| **Controls** (`bpl-letter-1963`) | Not applicable at a $17B market value. The model is a large passive minority stake, the framework's "non-voting partnership" with excellent managers (`brk-letter-1981#3`), like the GEICO, Washington Post and Capital Cities holdings (`brk-letter-1987#18`). |
| **No market forecasting** (`bpl-letter-1966-mid`); "Mr. Market is there to serve you, not to guide you" (`brk-letter-1987`) | No view on the market's direction is taken. The stock is up about 33% from its 52-week low ($35.04) and near its 52-week high ($49.12). That is a valuation fact already reflected above, not a timing signal. |
| **Fearful when others are greedy** (`brk-letter-1986`) | Mood toward this stock is not fearful: it trades at 19x earnings against about 10x in 1981. The framework would prefer to buy during fear, but it does not require waiting for it when value covers price. A better entry might come if Mr. Market offers one, and the investor would buy more then. |

## Stage 4 summary

- **§5 net-working-capital test:** fails (NWC about $0.05 per share, net current assets negative). The framework itself has moved past this test for franchise businesses (`brk-letter-1987#18`, `brk-letter-1981#3`).
- **Owner-earnings value, with the past carried forward at a 10% bond-like rate:** about **$61 per share** on base owner earnings, **about $75** if maintenance capex is near depreciation. Against $46.56, the margin of safety is **about 24–38%**.
- **Conservative cases** (slower growth, or a 12% rate): about $34–54, a thin margin or none.
- **Judgment:** a sensible price for an outstanding business, with a meaningful margin of safety resting on confidence in the franchise's durability rather than on cheapness.
- **Catalyst:** none needed. This is a long-duration compounding holding.
- **Gaps:**
  - 1988 results (their absence probably makes these values too low);
  - the Treasury yield on the as-of date;
  - the split between maintenance and growth capex;
  - the current market value of the affiliates.
