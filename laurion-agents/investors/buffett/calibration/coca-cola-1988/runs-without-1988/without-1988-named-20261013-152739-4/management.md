## §4 Rule 1: Honest, able management already in place; candid reporting

### Management in place
Roberto Goizueta (Chairman and CEO) and Donald Keough (President and COO) assembled the current team in 1981 (`ar-1987`). Over their tenure:
- ROE rose from about 20% to 27%.
- Soft drink operating income roughly doubled.
- The company kept to a stated plan, the "Strategy for the 1980s."

This is management with a record across several years and business conditions, which is what the acquisition criteria ask for ("management in place (we can't supply it)", `brk-letter-1982`).

### Ability
- **The core business was run well.** The franchise gained share every year in the packet. It recovered from the 1985 formula change within a year. The bottler system was restructured (CCE, TCC, the British joint venture, Germany cut from 102 bottlers to 68) so that the capital-heavy layer sits outside the parent.
- **They think in the right terms.** Management states a 12% after-tax cost of capital, ranks investments by return against it, and headlines ROE rather than EPS ("the combination of return on equity reached and per share earnings growth was the best since 1936," `ar-1986`). That matches the framework's primary test (`brk-letter-1979`).

### Candor
The framework asks for "the business facts that we would want to know if our positions were reversed" (`brk-letter-1983#2`), not "a public relations document" (`brk-letter-1979`).

**On the candid side:**
- One-time items are separated out, and the figures excluding them check against the statements (Stage 2).
- Currency effects on growth are disclosed every year.
- Management admits current-cost income is lower than reported.
- It writes that inflation gains on net monetary liabilities "do not represent an increase in funds available for distribution… and do not necessarily imply that incurring more debt would be beneficial." That is an unusually honest sentence for a company raising its own debt ceiling.
- Bad news is reported plainly: "we were not satisfied with the performance of Columbia Pictures' feature films" (`ar-1986`); Foods is "obviously in transition" (`ar-1987`).

**On the promotional side:**
- The 1987 report adds "$250 billion to the world economy" using a "multiplier factor of five." This is public-relations material.
- The letter to shareholders is short and adjective-heavy ("creative, decisive actions").
- Annual deconsolidations and restatements are disclosed consistently, but they make it hard to compare years.

**Verdict on candor:** acceptable to good. The financial review is more candid than the narrative sections, and the numbers can be relied on.

## §4 Rule 2: Buybacks only well below intrinsic value

The rule is that repurchases are good only "at prices well below per-share intrinsic business value" (`brk-letter-1984`).

### Prices paid (derived from treasury stock purchases)

| Year | Shares bought | Cost ($M) | Avg. price | P/E on that year's EPS |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1984 | 18.4M | 335.7 | $18.24 | 11.6x ($1.57) |
| 1985 | 15.0M | 379.9 | $25.33 | 14.7x ($1.72) |
| 1986 | 2.9M | 110.5 | $38.37 | 18.5x ($2.07, ex-unusual items) |
| 1987 | 14.1M | 605.1 | $42.79 | 17.6x ($2.43) |
| 1988 | not in packet | — | — | — |

The 40M-share program (more than 10% of shares, through 1990) had bought 6.6M shares at about $42.12 by the end of 1987.

### Assessment

- **1984–85:** buying at 11–15x earnings, in a business earning about 20% or more on all capital with a long runway, was very probably well below intrinsic value. These buybacks are the kind the framework approves of.
- **1986–87 and the current program:** buying at 17–19x reported earnings. That is about 21–24x the owner-earnings range of roughly $1.77–2.17. Whether these were "well below" intrinsic value depends on the growth rate, which is Stage 4's question. They are a closer call than 1984–85, not an obvious bargain.
- **The rationale given is the wrong one.** Management says repurchases began because "available cash and debt capacity exceeded the funds required" (`ar-1987`). That is an excess-cash argument, not a price-versus-value argument. The framework wants the second.
- **They are funded with borrowed money.** 1987 buybacks were financed largely through short-term borrowing, under a net-debt ceiling raised to 35% to lower the "weighted average cost of capital." Net debt of 15.3% is moderate. Still, the framework's preference for "little or no debt" and its 1977 observation that highly profitable firms "require relatively little debt capital" (`fortune-1977-inflation`) count against borrowing to buy stock.
- **Timing is unknown.** The 1987 range was $28.96–$53.13 and the packet does not show which quarters the shares were bought in. It cannot show whether management bought into the October crash.

### A valuation inconsistency

In 1985 the company **issued** about 7.1M treasury shares at roughly $23.80 to buy Embassy, while **repurchasing** 15M shares at about $25.33 that same year.

The framework requires the same yardstick for shares given and shares received (`brk-letter-1982#17`). If $25 was a price worth paying to buy Coca-Cola stock back, it was too low a price at which to hand out Coca-Cola stock for a television producer. Shares were also issued for Columbia in 1982: average shares rose from 372M (1981) to 408M (1983) at about 13x earnings. The 1984–87 buybacks (about 50M shares) mostly undid those issuances.

**Verdict on Rule 2:** the early buybacks pass. The recent ones are pending the Stage 4 valuation. The rationale and funding are weaker than the framework would like, and the 1982–85 share issuances for acquisitions fail the same-yardstick test.

## §4 Rule 3: Each $1 retained must create at least $1 of market value

The test is whether "retention, over time, delivers shareholders at least $1 of market value for each $1 retained" (`brk-letter-1983`).

### Period: end-1981 (start of current management) to the last close

**Earnings retained, 1982–87**
- Net income, 1982–87: $4,272M.
- Cash dividends (dividend per share × average shares): about $2,264M.
- Retained before the in-kind dividend: about $2,008M.
- Retained after the $335M CPE distribution: **about $1,673M.**
- 1988 is missing. If 1988 retention were roughly $500–600M (an assumption only, since 1988 results are not in the packet), the total would be about $2.2–2.3B.

**Market value created**
- End-1981: 372M shares × $11.58 = $4.31B.
- 1989-02-24: 372.4M shares × $46.56 = $17.34B.
- Gain: about **$13.0B**, before counting the CPE shares already distributed.

**Ratio:** about $5.5–7.8 of market value per $1 retained.

### Stress tests

1. **Strip out the re-rating.** The price/earnings multiple rose from about 9.9x (end-1981: $11.58 ÷ $1.17) to 19.2x. At the old 9.9x, market value would be about $8.96B, a gain of $4.65B. That is still about $2.0–2.8 per $1 retained.
2. **Add the debt.** Net debt went from roughly net cash of $117M (1981) to net debt of roughly $380–690M (1987, depending on what is netted). Adding about $0.5–0.8B to the capital base still leaves about $1.7–2.1 of value per $1, even at the old multiple.
3. **Business value.** Earnings from continuing operations rose from $432M (1981) to $916M (1987), an increase of $484M. Against about $2.2–2.8B of retained earnings plus added debt, that is roughly a 17–22% after-tax incremental return. Part of it comes from currency (Stage 2).

**Verdict on Rule 3: passes comfortably** on every version of the test, including those that remove the re-rating and add the debt.

**The pattern:** the 1982–86 period went outside the circle (entertainment) and paid partly in undervalued stock. From 1986 on, the trend has been back toward the core, with low-return assets separated out. The framework would credit the direction and remember the detour.

## Dividend and retention policy

Management plans to cut the payout ratio toward 40% (1982: 62.8%, 1987: 46%) so that more can be reinvested "in opportunities offering returns in excess of the cost of capital."

The difficulty: the core cannot absorb much capital. In 1987, $1.23B of cash came from operations against $300M of capex. Retained cash therefore goes mainly to:
- buybacks (good if priced below value),
- bottler stakes (about 5%),
- a growing cash balance ($1.47B).

The retention test has been passed historically. Whether it keeps being passed depends on management directing the extra retention to buybacks below value rather than to more low-return stakes. The packet shows both happening.

## Compensation

- Stock plans: 3M-share Restricted Stock Award Plan, 1983 Option Plan (6M shares), 1987 Option Plan (8M shares).
- 5.6M options outstanding at end-1987 (about 1.5% of shares).
- Annual grants of about 1.1–1.8M options plus about 0.2M restricted shares, roughly 0.4–0.5% per year, more than offset by buybacks. Issued shares rose only from 410.0M (1984) to 416.0M (1987).
- 1985 Performance Unit Agreements pay cash on the rise in the share price above $20.63 across 555,000 shares.

**Reading:** the dilution is modest. The pay is tied to the share price, not to returns on capital or per-share business value, so it rewards leverage and re-rating as well as real progress. The packet gives no pay amounts and no proxy statement.

## What the investor would rely on that the packet does not contain

1. **Meeting Goizueta and Keough.** The framework puts great weight on joining "high-grade, talented and likeable managers" (`brk-letter-1987#18`) and judging "the quality of the people" (`brk-letter-1986#10`). Documents cannot replace that judgment.
2. **Insider and director share ownership** ("We eat our own cooking," `brk-letter-1983#0`). The proxy statement is not in the packet.
3. **Executive pay amounts** and the performance terms of the awards.
4. **1988 results and 1988 buyback prices and volume.**
5. **The 1982 Columbia purchase price and terms**, needed to measure the full cost of the entertainment detour.
6. **Management's stated intentions** on further diversification versus concentrating on soft drinks, and on how it will use the new borrowing capacity.
7. **The buyback timing within 1987**, around the October crash.
8. **Succession** beyond the two principals.

## Stage 3 conclusion

**Strengths:**
- Management is able and in place, with a seven-year record.
- It thinks in returns and cost of capital, not EPS.
- Its financial disclosure is reasonably candid.
- The $1-retained test is passed by a wide margin, even after removing the re-rating.
- The current direction (back to the core, separating out low-return capital, buying back stock) is the right one.

**Weaknesses:**
- The 1982–86 move into entertainment, paid partly with shares issued at prices below value. This fails the same-yardstick test of `brk-letter-1982#17`.
- About $1B still held in a 5%-return CPE stake.
- Buybacks justified by excess cash rather than price against value, and funded partly with debt.
- Pay tied to the share price.

**Handed forward to §5 (valuation):** whether the current 40M-share program, at about $42–47, is buying "well below" intrinsic value, against owner earnings of about $1.65–2.40 per share with 1988 missing.
