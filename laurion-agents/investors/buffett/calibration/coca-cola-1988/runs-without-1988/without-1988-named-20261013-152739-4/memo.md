**The Coca-Cola Company: Recommendation to Buy (as of February 27, 1989)**

We recommend buying shares of The Coca-Cola Company at around $46.56. We would build the position over time to roughly a quarter of the portfolio, and buy more if the price falls without any damage to the business. This memo explains what the business is, why it meets our standards, what we think it is worth, and what would change our view. Everything here comes from the company's 1986 and 1987 annual reports and price data through February 24, 1989. No 1988 results were available to us.

At its heart, Coca-Cola sells syrup and concentrate to bottlers and fountain customers in more than 155 countries. Those customers make, distribute and sell the drinks, and the company collects what amounts to a royalty on the world's best-known trademark. Soft drinks produced more than 95% of operating income in 1987. The rest of the company is a juice business and a set of minority stakes in bottlers and in Columbia Pictures Entertainment. That juice business, sold mainly under the Minute Maid name, is small and has little competitive protection.

The business passes each of our screens. It is not a turnaround: earnings from continuing operations rose every year from $310 million in 1977 to $916 million in 1987. It is simple to understand. It does not need debt to operate. From 1977 to 1979 it earned about 21% on equity while debt was only 4–6% of capital. Today, net of excess cash, debt is about 15% of capital, and that borrowing pays for share repurchases and bottler stakes by choice rather than necessity.

The economics are what make the business exceptional. In 1987 the soft drink operations earned about 34% before tax on the assets they use. The company generated $1.23 billion of cash from operations and spent only $300 million on plant and equipment. Measured on all capital, debt included, it earns about 20% after tax, which is the same rate it earned in the late 1970s with almost no borrowing. The recent rise in return on equity to about 27% reflects a smaller equity base and modest leverage. The underlying economics have not changed.

The evidence for a durable advantage is concrete. The company has 44% of world soft drink sales and roughly double the volume of its nearest competitor. It holds 59% of the US fountain business and earns a gross margin of about 63% on concentrate. The most telling evidence is what happened in 1985, when management changed the formula of its flagship drink. Within a year, Coca-Cola classic was the best-selling soft drink in the United States, and the company's share reached a record. A franchise that survives a mistake of that size without losing customers is unusually strong. There is also room to grow: consumption per person outside the United States is about one-sixth of the US level.

Management under Roberto Goizueta and Donald Keough has a mixed but net-favourable record. On the positive side, they measure themselves by return on capital, report unusual items candidly, and have bought back more than 50 million shares since 1984. The best test is what retained earnings have produced. Since 1981 the company has kept about $1.7 billion of earnings, and its market value has risen by about $13 billion. Even if we assume the stock still traded at its 1981 earnings multiple, each retained dollar has produced about two dollars of value. On the negative side, the 1982–86 move into entertainment put capital into a business earning a fraction of what soft drinks earn, and it was paid for partly with shares issued at low prices. That stake is now separated from the core, but about $1 billion is still tied up in it at a low return. Recent repurchases have been justified by spare cash rather than by price against value, and partly funded with borrowing. We would want to meet management and review their ownership and pay before committing fully.

We value the company on owner earnings: reported profit adjusted for non-cash items, less the capital spending needed to sustain the business. On 1987 figures, owner earnings from the core business come to about $1.67 per share, or about $2.06 if maintenance spending is close to depreciation. Suppose earnings keep growing at roughly the 11% rate of the past decade, slow afterwards, and are discounted at about 10%, which is close to what the company pays on its own debt. On that basis the core is worth about $56 a share. Adding the bottler and entertainment stakes at market value brings the total to about $61, and to about $75 if maintenance spending is near depreciation. Against $46.56, that is a margin of safety of about 24% to 38%, and an expected return of roughly 12% to 15% a year before any change in the multiple.

The margin depends heavily on growth continuing. If growth falls to about 8%, or the right discount rate is 12%, value drops to between $34 and $54 and the margin disappears. This is not a statistical bargain: the stock trades at 19 times earnings and far above working capital. We are buying an outstanding business at a sensible price because the record gives good grounds to expect it to keep compounding.

For that reason we are not taking the maximum position. Currency gains flattered results in 1986 and 1987. The 1988 figures are unseen. The company also has about $394 million of loan guarantees and close ties to heavily indebted bottlers.

We would reconsider if 1988 results show volume or underlying operating income stalling, or if market share or concentrate margins erode. Another large acquisition outside soft drinks, paid for with undervalued stock, would also change our view, as would debt rising beyond management's own ceiling to fund low-return projects. Price movements alone would not lead us to sell. We intend to hold the shares for as long as the business and its management remain what they are today.
