# Stage 4: Valuation of Company A (AAA), as of 1989-02-27, price $46.56

**Result:** the stock fails the framework's bargain test by a wide margin, but on owner earnings it sits about 10–25% below a central intrinsic value of roughly $52–60 a share. That is a sensible price for an outstanding business, not a bargain. The margin of safety holds at a 10% discount rate and disappears at management's own 12% cost of capital.

## 5. Valuation, rule as written: buy below net working capital (`bpl-letter-1965`)

The only valuation rule in the framework is the partnership-era test: buy for less than net working capital, giving no value to plant or franchise. End-1987 balance sheet (`ar-1987`), $ millions:

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Current assets | 4,136.2 |
| Current liabilities | (4,118.5) |
| **Net working capital** | **17.7**, about $0.05 per share |
| Same, excluding the $335M CPE stock dividend payable (a non-cash liability, paid in January 1988) | 352.7, about $0.95 per share |
| Current assets less *all* liabilities ($5,131.8M) | (995.6), about −$2.67 per share |

At $46.56 the price is about 50 times the most generous net-working-capital figure, and the stricter measure (current assets less all liabilities) is negative. **On the framework's literal valuation rule, this is not a bargain and never would be.**

That result is expected. By the as-of date the framework's own writing has moved this test to the bargain category it calls "generals":

- "Our goal is to find an outstanding business at a sensible price, not a mediocre business at a bargain price" (`brk-letter-1987#18`).
- Berkshire "may very well pay a fairly fancy price for a Category 1 business if we are reasonably confident of what we are getting." Prices must "make sense, even should the future of the acquired enterprise develop much along the lines of its past" (`brk-letter-1981#3`).
- Book value and intrinsic value "differ widely" (`brk-letter-1986#0`). Paying six times book does not change the business's return on equity (`brk-letter-1987#1`).

So failing the working-capital test is not decisive for a business that Stage 2 found to be a franchise. The framework's tool for valuing such a business is **owner earnings** (`brk-letter-1986`), tested against the standard "makes sense if the future develops much along the lines of its past." The packet's quotes stop short of giving a formula, so I capitalize owner earnings with growth. I label the inputs as assumptions where the packet is silent.

| Growth in per-share owner earnings, years 1–10 | 8% / 10% / 12% | Past record: continuing-operations earnings grew about 13% a year, 1981–87 ($432M to $916M), helped by the falling dollar since 1986. Volume is growing 6–10% a year, and the company says it prices with inflation. 10% base, 8% conservative |
| Growth after year 10 | 5% | Assumption. At 4% the base value falls by about 12% |

The valuation uses 1987 figures because there are no 1988 results. If 1988 owner earnings grew about 10%, every value below rises about 10%. I do not assume that.

## Calculation: core value plus equity investments

First I value $1 of current owner earnings for each scenario: ten years at the stated growth, then 5% a year forever, discounted at the stated rate. Example, 10% discount and 10% growth:
- Years 1–10 are worth 10 × OE.
- Year-10 owner earnings are 2.594 × OE. A perpetuity growing at 5% from there is worth (2.594 × 1.05 ÷ 0.05) ÷ 2.594 = 21 × OE in today's money.
- Total: 31.0 × owner earnings.

| Scenario (discount / growth) | Multiple of owner earnings | Core value ($1.74 × multiple) | + Investments | **Value / share** | Margin vs $46.56 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 10% / 12% | 36.2x | 63.0 | 6.2 | **69.2** | 33% |
| **10% / 10% (base)** | 31.0x | 53.9 | 6.2 | **60.1** | **23%** |
| 10% / 8% (conservative) | 26.5x | 46.1 | 6.2 | **52.3** | **11%** |
| 12% / 12% | 25.0x | 43.5 | 6.2 | 49.7 | 6% |
| 12% / 10% | 21.6x | 37.6 | 6.2 | 43.8 | −6% |
| 12% / 8% | 18.7x | 32.5 | 6.2 | 38.7 | −20% |

**Reverse test: what does the price assume?**
- Taking out the $6.18 of investments leaves $40.38 per share for the core business, an owner-earnings yield of 1.74 ÷ 40.38 = 4.3%.
- At a 10% discount rate, $40.38 equals the value of those earnings growing about 5.5% a year forever. At 12% it equals about 7.4% a year forever.
- The business has grown about 13% a year, and volume growth alone has been 6–10% a year before pricing. At 10% the price therefore holds up even if the future is considerably worse than the past. At 12% it needs roughly the volume growth the company has been delivering.

**Expected return at a constant price-to-earnings multiple:** 4.4% owner-earnings yield plus 8–10% per-share growth gives about 12–14% a year. That is above the roughly 10% proxy for the cost of money and level with or above management's 12% hurdle.

## Margin of safety against the framework's hurdle

**The hurdle.** The packet's rules give no numerical margin of safety for buying a franchise. The partnership letters asked for "a substantial excess of value" (`bpl-letter-1961#3`, `bpl-letter-1962#4`), and the later writing accepts "a fairly fancy price" when Berkshire is "reasonably confident" (`brk-letter-1981#3`). I set the test as: the price must be at or below value in the conservative case (10% discount, growth below the past record), and show a meaningful margin in the base case.

**Result:**
- Conservative case: $52.3 against $46.56, an 11% margin. **Passes.**
- Base case: $60.1, a 23% margin, so the price is about 0.77 of value.
- At a 12% discount with 10% growth or less, **it fails**.

So the margin of safety rests on one judgment: whether this franchise is predictable enough to discount at roughly bond-like rates. Stage 2's evidence supports that judgment strongly: a century-old brand, 44% of world volume, the brand's survival after the 1985 formula change, and about 50% after-tax returns on operating capital. Even so, a margin of 11–23% is a sensible price, not a "toad-like" one.

**What would make the margin bigger or smaller:**
- **Unknown 1988 results.** Ten percent growth would lift the base value to about $66 and the margin to about 30%.
- **Currency.** 74% of operating income is earned abroad. A rising dollar would reverse part of the 1986–87 gains.
- **Investment holdings.** I used the end-1987 market value; their value at the as-of date is not in the packet.
- **Off-balance-sheet guarantees** of $394M plus $325M of CPE receivables sold with recourse. CPE has agreed to cover any losses on the receivables. Together these are worth at most about $1.90 a share and are probably mostly nil.
- **Capex.** If maintenance capex is at or below depreciation, as Stage 2 suggested, owner earnings are understated and so is every value above.

## 6. Catalyst / timing

- **No catalyst needed** (`brk-letter-1987`): "The market may ignore business success for a while, but eventually will confirm it." The value case rests on the business compounding about 10% a year, not on a revaluation. The 40-million-share buyback program adds to per-share value over time, but I have not counted it as a catalyst.
- **Mr. Market serves, not guides** (`brk-letter-1987`). The stock has risen from about $37–38 in early 1988 to $46.56, near its 52-week high of $49.12. In October 1987 it traded at $28.96. A higher price is not a reason to buy, and the recent run-up is not by itself a reason to wait. The only test is price against value, and that shows a modest margin.
- **No market forecasting** (`bpl-letter-1966-mid`). I make no forecast of the general market.
- **Work-out** (`bpl-letter-1957`): not applicable. There is no announced merger, tender offer or liquidation; the buyback is an open-market program.
- **Control** (`bpl-letter-1963`): not applicable at a $17.3 billion market value. A minority stake meaningful to Berkshire is possible given the stock's liquidity, consistent with "purchasing fractional interests in easily-identifiable princes" (`brk-letter-1981#3`).

## Carry-forward to Stage 5 (sizing and risk)

- **Central value:** about $52–60 per share, against a price of $46.56.
- **Margin:** about 11–23% at a 10% discount rate, none at 12%.
- **Expected return:** about 12–14% a year if the multiple holds.
- **Concentration:** depends on the "very low probability that anything could drastically change the underlying value" test (`bpl-letter-1965`), which Stage 2 largely supports.
- **Risks to size against:** currency, leverage policy, the low-return affiliates, and the missing 1988 results.
