# Stage 2: Business analysis of Company A (AAA), as of 1989-02-27

The core soft-drink concentrate business passes all three business-analysis tests by a wide margin. Its high returns on equity came before the debt, the trademark has measurable evidence of a moat, and owner earnings are close to reported earnings. The peripheral holdings (orange juice, the 49% entertainment stake and the bottling affiliates) are low-return and need to be valued separately at Stage 4.

## 3.1 Return on equity, not EPS growth

The framework's primary test is a high return on equity "without undue leverage, accounting gimmickry, etc." (`brk-letter-1979`). Three checks follow from that wording.

**The reported figures.** These come from the selected-data tables in `ar-1986` and `ar-1987`.

| Year | Return on average equity | Total debt / capital |
|---|---|---|
| 1977 | 20.6% | 3.5% |
| 1978 | 21.2% | 3.8% |
| 1979 | 21.1% | 6.3% |
| 1980 | 19.7% | 9.3% |
| 1981 | 19.9% | 9.1% |
| 1982 | 19.6% | 15.1% |
| 1983 | 19.4% | 15.1% |
| 1984 | 21.8% | 30.7% (restated) |
| 1985 | 23.5% | 27.7% |
| 1986 | 28.8% | 31.4% |
| 1987 | 27.2% | 45.6% gross; 15.3% net |

**Check 1: did leverage create the returns?** No. In 1977–79 the company earned about 21% on equity with debt at 3–6% of capital. That alone passes the test.

The rise from about 20% to about 27% after 1984 coincides with two things:
- more borrowing;
- heavy buybacks. Treasury stock went from $11.6 million at the start of 1984 to $1,309 million at the end of 1987.

Buybacks shrink the equity base, so part of the recent improvement is a smaller denominator rather than better business. For valuation I would treat the debt-free figure of about 20% as the floor and the recent 27% as partly financial.

**Check 2: does the reported ROE understate the core business?** Yes. At end-1987, equity of $3,224 million includes about $2,259 million of investments in affiliates:

| Affiliate | Carrying value |
|---|---|
| CPE (entertainment) | $989M |
| CCE (U.S. bottler) | $749M |
| TCC (Canadian bottler) | $84M |
| Other | $435M |

These produced only $118.5 million of equity income, about 5%.

Stripping them out gives a rough return on the operating business:
- 1987 operating income was $1,324 million. Adding back the $36 million provision gives $1,360 million. At the 35% effective tax rate that is about $884 million after tax.
- Capital employed: total capital of $5,926 million, less the affiliate investments ($2,259M), excess cash ($1,268M), the CPE note ($545M) and temporary investments ($308M), leaves about $1.55 billion.
- The result is roughly 50% or more after tax. It is approximate, because corporate fixed assets are left in the base.

The segment data points the same way. Soft-drink operating income was $1,432.5 million against $4,174 million of identifiable assets, a pre-tax return of 34% on gross assets. For 1986, with provisions added back, it was about 36%. International alone earned 52% pre-tax ($1,108.9M on $2,126.7M of assets).

For scale, the framework cites a Fortune finding that only 6 of the 1,000 largest companies averaged more than 30% on equity over a decade (`brk-letter-1987#1`). The core of Company A is in that range or above it.

**Check 3: accounting.** Two adjustments are needed before comparing years.
- 1986 earnings per share of $2.42 included a $375 million pre-tax gain on the CCE stock sale, worth $0.68 per share, partly offset by $180 million of provisions. Excluding both, 1986 net income was about $800 million.
- 1987 had a $40 million gain on TCC and a $36 million provision, which roughly cancel.
- On that basis, "underlying" net income grew 14.5% in 1987, matching management's figure.

Management's letters lead with earnings-per-share growth and stock-price total return. That is the yardstick the framework tells us not to use. It is an observation for Stage 3, not a defect in the business.

**Verdict on 3.1:** passes clearly.

## 3.2 Durable competitive advantage (the moat)

The framework's example of a moat is a cost advantage (GEICO, `brk-letter-1986#16`). Here the evidence is a combination of brand, scale and cost.

**Scale and share.**
- Company soft drinks are 44% of world soft-drink volume (excluding the USSR and China), with double the volume of the nearest competitor (`ar-1987`).
- U.S. share is above 39%, which is 9 points ahead of the main U.S. competitor (`ar-1986`).
- U.S. fountain share is 59%. The company outsells its nearest rival more than 2-to-1 in fountain, and took nearly three-quarters of that segment's growth over four years.

**Cost position.** Management claims "low-cost producer" status in most of its 155 countries. The packet gives no competitor cost figures to verify that claim.

**Pricing power and margin.**
- Gross margin on concentrate sold to U.S. bottlers is about 63% (`ar-1986`).
- Soft-drink operating margins in 1987 were 27% internationally and 15% in the U.S. The U.S. figure is lower because syrup sales include sweetener.
- The new CCE contracts let the company set prices "at its discretion", and it used that right in October 1986.
- Management states it can raise prices to keep up with inflation. This matters under the framework's inflation analysis (`fortune-1977-inflation`): businesses that need little capital and can reprice protect owners best.

**Durability under a test.** In 1985 the company changed the taste of its flagship product. Consumers forced the original back as "Brand A classic", which by 1986 was the best-selling soft drink in the U.S. A brand that survived its owner's own mistake shows unusual loyalty.

**Room to grow.**
- International per-person consumption was 16% of the U.S. rate in 1986.
- Even the U.S. rose from 203 servings per person in 1980 to 274 in 1987.
- More than 60% of volume is now international.

**Tax-cut check** (`brk-letter-1986#22`: a strong franchise keeps the benefit of a corporate tax cut). The statutory rate fell from 46% to 40% in 1987. Underlying net income grew 14.5% on revenue growth of 9.8%, which is consistent with the company keeping the benefit. Changes in the tax mix stop me from isolating the effect.

**Qualifications.**
- *Contracts limit U.S. pricing.* Standard contracts with U.S. bottlers other than CCE fix Brand A syrup prices by formula. The pricing freedom in the U.S. is therefore partly limited by contract.
- *Bottler concentration.* CCE handles about 45% of U.S. volume. The 49% stake and the discretionary-pricing contract manage this risk rather than remove it.
- *Currency.* 74% of operating income comes from outside the U.S. Key currencies rose 27% in 1986 and 16% in 1987. International operating income grew about 25% in 1987 excluding unusual items, and a meaningful part of that is translation, not business growth.
- *Cost of maintaining the moat.* Media advertising was $720 million in 1987, and co-operative advertising with U.S. bottlers ran about $234 million in 1986. This is the brand equivalent of GEICO continually "widening the moat".

**Parts outside the moat.**
- *Foods* is mainly orange juice, with prices that move with the crop. Operating income fell from $120 million to $67 million, including the $36 million charge. It is now about 5% of operating income.
- *Entertainment (CPE, 49%, $989M carrying value)* is a hit-driven business that I cannot forecast from this packet.
- *Bottlers* are capital-heavy and leveraged. CCE earned $88 million on $1.53 billion of equity (about 6%) and carries $2.72 billion of liabilities. This is the low-return, capital-hungry kind of business the framework's textile and Burlington lessons warn about (`brk-letter-1985#9`). The company has moved these businesses off its own balance sheet, but it still holds them.

**Verdict on 3.2:** the core franchise passes. The peripheral holdings should be valued at their market or carrying value, not credited with the franchise's economics.

## 3.3 Owner earnings, 1987

The framework defines owner earnings as reported earnings plus depreciation and other non-cash charges, less average capital expenditure (`brk-letter-1986`). All figures below are $ millions.

| Item | 1987 |
|---|---|
| Net income | 916.1 |
| + Depreciation | 151.9 |
| + Goodwill amortization | 1.6 |
| − Capital expenditures | (299.6) |
| **Unadjusted owner earnings** | **770.0**, about $2.07/share on 372.4M shares |
| − Non-cash TCC gain (pre-tax; tax not given) | (39.7) |
| + Restructuring provision (one-time) | 36.4 |
| **Adjusted** | **~767**, about $2.06/share |

**Sensitivities.** These move the figure in both directions.

| Adjustment | Result |
|---|---|
| Exclude the $79.7M of equity income that was booked but not received as dividends | ~$687M, about $1.85/share |
| Also use average 1985–87 capex of $353M instead of 1987's $300M | ~$634M, about $1.70/share (floor) |
| Assume maintenance capex roughly equals depreciation | close to reported net income, about $2.45/share (ceiling) |

The case for the ceiling: soft-drink capex was $170 million against soft-drink depreciation of $103 million, and the excess is described as capacity expansion and international infrastructure, which is growth spending.

**Central estimate:** about $2.00–2.10 per share for 1987. One-time items are excluded. So is the 1987 working-capital release of $273 million, which should not be treated as recurring cash.

Owner earnings sit close to reported earnings. That is what the framework expects from a business that needs little capital, unlike Burlington-type businesses whose reported profits are absorbed by reinvestment.

## Facts the framework wants that the packet lacks

1. **1988 results.** The latest audited year is 1987. 1988 earnings, owner earnings and buybacks are unknown.
2. **Split of capex into maintenance and growth.** The owner-earnings figure depends on it, and the range above ($1.70–$2.45) reflects this gap.
3. **Growth in local currencies.** Without it, I cannot separate real international business growth from the effect of a falling dollar.
4. **Current-cost (inflation-adjusted) earnings.** Management says they "would be less than reported" but gives no figure.
5. **Competitor economics.** There are no cost, margin or pricing data for the main competitor to confirm the cost advantage.
6. **Concentrate price history.** Real prices per gallon over time would show directly whether the company has pricing power.
7. **Market values of the affiliate holdings at the as-of date.** The packet only says that at end-1987 market value was "over $2.3 billion" and that publicly traded investees exceeded carrying value by $44 million.
8. **Off-balance-sheet exposures.** Guarantees total $394 million, and the company has guaranteed $325 million of CPE receivables that were sold with recourse. The packet does not say what losses, if any, these might produce.

## Carry-forward to later stages

- Underlying earning power is about $2.00–2.10 of owner earnings per share in 1987, from a franchise earning returns that few companies match.
- The affiliate holdings of about $2.26 billion should be valued separately and conservatively.
- Management's emphasis on EPS growth, its appetite for leverage, and whether its buybacks were made below intrinsic value go to Stage 3.
