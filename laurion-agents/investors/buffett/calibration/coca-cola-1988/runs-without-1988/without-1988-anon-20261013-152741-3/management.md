# Stage 3: Management and capital allocation for Company A (AAA), as of 1989-02-27

Management passes this stage. It is able, has been in place since 1981, and is clearly oriented toward shareholders in how it handles the core business. Retained earnings pass the $1-for-$1 test even after removing the market's re-rating. The 1984–85 buybacks were clearly below value. The later buybacks, the leverage policy, the past diversification and the promotional tone of the reports are cautions, not disqualifications.

## 4.1 Honest, able management already in place; candid reporting

**In place.** The chairman/CEO and the president assembled the current team in 1981, so it has had seven years of results (`ar-1987`). The CFO signs the management report in both years. Succession is not discussed.

**Able.** The results since 1981 support this:
- Earnings per share from continuing operations rose from $1.17 (1981) to $2.43 (1987), about 13% a year.
- Return on equity rose from about 20% to about 27%. Stage 2 showed part of that rise came from financial engineering, but the operating improvement is real. Soft-drink pre-tax return on identifiable assets went from about 27% in 1985 to about 34–36% in 1986–87.
- Market share rose every year in the U.S., in fountain and internationally.
- Management recognized the 1985 marketing mistake and recovered from it quickly: Brand A classic was the top U.S. soft drink by 1986.

**Candor.** The framework's standard is that owners should not receive "a public relations document" (`brk-letter-1979`). Its guideline is to tell owners the facts "we would want to know if our positions were reversed" (`brk-letter-1983#2`).

What the reports do well:
- They quantify unusual items per share in both years: +$0.68 for the 1986 gain and −$0.33 for the 1986 provisions.
- The 1987 letter strips the 1986 items out before claiming growth.
- They state where currency helped, for example international income "aided by a 16 percent strengthening of key currencies."
- They say current-cost income "would be less than reported."
- They say inflation gains on net monetary liabilities "do not represent an increase in funds available for distribution."
- They admit the film studio's 1986 performance was unsatisfactory and that Foods is "in transition" with declining earnings.
- They disclose the off-balance-sheet guarantees and the receivables sold with recourse.

What reads like a public relations document:
- **Promotional claims.** The 1987 report claims the system adds "$250 billion to the world economy" using a "multiplier factor of five", calls international potential "limitless", and includes a "value creation process" diagram.
- **Headline metrics.** Both letters lead with earnings-per-share growth and stock-price total return. Those are exactly the yardsticks the framework says are not the primary test (`brk-letter-1979`).
- **The 1985 product change.** It is described as "the effort begun in 1985 to revitalize our sugar colas." It was really a mistake the company reversed, and an owner would want it called that.
- **Repeated restatements.** Bottlers in 1986 and entertainment and Canada in 1987 were moved out of the consolidated figures. This is disclosed properly and comes with restated tables, but it changes reported margins and makes comparisons across years harder.

Verdict on candor: the material facts are disclosed accurately, wrapped in promotional prose. That is a mild negative, not a sign of dishonesty.

## 4.2 Each $1 retained must create at least $1 of market value

The framework's test is whether retention "over time, delivers shareholders at least $1 of market value for each $1 retained" (`brk-letter-1983`). I measure per share from end-1980 (just before the current team) to end-1987 (the last audited year).

| Per share, 1981–1987 | $ |
|---|---|
| Sum of net earnings per share (1.30+1.32+1.37+1.59+1.84+2.42+2.43) | 12.27 |
| Sum of cash dividends per share (.77+.83+.89+.92+.99+1.04+1.12) | (6.56) |
| In-kind CPE dividend, 1987 (carrying value) | (0.90) |
| **Retained per share** | **4.81** |
| Stock price, end-1980 → end-1987 ($11.13 → $38.13) | +27.00 |

On this basis each $1 retained produced about $5.60 of market value, measured at the post-crash end-1987 price.

**Stress test: remove the re-rating.** The price-earnings multiple rose from about 9.8x in 1980 ($11.13 ÷ $1.14) to 15.7x at end-1987. At an unchanged 9.8x, the 1987 price would have been about $23.80. That is a gain of $12.70 on $4.81 retained, still about 2.6 times. The test passes without credit for the market's mood.

**Caveats.**
- The 1986 EPS of $2.42 includes the net $0.35 of one-time items. Removing it lowers the retained figure and makes the ratio slightly better.
- 1988 retentions are unknown. 1988 dividends of $1.20 are known; 1988 earnings are not.

**Where the retained money went.** The core business uses little capital: soft-drink capex was $170 million in 1987. So most retained cash went to three places:

- **Buybacks (see 4.3).** These were the main outlet.
- **Bottlers.** CCE bought bottlers for $2.25 billion using CCE's own debt. The company also bought Miami ($325M), Rainwater ($211M) and Memphis bottlers, then resold them to CCE "at approximately carrying value." The company acted as an intermediary to restructure the bottling system rather than earning a return on these purchases. The holdings it kept earn about 5–6% (CCE: $88M of net income on $1.53B of equity). The strategic argument is that stronger bottlers sell more concentrate. That benefit is real but not measurable from the packet.
- **Diversification.**
  - *Entertainment.* The film studio was bought in 1982 (price not in the packet). Embassy and Tandem followed in 1985 for about $445 million, partly paid with 7.1 million treasury shares at roughly 13–15x earnings, and Merv Griffin in 1986 for about $200 million. Entertainment operating income grew from $121 million to $235 million. In 1987 the company combined it into CPE, spun off part to shareholders and kept 49%. Without the 1982 purchase price I cannot tell whether this earned its cost of capital.
  - *Foods.* Operating income was flat to down from 1984 to 1987: $124M, $118M, $120M, then $67M after a $36M charge.
  - These were moves away from the franchise, which the framework's "silk purses out of silk" preference warns against (`brk-letter-1987#18`).

**Recent direction is better.** Management now calls the company "first and foremost a marketer of soft drinks" (more than 95% of operating income). It moved the capital-heavy and hit-driven businesses into separately financed affiliates. It states a 12% after-tax hurdle rate and says it prefers "investments within our existing lines of business" (`ar-1987`). That is the right direction of travel.

**Dividend policy.** Management is lowering the payout ratio toward 40% "to reinvest more." This is acceptable only because the record shows retained dollars have been worth more than a dollar. Since the core business cannot absorb much more capital, the extra retention will in practice go to buybacks and bottler stakes. Price discipline on buybacks therefore becomes central.

Verdict on 4.2: passes comfortably on the aggregate record. The diversification episodes were weak uses of capital that the strength of the core business hid.

## 4.3 Buybacks only well below intrinsic value

The framework's rule is that buybacks create value only at "prices well below per-share intrinsic business value" (`brk-letter-1984#4`). The same passage says a consistent record of buybacks is evidence that management is oriented toward owners.

| Year | Shares bought (M) | Cost ($M) | Average price | P/E on that year's EPS |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1984 | 18.4 | 335.7 | ~$18.24 | ~11.6x ($1.57) |
| 1985 | 15.0 | 379.9 | ~$25.33 | ~14.7x ($1.72) |
| 1986 | 2.9 | 110.5 | ~$38.37 | ~15.9x reported; ~18.5x on ~$2.07 underlying |
| 1987 | 14.1 | 605.1 | ~$42.80 | ~17.6x ($2.43) |

From 1984 to 1987 the company repurchased about 50 million shares, roughly 12% of the 1984 count. A further 40-million-share program runs to 1990. Of that program, 6.6 million shares had been bought by end-1987 at about $42.12.

**Assessment.**
- **Owner orientation is demonstrated.** These are large repurchases, made repeatedly, through a stock-market crash. The share count fell from 396 million (1984 average) to 372 million (end-1987). This is the "demonstrated" behaviour the framework rewards (`brk-letter-1984#4`).
- **1984–85 purchases.** At 12–15x earnings for a business earning roughly 50% on its operating capital, these were almost certainly well below intrinsic value.
- **1986–87 purchases.** At about 16–18x earnings, and partly around the pre-crash peak (Q3 1987 high $53.13), whether they were "well below" value depends on the Stage 4 valuation. A rough check:
  - The 1987 earnings yield on the price paid was about 5.7%, or about 4.8% on owner earnings of roughly $2.05.
  - Add per-share growth of about 10–13% and the implied return is mid-to-high teens.
  - That is above management's own stated 12% cost of capital, so the purchases probably still added value, but by a smaller margin.
- **Management's stated reason** is that "available cash and debt capacity exceeded the funds required." That is a surplus-cash rationale, not a price-versus-value test. Management does not say it buys only when the shares are cheap.
- **Funding.** 1987 buybacks were partly funded by the $1.2 billion rise in short-term debt. Borrowing to buy back stock in a business that does not need debt is financial engineering. It is tolerable at 15.3% net debt to capital, but it is the kind of leverage the framework distrusts (`fortune-1977-inflation`; "little or no debt", `brk-letter-1982`).

## Leverage policy

At end-1986 management raised its ceiling for net debt to capital to 35%, explicitly "to reduce the weighted average cost of capital," and calls its finance policies "aggressive." Actual net leverage of 15.3% is modest, and operating cash flow of $1.2 billion covers it comfortably. This is a philosophical difference with the framework, which wants returns earned "without undue leverage" (`brk-letter-1979`). It is not a present danger. Stage 2 already showed the business earned about 20% on equity with almost no debt, so any added leverage is optional.

## Compensation and dilution

What the packet shows:
- **Options.** 5.6 million outstanding at end-1987, about 1.5% of shares. The 1987 Stock Option Plan covers 8 million shares and includes stock appreciation rights.
- **Restricted stock.** The award plan covers 3 million shares, with about 180,000–280,000 granted a year.
- **Performance units.** Cash awards based on the stock price above $20.63, on 555,000 shares.
- **Dilution.** About 1.2–1.3 million shares a year are issued to employees, against 14 million repurchased. Net dilution from compensation is roughly 0.3% a year and is more than offset by buybacks.
- **Incentives.** Pay is tied to the stock price, not to return on equity. That is consistent with management's EPS and total-return framing.
- **Share issuance for acquisitions.** 7.1 million treasury shares went into the 1985 Embassy deal. Under `brk-letter-1982#17`, shares should be issued only when owners "receive in business value as much as we give." Using shares from a business of this quality to buy a hit-driven studio business fails that standard on its face. The packet cannot settle the question.

What the packet lacks: executive salaries and bonuses, and the size of option grants to the top officers (the proxy statement is not included).

## What the investor would have relied on that the packet doesn't contain

1. **Personal assessment of the CEO and president.** The framework rests on "people we like and admire" and on high-grade, talented managers (`brk-letter-1987#18`). That judgment comes from meeting and dealing with them, which no annual report can substitute for.
2. **Insider and director ownership.** The framework values directors who "eat our own cooking" (`brk-letter-1983#0`). The board includes bankers, an investment banker (Allen & Co.), a newspaper owner and other CEOs. Their shareholdings and any business ties to the company are not disclosed here.
3. **Proxy compensation detail.** Pay levels, the bonus formula, and whether incentives reward returns on capital or per-share growth.
4. **How management decides on buybacks.** Whether it compares price to intrinsic value or simply spends surplus cash. Only direct conversation or a statement of policy would establish this.
5. **The full cost of the entertainment venture.** The 1982 studio purchase price and the cumulative cash put in, to judge whether that diversification earned its cost.
6. **1988 actions.** Buybacks under the 40-million-share program, any acquisitions, and changes in leverage since end-1987.
7. **Succession and depth.** Ages of the top two officers and the bench behind them.

## Carry-forward to Stage 4

- Treat management as **able and owner-oriented in the core business**. Retained capital has been worth well over $1 per $1, even after removing the market's re-rating.
- Buyback discipline is only demonstrated at low multiples. At today's price of about 19x 1987 earnings, the value of continued buybacks depends on the valuation.
- Cautions to weigh: a declared appetite for leverage, a history of diversifying away from the franchise (since reversed), promotional reporting, and undisclosed compensation and insider ownership. None of these is a hard negative. The judgment the framework treats as decisive, a direct assessment of the people, is not available from this packet.
