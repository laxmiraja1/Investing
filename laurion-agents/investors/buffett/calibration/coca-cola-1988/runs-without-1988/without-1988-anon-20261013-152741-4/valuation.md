# Stage 4: Valuation (Framework Section 5) and Catalyst/Timing (Section 6)

## 5a. The framework's stated valuation rule: buy below net working capital

The only rule in the framework's Valuation section is the partnership-era bargain test (`bpl-letter-1965`): buy when net working capital alone, before any value for plant, covers the price.

Year-end 1987 balance sheet (`ar-1987`), on 372.4M shares:

| | $M | Per share |
|---|---|---|
| Current assets | 4,136.2 | |
| – Current liabilities | (4,118.5) | |
| **Net working capital** | **17.7** | **$0.05** |
| Same, excluding the $335M in-kind dividend payable (settled in CPE stock, not cash) | 352.7 | $0.95 |
| Current assets – *all* liabilities (4,118.5 + 803.4 LTD + 209.9 deferred tax) | (995.6) | –$2.67 |
| Book value | 3,223.8 | $8.66 |
| **Price, 1989-02-24** | | **$46.56** |

**Result: fails decisively.** The price is about 50x the most generous working-capital figure and 5.4x book. Judged by its only stated valuation rule, the framework would say this is no bargain.

The framework's own later passages show why this test doesn't fit a company like this one:
- "Our goal is to find an outstanding business at a sensible price, not a mediocre business at a bargain price" (`brk-letter-1987` #18). The same letter says the search for bargains led to "an education" in poor businesses.
- "We may very well pay a fairly fancy price for a Category 1 business if we are reasonably confident of what we are getting" (`brk-letter-1981` #3).
- The working-capital test dates from 1965. By 1989 the framework applies it to bargains, not to franchises.

The yardstick these passages point to is in the same 1981 passage: buy "at prices that will make sense, even should the future of the acquired enterprise develop much along the lines of its past." Its input is owner earnings (Section 3). So I value on that basis. I project only by extending the company's own record, because the framework says "future projections are of little interest" (`brk-letter-1982`). This is a check of whether the price makes sense if the past repeats, not a forecast.

## 5b. Owner-earnings value: does the price make sense if the future resembles the past?

**Inputs from the packet:**
- Owner earnings for 1987 were $2.05–2.45 per share, midpoint **$2.25** (Stage 2).
- Growth record, EPS from continuing operations (`ar-1987` selected data):
  - 1977 $0.84 → 1987 $2.43: **11.2% a year over 10 years**
  - 1981 $1.17 → 1987 $2.43: **13.0% a year under current management**
  - 1984 → 1987: 15.7% a year, helped by a falling dollar (currencies rose 27% in 1986 and 16% in 1987), so I don't use it as the base.
- These rates already include the effect of buybacks, since past per-share growth did too.
- Dividend: $1.20 in 1988. Management is moving the payout ratio toward 40%.

**What the packet doesn't give me, and what I assume instead:**
- **1988 earnings are missing.** I assume 1988 owner earnings grew at the scenario rate from the 1987 base.
- **There is no long-term government bond yield in the packet.** That would be the natural opportunity cost. I use management's own stated 12% after-tax cost of capital (`ar-1986`, `ar-1987`) and show 10% as an alternative.
- **The framework sets no numeric margin-of-safety threshold.** The partnership letters ask only for a "substantial excess of value" that creates a "comfortable margin of safety" (`bpl-letter-1962` #4).

**Method.** I take ten years of dividends plus owner earnings in year 10 times an exit multiple, discounted. I use exit multiples below today's roughly 21x owner earnings, so the result doesn't depend on Mr. Market staying generous.

| Scenario | Growth in owner earnings | 1998 owner earnings per share | Exit multiple | Value at 12% | Value at 10% |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **A: past continues** (between the 10-year and post-1981 records) | 12% | $7.83 | 15x | **≈ $50** (terminal PV $37.8 + dividends PV $12.0) | **≈ $58.5** |
| B: growth slows to roughly half of the post-1981 rate | 8% | $5.25 | 13x | ≈ $32 | |
| C: international growth delivers (per-person consumption abroad is 16% of the U.S. level) | 15% | $10.48 | 18x | ≈ $75 | |

**Cross-check by splitting out the investments:**
- The stakes in the bottlers and CPE were worth over $2.3B at market at the end of 1987, about **$6.20 per share**.
- Taking them out, the market prices the concentrate and foods business at about $40.40 per share.
- Owner earnings excluding equity income are about $651M, or $1.75 per share. That makes the core about **23x owner earnings, a roughly 4.3% yield.** By the 1981 letter's wording, this is a "fairly fancy" price.

**Other yields at $46.56:**
- earnings yield on reported 1987 EPS: 5.2%
- owner-earnings yield: 4.4–5.3%
- dividend yield: 2.6%

## 5c. Margin of safety

| Test | Hurdle | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Framework rule: price below net working capital (`bpl-letter-1965`) | Price < $0.05–0.95 per share | **Fails.** Price is $46.56. |
| Sensible price if the past repeats (`brk-letter-1981` #3, `brk-letter-1987` #18), discounted at 12% | Value comfortably above price | Value ≈ $50 against $46.56. **Margin about 7%: thin.** |
| Same, discounted at 10% | | Value ≈ $58.5. **Margin about 20%.** |
| Range of scenarios | | $32–75. The price sits inside the range, slightly below Scenario A. |

**Interpretation.**
- If the past repeats, the price implies an annual return of roughly 12–13% before tax. That is about the business's own cost of capital: a fair price, not a bargain.
- The real protection is not a discount to value. It is how predictable the business is: a wide moat, little capital needed to keep it going, and buybacks that raise per-share value (Stages 2–3).
- That predictability is the condition the framework attaches to paying a "fancy price."
- The valuation is very sensitive to the discount rate: Scenario A moves from $50 at 12% to $58.5 at 10%. That sensitivity is one reason the framework distrusts projections, and why I give the most weight to Scenario A.
- The price is not "toad-like" (`brk-letter-1981` #3). The stock traded at $29–35 in October 1987 and through early 1988, where the margin against Scenario A would have been 30–40%. It now sits near the top of its 52-week range ($35.04–49.12), up about a third from the 1988 low.

**What would change this assessment:**
1. **1988 results.** If 1988 owner earnings grew at the 1987 underlying pace of 17%, the base would be about $2.63, and Scenario A at 12% rises to about $52.
2. **The government bond yield,** which sets the real opportunity cost.
3. **Growth with the currency effect removed.** If much of 1986–87 was currency translation, Scenario B deserves more weight.

## 6. Catalyst and timing

**No catalyst needed** (`brk-letter-1987`): "The market may ignore business success for a while, but eventually will confirm it," and "Mr. Market is there to serve you, not to guide you."
- This is a "general" in the framework's terms: there is no timetable, and value builds through compounding owner earnings.
- The buyback program reinforces this. Up to 40M shares (more than 10% of the company) are to be retired by 1990, and 6.6M were done by the end of 1987. Each share retired below value adds to per-share value.

**Work-out** (`bpl-letter-1957`): **not applicable.** No sale, merger, tender or liquidation has been announced. The CPE distribution was completed on 1988-01-15.

**Control** (`bpl-letter-1963`): **not applicable.** The company is worth about $17.3B, so control is out of reach. Buybacks would passively raise a large holder's percentage, but that would not amount to control.

**Timing:** the price is to be judged against value, not against forecasts of the market. The packet shows the stock near its high, which is not a sign of widespread fear about this company. Whether the general market is greedy or fearful isn't something the packet can tell me. That belongs to the risk and temperament stage.

## Stage 4 conclusion

- **On the framework's literal valuation rule, the company fails:** it is nowhere near a working-capital bargain.
- **On the owner-earnings, sensible-price standard** that the framework's own 1981 and 1987 passages set for outstanding businesses, the price is roughly fair:
  - intrinsic value about **$50 per share** (range $32–75) against **$46.56**;
  - margin of safety about **7%** at a 12% discount rate, about **20%** at 10%.
- No catalyst is required.

The case for buying depends almost entirely on the durability of the moat and the record of capital allocation, not on a discount to value. Position size, the next stage, should reflect that thin margin.
