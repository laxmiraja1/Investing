# Stage 3: Management and capital allocation (Framework Section 4)

The section has three rules:
- honest, able management already in place, reporting candidly (`brk-letter-1979`);
- buybacks only at prices well below intrinsic value (`brk-letter-1984` #4);
- every $1 retained must produce at least $1 of market value (`brk-letter-1983`).

I take the measurable test first, then buybacks, then the reinvestment record, then candor and pay.

## 4a. The $1 retained test

**Period:** the current team was assembled in 1981 (`ar-1987` letter). I use the end of 1980 as the starting point.

**Retained earnings per share, 1981–1987.** These are restated, post-split figures from `ar-1987` selected data and `ar-1986`. The 1978 dividend appears in the scan as ".98", which is an OCR error, and doesn't affect this period.

| | 1981 | 1982 | 1983 | 1984 | 1985 | 1986 | 1987 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Net income per share | 1.30 | 1.32 | 1.37 | 1.59 | 1.84 | 2.42 | 2.43 |
| Cash dividend | .77 | .83 | .89 | .92 | .99 | 1.04 | 1.12 |
| Retained | .53 | .49 | .48 | .67 | .85 | 1.38 | 1.31 |

- Total retained over the period is about **$5.71 per share**.
- About **$4.81** if the $0.90 in-kind distribution of entertainment stock in 1987 is counted as a payout. That is the right treatment: it handed back capital previously retained in the entertainment business.

**Market value created:**
- The share price went from $11.13 (end 1980) to $38.13 (end 1987), a gain of **$27.00 per share**. On top of that, holders received the entertainment shares.
- Today's price is $46.56, a gain of **$35.43** from the starting point. The 1988 retained amount is unknown because 1988 earnings aren't in the packet. Assuming roughly $1.50 retained in 1988, total retained would be about $6.30–7.20.

| | Market value created | $ retained | Market value per $1 retained |
|---|---|---|---|
| End 1980 → end 1987 | $27.00 | $4.81–5.71 | **≈ $5** |
| End 1980 → 1989-02-24 (1988 retained estimated) | $35.43 | ≈ $6.30–7.20 | **≈ $5** |

**Caveat:** much of the gain comes from a higher valuation multiple. The price/earnings ratio rose from about 9.8x 1980 earnings to 19.2x 1987 earnings.

**Testing on earnings alone, without the rerating:**
- EPS rose $1.29, from $1.14 in 1980 to $2.43 in 1987, against about $5.71 retained.
- That is roughly a 22% return on the retained dollars. This is crude: buybacks and debt also contribute.

**Verdict: passes by a wide margin,** whether measured by market value or by earnings.

## 4b. Buybacks

**Repurchase history** (from the shareholders' equity statements):

| Year | Shares bought | Cost | Average price | Approx. P/E paid* |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1984 | 18.4M | $335.7M | $18.24 | ~11.6x |
| 1985 | 15.0M | $379.9M | $25.33 | ~14.7x |
| 1986 | 2.9M | $110.5M | $38.37 | ~18.5x (on $2.07 underlying) |
| 1987 | 14.1M | $605.1M | $42.80 | ~17.6x |
| **Total** | **≈50.4M** | **≈$1.43B** | **≈$28.40** | |

\*Price divided by that year's EPS from continuing operations. For 1986 I use the $2.07 underlying figure.

**Scale:** about 12% of the share count was retired. Average shares were 408M in 1983, and 372M were outstanding at the end of 1987. A further program covers up to 40M more shares (over 10% of the company) by 1990. By the end of 1987, 6.6M of those had been bought at about $42.12 each.

**Against the framework's two benefits** (`brk-letter-1984` #4):
- **The arithmetic benefit.** The 1984–85 purchases, at 12–15x earnings for a business earning about 22–25% on equity, were almost certainly well below intrinsic value. Every tranche cost less than today's $46.56.
  - The 1986–87 purchases, at about 17–18x earnings, are less obviously "well below" value.
  - At $42.80, the owner-earnings yield was about 5.3% (Stage 2 midpoint $2.25). That works only if growth is strong. Stage 4 has to settle whether value was well above $42.80.
- **The demonstrated benefit.** The framework stresses that the key word is "demonstrated." Management has bought steadily and heavily for four years, so this is shown in actions, not words.

**A caution on motive.** The stated trigger is not price against value. Repurchases "commenced after management determined that the Company's available cash and debt capacity exceeded the funds required" (`ar-1986`, `ar-1987`). Part of the 1987 buying was funded by short-term debt. So the buyback is partly a way to use up excess cash, and only implicitly a judgment about price.

**A partial offset.**
- In 1985 the company issued about 7.1M treasury shares, at roughly $24 each, to buy Embassy, a television production company. In the same year it was buying back stock at about $25.
- The 1982 letter allows issuing shares only when "our owners receive in business value as much as we give" (`brk-letter-1982` #17). Paying in a very high-return franchise's stock for an entertainment business doesn't clearly meet that standard.
- The amount is small, about 1.7% of shares, and was swamped by the repurchases.

## 4c. Reinvestment and acquisition record

**1. Diversification, 1982–86: a weak spot.**
- The company bought the film studio in 1982; the price isn't in the packet. It then added Embassy and Tandem in 1985 (about $445M) and Merv Griffin in 1986 ($200M).
- On 1986 figures, entertainment earned $235M of operating income on $2.17B of assets, about 11% pretax. The core soft-drink business earned about 34–36%.
- This was a move away from simple, high-return businesses, the type the framework prefers (`brk-letter-1982`).

**2. The reversal: a point in management's favour.**
- In 1987 management combined entertainment with Tri-Star (the second film studio) into CPE and distributed 51% of it to shareholders. Coca-Cola keeps a 49% stake carried at $989M.
- CPE earned about 4.4% on net assets in 1987.
- The company also sold off unrelated low-return operations: Presto/Winkler plastics in 1985 for $112M and Ronco pasta in 1984.
- Management now says it prefers "investments within our existing lines of business" (`ar-1987`).
- Recognising mistakes and undoing them is the behaviour the framework respects. The entertainment capital, however, has earned far less than it would have in soft drinks.

**3. Bottler investments: strategically defensible, low return on their own.**
- The company assembled about $2.25B of bottlers inside CCE, funded with CCE's own debt, then sold 51% to the public at a $375M gain.
- Since then it has bought further stakes: Consolidated ($63M for 20%), Johnston ($54M for 21%), and the UK joint venture. It also bought and resold the Miami, Rainwater and Memphis bottlers at about carrying value.
- The 49% CCE stake earned about 5.8% in 1987 ($43M on $749M carrying value).
- The rationale is to strengthen the system that sells the concentrate, so the real return shows up in concentrate volume and pricing. That can't be verified from the packet.
- The structure keeps capital-heavy, debt-funded bottling off the concentrate company's balance sheet. It reduces reported risk without removing the economic risk.

**4. Foods.**
- Foods took $114M of capital spending in 1985 and earned about 7% margins, followed by a 1987 restructuring and a management change.
- This is a small amount of capital that hasn't paid off.

**5. Leverage policy: a yellow flag.**
- In 1986 management raised its target ratio of net debt to capital to 35%, in order to lower the weighted cost of capital and "increase total return." The stated after-tax cost of capital is about 12%.
- The ratio is 15.3% today, but rising.
- The framework wants high returns "without undue leverage" and businesses "employing little or no debt." It also notes that highly profitable companies "require relatively little debt capital" (`fortune-1977-inflation` #6).
- Company A doesn't need this debt. Choosing to add it is a matter of financial engineering, not operating necessity.
- There are also off-balance-sheet obligations:
  - $394M of guarantees;
  - $325M of CPE receivables sold with recourse, which CPE has agreed to indemnify;
  - about $300M of extra debt once the finance subsidiary (CCFC) is consolidated in 1988.

**6. Dividend policy.**
- Management is lowering the payout toward 40% of earnings to retain more. The cash payout was 46% in 1987.
- The core business can use only about $170M a year of capital, so the extra retention will go to buybacks and bottler stakes.
- Given the record in 4a, this is acceptable. Whether it stays acceptable depends on buyback prices and the returns on bottler investments.

## 4d. Candor and compensation

**In favour of candor:**
- One-off items are clearly quantified: a net $0.35 per share in 1986, $180M of provisions with their breakdown, and the gain broken out.
- The 1987 letter states growth "excluding 35 cents per share of unusual items in 1986."
- Currency effects are explained, with tables for each currency.
- The report admits dissatisfaction with film-studio results and a leadership change in 1986, and calls Foods "obviously in transition" in 1987.
- The company publishes its cost of capital and debt targets.

**Against:**
- The reports are glossy promotional documents, which the framework explicitly says it doesn't want (`brk-letter-1979`). Examples:
  - a "$250 billion to the world economy" claim built on a multiplier;
  - "The potential appears limitless";
  - headline emphasis on the stock's total return (38% and 40%) rather than per-share business value.
- The 28.8% ROE headline for 1986 includes the bottler gain without adjustment.
- The 1985 formula change is described only as the "new taste," which "stabilized."
- The value-creation vocabulary needs the 1984 letter's warning about "maximizing shareholder wealth (this season's favorite)." Here, unlike in that warning, the actions back up the words.

**Compensation and dilution** (the packet shows structure only):
- 5.6M options outstanding, about 1.5% of shares;
- 8M shares authorised under the 1987 option plan, plus restricted stock and stock appreciation rights;
- cash awards tied to the stock price on 555,000 shares, measured from a $20.63 base.
- Dilution from employee issuance is about 1.2–1.3M shares a year, roughly 0.35%, small against the buybacks.
- Pay is linked to the share price, not to returns on capital. Amounts, salaries and bonuses aren't given.

## What the investor would have relied on that the packet lacks

1. **Direct personal judgment of the people.** The framework's management verdicts rest on knowing the managers ("tops in both ability and integrity," `brk-letter-1984` #31; `brk-letter-1987` #18). The packet doesn't name the CEO and has no record of any meeting or acquaintance.
2. **Insider and director ownership.** The framework values directors who "eat our own cooking" (`brk-letter-1983` #0). The board is listed, but holdings aren't.
3. **Proxy compensation detail:** salaries, bonuses, and how the performance-unit agreements are triggered.
4. **The 1988 record:** shares actually repurchased under the 40M program and the prices paid, debt levels, and any new acquisitions.
5. **The price paid for the film studio in 1982,** and the market value of the CPE stake now. Both are needed to score the full entertainment round trip.
6. **Bottler-side evidence** that the CCE and joint-venture stakes improved concentrate economics.
7. **Management's internal yardstick for buybacks,** that is, whether price is judged against value at all.

## Stage 3 verdict

Management **passes**, with reservations.

**Strengths:**
- The $1 retained test passes at about $5 of market value per $1 retained.
- Buybacks have been large and repeated, mostly at prices clearly below what the business now appears to be worth.
- The entertainment diversification was recognised and largely unwound, and unrelated businesses were sold.
- Reporting quantifies one-off items and admits weak spots.

**Reservations to carry into valuation and sizing:**
1. The 1982–86 diversification wasted some capital, and the 1985 share issue for Embassy was at a questionable exchange.
2. The 35% debt target is leverage the business doesn't need.
3. Retained cash increasingly goes to bottler stakes that earn about 6% on their own.
4. Recent buybacks at about 17–18x earnings are justified by "excess cash" rather than an explicit test of price against value.
5. The reports are promotional in tone.

None of these is disqualifying. The judgment the framework relies on most, firsthand confidence in the managers' character, can't be made from this packet.
