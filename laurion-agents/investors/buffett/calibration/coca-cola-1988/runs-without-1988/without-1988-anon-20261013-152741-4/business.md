# Stage 2: Business analysis (Framework Section 3)

The framework names three measures, applied in this order: return on equity, the durable competitive advantage, and owner earnings.

## 3a. Return on equity, not EPS growth, is the primary test

The rule (`brk-letter-1979`) asks for "a high earnings rate on equity capital employed (without undue leverage, accounting gimmickry, etc.)", not steady per-share gains. I check that standard first and then test the two exclusions it names.

**Reported ROE on average equity (`ar-1987` selected data):**

| 1977 | 1979 | 1981 | 1983 | 1984 | 1985 | 1986 | 1987 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 20.6% | 21.1% | 19.9% | 19.4% | 21.8% | 23.5% | 28.8% | 27.2% |

**Check 1: accounting one-offs.**
- 1986 includes a $375M gain on the CCE stock sale (+$0.68 per share) and $180M of provisions (–$0.33). Net, that adds $0.35. Underlying 1986 EPS was about $2.07.
- 1987 includes a $40M gain on the Canadian bottler (TCC) and a $36M Foods provision. These roughly cancel, so underlying 1987 EPS is about $2.43, up roughly 17%.
- On that basis, the 1986 underlying ROE was about 25%, not 28.8%.
- Goodwill amortization is trivial ($1.6M). Inventory is partly on LIFO, with a $19M reserve. The U.S. pension plan holds more assets than it owes ($454M against a $399M projected obligation).
- I found no sign of aggressive accounting, though see the equity-income point under 3c.

**Check 2: leverage.**
- Gross debt rose from $1.14B in 1985 to $2.70B in 1987 (restated). Treasury stock rose to $1.31B, and the in-kind dividend of $335M also reduced equity. All of this shrinks the denominator, so part of the rise in ROE since 1983 comes from financial structure.
- Debt was only 3.5% of capital in 1977, so the ~20% ROE of that era was earned almost without leverage.
- To strip out leverage for 1987:
  - Net debt is $2,702M less $1,268M of excess cash, the $545M CPE note repaid in January 1988, and $308M of temporary investments sold that month. That leaves about $581M.
  - Net interest was $279M expense less $207M income, or about $72M pretax, about $47M after tax.
  - (916 + 47) ÷ (3,224 + 581) ≈ **25% return on net capital employed.**
- The returns are therefore not a product of leverage. The rising use of debt remains a capital-allocation question for Stage 3.

**Check 3: the core business by itself.** Soft-drink segment figures (`ar-1987` Note 16):

| | 1986 (before provisions) | 1987 |
|---|---|---|
| Operating income, USA + International | $1,181M | $1,433M |
| Identifiable assets | $3,287M | $4,174M |
| Pretax return on identifiable assets | ~36% | ~34% |
| International operating margin | | 27% |
| U.S. operating margin | | 15% |
| Foods margin (before its $36M provision) | | ~7% |

- Some of the 1987 jump in U.S. assets may be the $308M temporary bottler investment. The packet doesn't say which segment holds it. If it sits in U.S. assets, the return on core assets is higher than shown.

**Benchmark.** The 1987 letter notes that only six of Fortune's 1,000 largest companies averaged over 30% ROE across a decade, with Commerce Clearing House the best at 40.2% (`brk-letter-1987` #1). Company A's ten-year average of about 21–22% falls short of that group. Its current run rate of about 25–27% unleveraged sits near the top of large American industry, and it is improving.

**EPS growth, given second place by the framework.** Average shares fell from 396M (1984) to 377M (1987), so per-share growth is helped by buybacks. That isn't a fault, but it's why the framework looks at ROE first.

**Verdict on 3a: passes.** The business earns a high return on its own capital without relying on leverage.

## 3b. Durable competitive advantage (the moat)

The GEICO passage (`brk-letter-1986` #16) asks for an advantage competitors can't easily copy, and whether management is widening it. GEICO's advantage was cost. Company A's is a trademark joined to a distribution system and low production cost.

**Evidence the moat exists, from the packet:**
- **Share.** Company A has about 44% of world soft-drink volume outside the USSR and China, and its 1987 volume was double that of the nearest competitor (`ar-1987`). In the U.S. it holds over 39% of the market, about 9 points ahead of its main rival. It has 59% of the fountain segment and 49% of vending (`ar-1986`).
- **Taking most of the growth.** The company won more than 60% of U.S. market growth in 1986, and about three-quarters of fountain growth over four years. Its growth in chain restaurants was more than triple the rival's (`ar-1986`, `ar-1987`).
- **Margins.** U.S. concentrate gross margin was about 63% (`ar-1986`), and the consolidated gross margin is 52.6%.
- **Pricing power.**
  - New contracts with CCE let the company set prices at its discretion, and it used that right in October 1986.
  - 1987 operating income benefited from price increases. Management says it can "adjust prices to compensate for increasing costs."
  - The 1986 letter argues that a strong, unregulated franchise keeps the benefit of a corporate tax cut rather than passing it on (`brk-letter-1986` #22). By that test, Company A should keep the U.S. rate cut to 34%. The effect is modest, because only about $356M of 1987 pretax income was U.S.-sourced.
- **Low capital needs.**
  - Core soft-drink capital spending was $170M in 1987 against $103M of depreciation.
  - Pretax cash conversion was about 95%: (1,433 + 103 – 170) ÷ 1,433.
  - The 1977 inflation article says highly profitable companies "require relatively little debt capital" while laggards never get enough (`fortune-1977-inflation` #6). The packet shows this split within Company A's own system:
    - Company A has moved the capital-heavy bottling into affiliates it owns 49% of.
    - CCE earned $88M on $1.53B of equity in 1987, about 6%, while carrying $2.7B of liabilities.
    - The concentrate company keeps the high-return part of the system.
- **Durability of the product.** It is a 100-year-old product with little change. The 1987 letter says "Severe change and exceptional returns usually don't mix" (`brk-letter-1987` #3).
- **Room to grow.** International consumption per person was 16% of the U.S. level in 1986. U.S. consumption per person rose from 203 servings in 1980 to 274 in 1987. International markets now supply 65% of volume and about 77% of soft-drink operating income.

**Is management widening it?**
- It is restructuring bottlers into larger, more efficient units (Germany went from 102 bottlers to 68).
- It is switching from syrup to concentrate, which cuts freight costs.
- It is expanding fountain sales abroad, up 18% in 1987.

These are the kind of moat-widening steps the GEICO passage praises.

**Things that weaken or obscure the moat:**
1. **Currency flattered the results.** Key currencies rose 27% against the dollar in 1986 and 16% in 1987. International operating income grew 25% in 1987, and much of that is translation. Currency-neutral growth is probably low double digits or less, but the packet doesn't give that figure.
2. **The business depends on heavy marketing.** Media spending was $720M in 1987, and total selling expenses were $2.11B, about 28% of revenue. The packet can't tell me whether the brand would hold if that spending were cut.
3. **The 1985 reformulation.** The company changed the flagship formula and then reintroduced the original as "classic." The brand recovered to record share, which is evidence of how durable it is. The episode also showed management can misjudge its own franchise.
4. **Price limits in U.S. bottler contracts.** Most standard U.S. contracts with other bottlers use formula pricing for the flagship brand (`ar-1986`). Pricing power is not unlimited in the U.S.
5. **The Foods business is a weak sideline.** It is competitive, low-margin, and its profit fell from $120M to $67M after its provision.

**Verdict on 3b: passes.** This is a wide, demonstrable moat. Competitors' costs and capital needs can't match Company A's, and the trademark holds up even through management's own mistakes.

## 3c. Owner earnings

The framework defines owner earnings (`brk-letter-1986`) as reported earnings plus depreciation, amortization and other non-cash charges, less the average annual capital spending needed to maintain the business. For 1987:

| | $M |
|---|---|
| Net income | 916.1 |
| + Depreciation | 151.9 |
| + Goodwill amortization | 1.6 |
| – Capital expenditures (1987) | (299.6) |
| **Owner earnings, using total 1987 capex** | **≈ 770** |

Per share, on 372.4M shares at year-end:

| Version | Calculation | Per share |
|---|---|---|
| Using total 1987 capex | as above | ≈ **$2.07** |
| Using average 1985–87 capex of $352M | 916 + 154 – 352 | ≈ $1.93 |
| If maintenance capex ≈ depreciation (rest is growth spending, e.g. bottling infrastructure abroad, a headquarters building) | 916 + 154 – 152 | ≈ $2.46 |
| Excluding undistributed affiliate earnings | first row less $80M | ≈ $1.85 |

**Adjustments the formula raises:**
- **Equity income.** $118.5M of 1987 earnings is equity income, of which only about $39M arrived as cash dividends. The rest, $79.7M, is undistributed (`ar-1987`, statement of changes).
  - The framework says undistributed earnings of investees count if they produce full value (`brk-letter-1981` #3).
  - CCE earns about 6% on its equity, so I wouldn't count its retained earnings at full value.
  - CPE (entertainment) is outside the core and outside the simple-business preference.
  - I therefore discount this portion. That gives the $1.85 row above.
- **Deferred taxes** were –$28.7M in 1987 but +$42M and +$61M in the two prior years. They roughly wash out over the period, so I make no adjustment.
- **Working capital** fell by $273M in 1987. That is a one-time cash release, and I exclude it.

**Working range for valuation:** about **$2.05–2.45 per share** on the 1987 base. The midpoint is about $2.25, which is a little below reported EPS of $2.43. The core concentrate business needs little reinvestment. The gap between reported earnings and owner earnings comes mainly from growth spending and from affiliate earnings that aren't paid out, not from upkeep of the business.

## Facts the framework wants that the packet does not contain

1. **1988 results.** No full-year or interim figures are available. The 1988 dividend of $1.20 per share and the stated 40% payout target suggest growth continued, but that is inference. Stage 4 will have to value off the 1987 base.
2. **How capital spending splits between maintenance and growth.** This is the main uncertainty in owner earnings. I'd assume maintenance is close to depreciation for the core.
3. **Currency-neutral growth in volume, price and profit** by country, to separate real progress from translation gains.
4. **Concentrate gross margin abroad.** Only the U.S. figure (about 63%) is given.
5. **Competitors' figures.** All share claims come from the company. I'd want the main rival's margins and share trends.
6. **Bottler economics and contracts.** I'd want CCE's dividends, the terms and expiry of the formula-pricing contracts, and the full look-through earnings of the bottlers.
7. **Current market value of the affiliate stakes.** The 1987 report gives only a $44M total excess over book value, plus a $2.3B value for the investments.
8. **Longer segment history** for the soft-drink business alone before 1985, which would show whether its returns have held over a full decade.
9. **Brand evidence that doesn't come from management's claims:** how sales respond when advertising spending changes.
10. **Management's own stock ownership and pay structure.** Only option and restricted-stock totals are given. This belongs to Stage 3.

## Stage 2 conclusion

On all three measures this is an outstanding business with favourable long-term economics, which is what the framework looks for (`brk-letter-1987` #18).
- Unleveraged returns on capital are about 25%, and returns on core assets are in the mid-30s pretax.
- It has a wide moat that management is widening.
- Owner earnings are about $2.05–2.45 per share, and the core concentrate business needs little reinvestment.

Two questions remain open:
- **Capital allocation (Stage 3):** the rising leverage, the buybacks, and the past diversification into entertainment, now spun off.
- **Price (Stage 4):** at $46.56 the stock trades at about 19x reported EPS and about 21x the midpoint of owner earnings.
