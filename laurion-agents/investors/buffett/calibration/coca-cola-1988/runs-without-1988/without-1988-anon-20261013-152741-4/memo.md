# Company A: Recommendation to Buy, Sized at About 20% of the Portfolio

Company A sells soft-drink syrup and concentrate to independent and part-owned bottlers in more than 155 countries, under what its own report calls the world's best-known trademark. In 1987 its brands accounted for about 44% of soft drinks sold outside the Soviet Union and China, twice the volume of the nearest competitor. In the United States it holds more than 39% of the market and 59% of fountain sales. Soft drinks produce more than 95% of operating income. The company also owns a modest juice business and 49% stakes in its largest bottlers and in a spun-off entertainment company. We are recommending a purchase at the recent price of $46.56, building to roughly a fifth of the portfolio.

The company passes our gatekeeping tests without difficulty. It is not a turnaround: earnings per share have risen every year since 1976, and the dividend has been raised for 26 straight years. The product is simple, has changed little in a century, and involves no technology we cannot follow. It also clears our size threshold by a wide margin, with 1987 net income of $916 million and a market value of about $17 billion. The core business does not need borrowed money. Management has chosen to use some, which we return to below.

The business analysis is where the case is strongest. Measured by our primary test, return on equity employed, the company earned 27% on average equity in 1987. Because some of that figure reflects buybacks and debt, we also stripped out net borrowing. On that basis the return on capital was still about 25%. The soft-drink segments earned roughly 35% pretax on their identifiable assets, and the company earned about 20% on equity in the late 1970s with almost no debt at all.

The competitive advantage rests on three things working together: the trademark, a global bottling system, and low production cost. Concentrate sold in the United States carried a gross margin of about 63%, and management can set prices on its largest bottler contract at its discretion. The core business spent only $170 million on plant in 1987 against $1.4 billion of segment operating income. As a result, owner earnings (reported earnings plus depreciation, less capital spending) were about $2.05 to $2.45 a share, close to reported earnings of $2.43. There is also a long runway: consumption per person outside the United States is about one-sixth of the American level.

Management's record has flaws. From 1982 to 1986 it put capital into film and television, where returns were a fraction of those in soft drinks, and in 1985 it issued shares to buy a production company. It has since reversed course. Entertainment was combined with a second studio and 51% distributed to shareholders, and unrelated plastics and pasta businesses were sold. The more important evidence is what retained earnings have produced. Since the current team took over in 1981, each dollar kept in the business has been matched by about five dollars of market value. Earnings growth alone implies a return of about 22% on the retained capital. Management has also retired about 12% of the shares since 1984 at an average of $28, well below today's price, and has authority to buy up to 40 million more.

Our reservations are real. Management has set a target of 35% net debt to capital, which is leverage this business does not need. More of the retained cash is going into bottler stakes that earn about 6% on their own. The annual reports are promotional in tone. We also have no first-hand knowledge of the people running the company.

On value, the company is not a bargain by our old standard. It trades at 5.4 times book value, and its net working capital is almost nil. For a business of this quality, the relevant question is whether the price makes sense if the future looks broadly like the past. We assumed owner earnings grow 12% a year, between the ten-year record of 11% and the 13% achieved since 1981. We counted ten years of dividends, applied an exit multiple below today's, and discounted at management's own 12% cost of capital. That gives about $50 a share; a 10% rate gives about $58. Slower growth of 8% would justify only about $32, while 15% growth driven by international markets would support about $75.

At $46.56, the margin of safety is therefore thin, roughly 7% to 20% depending on the discount rate. What protects us is the predictability of the business rather than a discount in the price. That combination is why we are sizing the position at about half of the 40% we would commit when both the facts and the price are compelling. We would buy steadily below about $50 and more aggressively if the price returned to the $35 to $40 range seen in 1988. No catalyst is required: compounding earnings and continued buybacks should, over time, carry the share price with them.

We intend to hold indefinitely. A higher share price would not cause us to sell. Several developments would make us reconsider. The first is 1988 results showing that recent growth was mostly currency translation, since the dollar's fall flattered 1986 and 1987. Others are a sustained loss of share or pricing power, unleveraged returns falling persistently below about 20%, or debt pushed beyond management's own target. A return to large acquisitions outside soft drinks, especially paid for in stock, would also count, as would any evidence that management's reporting cannot be trusted.
