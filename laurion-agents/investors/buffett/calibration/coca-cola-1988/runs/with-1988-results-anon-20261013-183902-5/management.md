# Stage 3: Management and capital allocation (§4)

The section has three rules, taken in order: management in place and candid, buybacks only well below value, and the $1-retained test. Acquisitions, share issuance and compensation fall under the capital-allocation rules, so I treat them there.

---

## §4.1 Honest, able management in place; candid reporting (`brk-letter-1979`, `brk-letter-1983#2`)

**In place and able.** The chairman, the president and the senior operating heads have run the company as one team since 1981 (`ar-1987`: "When we assembled our present management team in 1981…"). The results under them:

- ROE rose from about 22% in 1981 to 33% in 1988. Stripped of leverage, it is still about 23–25% (Stage 2).
- World share rose from under 38% to nearly 45% (`ar-1988`).
- International soft-drink margin rose from 22.9% (1985) to 29.7% (1988).
- Soft drinks went from the largest of four sectors to more than 90% of operating income (`ar-1988`).
- Non-core businesses were sold: plastics (1985), pasta (1984), coffee (January 1989).
- Entertainment and bottling were moved into 49%-owned affiliates (`ar-1986`, `ar-1987`, `ar-1988`).

This is the record of operators who know where their franchise is, which is the "define with extraordinary realism their area of special competence" quality praised in `brk-letter-1984#7`.

**Candor: evidence for.**
- **One-offs disclosed.** The 1986 letter says EPS was "enhanced by approximately 35 cents per share" by the bottling-IPO gain net of provisions (`ar-1986`). The 1987 letter recomputes growth excluding unusual items: net income up 14.5%, not the reported −1.9% (`ar-1987`).
- **Weaknesses named.** "We were not satisfied with the performance of the film studio's feature films" (`ar-1986`). "Our foods business is obviously in transition" (`ar-1987`). The foods restructuring is tied to "a change in senior management" (`ar-1987`, Note 13).
- **Adverse items flagged.** The reports show currency effects by country and disclose that adopting FASB 96 could cut equity by about $175M (`ar-1988`).
- **Restatements explained.** Each deconsolidation comes with restated prior years and an auditor consistency note.

**Candor: evidence against.** Parts of the reports read as the "public relations document" that `brk-letter-1979` says owners should not receive:
- "The potential appears limitless" (`ar-1986`).
- A "multiplier factor of five" claiming the system adds "$250 billion to the world economy" (`ar-1987`).
- Pages of customer anecdotes (`ar-1988`).
- The 1985 change to the flagship formula is described only in neutral or positive terms. No report calls it a mistake.
- The repeated restatements are disclosed, but they make the trend hard to follow without reconstruction.

**Verdict:** honest in substance on the numbers that matter, and promotional in tone. The tone is a mild negative, not a disqualifier.

## §4.2 Buybacks only well below intrinsic value (`brk-letter-1984#4`)

Average prices paid, computed from the treasury-stock lines (`ar-1986`, `ar-1987`, `ar-1988`):

| Year | Shares bought | Cost | Average price | Year-end price |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1984 | 18.4M | $335.7M | ≈ $18.24 | $20.79 |
| 1985 | 15.0M | $379.9M | ≈ $25.33 | $28.17 |
| 1986 | 2.9M | $110.5M | ≈ $38.37 | $37.75 |
| 1987 | 14.2M | $605.1M | ≈ $42.70 | $38.13 |
| 1988 | 19.0M | $758.2M | ≈ $39.94 | $44.63 |
| **Total** | **69.4M** | **$2,189M** | **≈ $31.55** | |

**What the record shows.**
- Average shares fell from 408M (1983) to 364.6M (1988), and to 355M at year-end.
- The 1984–85 repurchases, at roughly 11–14x earnings, were plainly made well below what the business was worth. They are the "demonstrated" pro-shareholder action that `brk-letter-1984#4` says lets the market trust management.
- The 1987–88 purchases are less clear. At about $40–43 they cost roughly 14–18x owner earnings of about $2.40–2.90 (Stage 2), an initial yield of about 6–7% that grows. Whether that is "well below" intrinsic value can only be settled in the valuation stage.
- The 1987 average of $42.70 was paid in a year with a high of $53 and a crash low of $29 (`ar-1987`). The packet does not show whether buying was concentrated at the low.

**Management's stated rationale has improved.**
- Earlier, buybacks began "after management determined that… available cash and debt capacity would exceed the funds required" (`ar-1986`). That is a surplus-cash test, not a price-versus-value test.
- The 1988 report says buying will continue "as long as investing in our own stock remains one of the best uses of our excess cash" (`ar-1988`). That is a comparison of returns, closer to the framework.
- Neither statement ties purchases explicitly to price relative to value.

**Funding.** Part of the buybacks was paid for with debt. Debt to capital reached 48.4% at end-1987, and the net-debt ceiling was raised to 35% in 1986. Preferred stock of $300M was issued in 1988. This is borrowing by choice, not need, and debt came back down to 38.8% by end-1988. The framework's preference for "little or no debt" (`brk-letter-1982`) makes this a yellow flag.

**Earlier share issuance.** Average shares rose from 372M (1981) to 408M (1983). That points to about 36M shares, roughly 10%, issued around the June 1982 film-studio purchase (`ar-1986` notes that purchase only as "a purchase transaction"). Embassy was also bought in 1985 with 7.1M treasury shares (`ar-1986`, Note 12). By the standard in `brk-letter-1982#17` (issue shares only when you receive as much value as you give), stock sold at about 10x earnings for entertainment assets was probably expensive currency. Later buybacks at higher multiples partly paid for undoing it. The packet does not give the 1982 terms, so this is an inference, not a finding.

## §4.3 Each $1 retained should create at least $1 of market value (`brk-letter-1983`)

**Per-share test, 1981–1988.** The period starts with the arrival of the current management.
- Earnings retained per share: the sum of (net income per share − cash and in-kind dividends per share) is about **$6.46**. The $0.90 entertainment in-kind dividend in 1987 is counted as paid out.
- Market value per share went from $11.13 (end-1980) to $44.63 (end-1988), an increase of **$33.50**.
- **Ratio: about $5.20 of market value per $1 retained.**

**Aggregate cross-check, 1984–1988.**
- Reinvested earnings rose $1.89B, from $2,494M to $4,385M.
- Market value rose from about $7.3B to $15.8B, an increase of about $8.5B, after spending $2.19B on buybacks.
- Adding back the buybacks gives roughly $5.6 of value per $1 retained.

**Caveat.** Part of the gain is a higher multiple: about 9.8x earnings at end-1980 against 15.7x at end-1988. On earnings alone, EPS rose $1.71 on $6.46 retained, about a **26% incremental return**. That fits Stage 2's leverage-stripped ROE of 23–25%, so the test passes on business results, not only on market sentiment. The test also blends leverage, buybacks and a weak dollar into one figure.

**Where retained capital earned less:**
- **Entertainment (1982–87).** The 1985 Embassy and Tandem acquisitions cost about $445M, and Merv Griffin cost about $200M (`ar-1986`). The whole sector then went into a 49% stake in the combined entertainment affiliate. That stake took a $114M write-down within ten weeks of the combination (`ar-1988`, Note 3) and is carried at $598M. The packet gives no full return on this detour.
- **Affiliate stakes, about $1.9B.** They earned 2.5–6% on book in 1986–88 (Stage 2). Publicly traded investees are worth about $291M more than carrying value (`ar-1988`), so on market value they pass the $1 test. On earnings they so far do not.
- **Foods.** Returns on assets fell from 20% to 13%.

These blemishes were funded by a business that generated about $1.2B of operating cash a year. Management has since reversed most of them, which counts in its favor.

- The 1988 jump in option grants.

## What the investor would have relied on that I lack

1. **Direct judgment of the people.** `brk-letter-1987#18` says Berkshire buys only businesses run by "high-grade, talented and likeable managers," which is judged by knowing them. I cannot assess character, rationality or temperament beyond the documents.
2. **Insider and director ownership.** `brk-letter-1983#0` ("We eat our own cooking") makes this important, but it is not given. The board is mostly outsiders, including bankers whose institutions may lend to the company.
3. **Monthly buyback prices.** Needed to judge whether management buys more when the price is low, for example in October 1987.
4. **Terms and returns of the 1982 film-studio deal.** Needed to settle whether the share issuance destroyed value.
5. **Management's stated limits on future diversification.** The question is whether, with debt capacity of about $727M (`ar-1988`), they would stay in their own lines of business, as `ar-1987` says they prefer.
6. **Long-term interest rates.** Needed to judge whether buybacks at about 6–7% owner-earnings yields are "well below" value.

## Verdict on §4

**Passes, with reservations.** This is an able, continuous management team that has shown owner orientation: large buybacks at clearly favorable early prices, more than $5 of market value per $1 retained, and a hard refocus on the franchise after the entertainment detour.

The reservations are:
- The early-1980s share issuance and the entertainment diversification.
- A taste for borrowing to boost returns.
- Buybacks justified by surplus cash, not explicitly by value.
- A promotional reporting tone.

None of these is a reason to stop. The decisive remaining question is price, and whether the current $46.56, like the 1987–88 buybacks at about $40, sits well below intrinsic value. That goes to Stage 4 (valuation).
