# Stage 2: Business analysis (§3)

The three tests in §3 are taken in the framework's order: return on equity, then the moat, then owner earnings.

---

## §3.1 Return on equity, not EPS growth (`brk-letter-1979`)

The rule's test is "a high earnings rate on equity capital employed (without undue leverage, accounting gimmickry, etc.)." EPS growth was strong (about 13–14% a year from 1981 to 1988, `ar-1988` selected data), but under this rule it is not the measure. The questions are what equity earns, and how much of that comes from leverage or accounting.

**Reported record** (`ar-1988` selected data; net income to average common equity):

| Year | ROE | Total debt / total capital |
|---|---|---|
| 1978 | 22.6% | 3.8% |
| 1979 | 23.0% | 6.3% |
| 1980 | 21.1% | 9.3% |
| 1981 | 22.2% | 9.1% |
| 1982 | 20.3% | 15.1% |
| 1983 | 19.6% | 15.2% |
| 1984 | 22.2% | 32.3% |
| 1985 | 25.3% | 30.3% |
| 1986 | 29.1% | 34.7% |
| 1987 | 27.5% | 48.4% |
| 1988 | 33.3% | 38.8% |

The decade average for 1979–88 is about 24%. For comparison, `brk-letter-1987#1` notes that only six of Fortune's 1,000 largest companies averaged above 30% over the prior decade. The company is not in that group on its ten-year average, but it is well above ordinary.

**Gimmickry check: the one-off items.**
- **1986.** Reported earnings include a $375M pretax gain ($262M after tax) from the bottling affiliate's IPO, offset by $180M of provisions ($128M after tax) (`ar-1986`). Excluding both, net income is about $800M and ROE about 24.6%.
- **1987.** The $40M Canadian IPO gain and the $36M foods provision roughly cancel.
- **1988.** This is the cleanest year. Equity income contains a share of a bottling-affiliate gain, but it is offset by the entertainment affiliate's write-downs. Equity income in total is only $48M.

Gains from selling stock in subsidiaries are not operating earnings, and the framework would exclude them.

**Leverage check.**

The rise in ROE from roughly 20% to 33% happened alongside debt/capital rising from about 6–9% to 39–48%, and treasury stock growing from $347M at end-1984 to $2,069M at end-1988 (`ar-1988` equity statement).

Two adjustments remove most of that effect:
- **Return on equity plus net debt.** Net interest was small in 1988: $230.5M of interest expense against $199.3M of interest income. Earnings before net interest, divided by common equity plus preferred plus net debt (about $4.2B on average), give roughly **25%**.
- **Adding back the buybacks.** Restoring about $1.72B of post-1984 repurchases to equity, and crediting about 8% pre-tax interest on that cash, also gives roughly **23–24%**.

The best evidence is the earlier record: 1978–81 shows ROE of 21–23% with debt/capital under 10%. That is the "good returns on equity while employing little or no debt" the rule asks for. The recent 33% contains a real but secondary financial boost.

**Where the return actually comes from.**

| | 1985 | 1986 (ex-provisions) | 1987 | 1988 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Soft-drink operating income / identifiable operating assets (pretax) | 27% | 36% | 34% | **44%** |
| International operating margin | 22.9% | 24.5% | 27.0% | 29.7% |
| U.S. operating margin | 11.6% | 14.5% | 15.3% | 15.4% |
| Consolidated gross margin | 50.5% | 50.5% | 52.6% | 55.6% |
| Foods operating income / assets | n/a | 20% | 16% | 13% |
| Equity income / investments in affiliates | n/a | 6.3% | 5.1% | 2.5% |

(Segment data from `ar-1987` and `ar-1988`. The 1988 soft-drink figure is $1,690.7M on $3,809M.)

The 33% blended ROE is an average of three things:
- An extraordinary concentrate business earning roughly 44% pretax, and about 29% after tax, on its gross operating assets.
- A mediocre foods unit.
- About **$1.9B** of affiliate stakes earning low book returns. These are bottlers and the entertainment affiliate, and they amount to about 57% of shareholders' equity.

The deconsolidations moved capital-intensive, debt-funded bottling off the company's own balance sheet (`ar-1987`: "freeing each company to adopt appropriately leveraged capital structures"). That flatters the parent's ratios. It does not change the system's economics.

**Verdict on §3.1:** passes. Stripped of leverage and one-offs, the business earns about 23–25% on equity. Its core earns far more, and it did so in years with almost no debt.

## §3.2 Durable competitive advantage (`brk-letter-1986#16`)

In GEICO's case the moat was a cost gap. Here the castle is the trademark, and the moat is consumer preference plus the global bottling and distribution system. The rule asks two things: is the advantage real, and is management widening it?

**Evidence the moat is real:**
- **World share.** About 45% of world soft-drink sales, "more than double the number anyone else sells" (`ar-1988`). 1987 volume was double the nearest competitor's (`ar-1987`).
- **U.S. share.** Above 40% in the U.S. (`upi-1989-01-31`). In 1986 it was 9 points ahead of the primary competitor, with a 59% fountain share and 49% of vending (`ar-1986`). Fountain sales outsell the nearest rival more than 2-to-1 (`ar-1987`).
- **Consumer attachment.** After the 1985 change in the flagship formula, the company had to bring back the original as "classic" within the same year. That product then became the best-selling U.S. soft drink (`ar-1986`). Independent 1988 surveys put the trademark "off the charts" against the runner-up (`ar-1988`).
- **Pricing power.**
  - Concentrate gross margin was about 63% in 1986 (`ar-1986`).
  - The company raised prices to its largest bottler in October 1986 under new flexible-pricing contracts (`ar-1986`).
  - 1987 and 1988 operating income benefited from "price increases" (`ar-1988`).
  - Latin America in 1988 grew volume 1% but operating income 17% by emphasizing margin (`upi-1989-01-31`).
  - Management states it "is able to adjust prices to compensate for increasing costs" (`ar-1986`/`ar-1987`/`ar-1988`). That is the trait the inflation article (`fortune-1977-inflation#6`) identifies in highly profitable companies that "require relatively little debt capital."
- **Capital lightness.** This is the opposite of the Burlington pattern in `brk-letter-1985#9`.
  - Soft-drink capex in 1988 was $239M against $1,691M of soft-drink operating income.
  - Total capex was $387M on $8.3B of revenue.
  - The trademark that produces the earnings sits on the books at almost nothing: goodwill and intangibles are $56.5M. As with See's, the economic goodwill does not show on the balance sheet.

**Evidence the moat is widening:**
- World share rose from under 38% to nearly 45% in eight years, and daily servings rose from 359M to 559M (`ar-1988`).
- International volume grew 9% in 1987 and 7% in 1988.
- International per-capita consumption is about 51 servings a year against 274 in the U.S. (`ar-1988`, `ar-1987`), so the same product has a long runway.
- Management is spending to widen the moat. Advertising was $842M in 1988, up from $648M in 1986, and the company is investing in coolers, vending and fountain systems (`ar-1988`). All of this is expensed, so ROE is already net of it.

**Soft spots:**
- **Foods.** About 5% of operating income. Juice is commodity-priced, and the company's orange juice ranks second in chilled. Volume fell 22% in frozen orange juice in 1988 (`upi-1989-01-31`).
- **Currency.** 76–79% of operating income comes from outside the U.S. Key currencies strengthened against the dollar by 27%, 16% and 10% in 1986–88, which flattered reported growth.
- **Contract limits in the U.S.** Standard contracts with most U.S. bottlers set flagship syrup prices by formula (`ar-1986`), which caps U.S. pricing freedom outside the largest bottler.
- **High-inflation markets.** Argentina and Brazil saw volume and operating income fall under triple-digit inflation (`ar-1988`).

None of these touches the core trademark franchise.

**Verdict on §3.2:** a wide and widening moat around a simple product. This is the kind of "favorable long-term economics" business described in `brk-letter-1987#18`.

## §3.3 Owner earnings (`brk-letter-1986`)

The definition is reported earnings, plus depreciation and amortization and certain other non-cash charges, minus the average capex needed to maintain the business's competitive position and volume.

| 1988, $M | Conservative (all capex treated as maintenance) | Middle (3-year average capex) | Generous (maintenance capex ≈ depreciation) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Net income | 1,044.7 | 1,044.7 | 1,044.7 |
| + Depreciation and amortization | 169.8 | 169.8 | 169.8 |
| + Deferred taxes | 43.9 | 43.9 | 43.9 |
| − Capex | (386.8) | (345.4) | (169.8) |
| **Owner earnings** | **≈ 872** | **≈ 913** | **≈ 1,089** |
| Per share (364.6M average shares) | ≈ $2.39 | ≈ $2.50 | ≈ $2.99 |

Adjustments to these figures:
- Subtract about $6M of preferred dividends from each column.
- Subtract about $36M of undistributed equity income. Under `brk-letter-1981#3`, undistributed investee earnings count only if they eventually produce value. Given the affiliates' low returns, that is not yet proven.
- 1988 also used about $84M of working capital (`ar-1988` cash flow statement).

As a cash cross-check, operating cash flow of $1,167M minus capex of $387M gives about $780M.

**Owner earnings fall in the range of about $0.83–1.05B, or roughly $2.30–2.90 per share.** Reported EPS of $2.85 sits at the top of that range. Much of the capex goes to capacity expansion and international infrastructure, so the true figure is probably in the upper half. The 1986 provisions were mostly non-cash (`ar-1986`) and fall into the "certain other non-cash charges" category, but they do not affect 1988.

## Facts the framework wants that the packet lacks

1. **Maintenance capex versus growth capex.** This is the crux of owner earnings. As a working assumption, I'd treat maintenance capex as somewhere between depreciation and total capex.
2. **Local-currency (constant-exchange) operating income growth.** Without it, I cannot separate franchise growth from the weak dollar. I'd treat about 7% volume growth plus pricing as the underlying rate.
3. **Concentrate price and margin history by major country.** The only figure is the 1986 U.S. 63% gross margin. Japan is the top profit contributor, but its economics are not disclosed.
4. **Bottler contract terms internationally.** Their duration and whether they are perpetual matter, as does how much support the company must give bottlers. Cooperative payments to the largest bottler alone were $68M in 1988.
5. **Bottler economics on a look-through basis.** The packet does not show whether the $1.9B in affiliate stakes is the real price of keeping the system strong, which would make it part of the capital cost of the moat, or optional.
6. **Competitor financials** (the primary competitor's share trend and margins). Without them, the moat cannot be measured directly the way GEICO's cost gap was.
7. **1988 10-Qs and segment liabilities.** These would let me compute segment returns on net, not gross, operating assets.

## Carry-forward to later stages

- **Stage 4 (management).** The ROE is now partly engineered: the net debt ceiling was raised to 35%, and buybacks totalled about $760M in 1988 at an average of about $40 a share (`ar-1988`). Whether those buybacks were made "well below intrinsic value" (`brk-letter-1984#4`) is a management question. So is whether the retained dollars put into entertainment and bottler stakes earned $1 of value per $1 (`brk-letter-1983`).
- **Stage 5 (valuation).** Use owner earnings of roughly $2.40–2.90 per share against the $46.56 price, and value the core concentrate business separately from the $1.9B of affiliates. The affiliates' market value exceeded carrying value by $291M at end-1988 (`ar-1988` Note 3).
