**Memo: Purchase of Company A (AAA), as of February 27, 1989**

We recommend buying Company A at about $46.56 per share, building toward roughly a quarter of the portfolio and holding indefinitely. This memo sets out what the business is, why it passes our tests, what we think it is worth, and what would change our view. (We recognize the company; the analysis below uses only the documents in our file.)

The business is simpler than its annual reports suggest. More than 90 percent of operating income comes from one activity: selling soft-drink concentrate and syrup, under the most widely recognized trademark in the world, to bottlers in more than 160 countries (ar-1988). Bottling, the capital-intensive and debt-heavy part of the system, has been moved into affiliates the company owns 49 percent or less of. Entertainment, a diversion of the early 1980s, has been spun out the same way. A juice business remains, but it contributes about 5 percent of operating income and is weakening.

It passes our gatekeeping tests without difficulty. It is the opposite of a turnaround: earnings per share have risen every year from 1976 to 1988, and the dividend has been raised 27 years in a row. It needs no technical judgment we lack. Its size, about $1 billion of 1988 net income, means a position here can matter to us.

On business quality, return on equity is the test that counts, provided it is not inflated by leverage or one-time gains. The headline figure of 33 percent in 1988 is partly engineered by share repurchases and borrowing. Stripping those out leaves about 23 to 25 percent. In 1978 to 1981, when debt was under 10 percent of capital, the company earned 21 to 23 percent on equity. The core concentrate business earned about 44 percent pretax on its operating assets in 1988.

The moat behind these returns is consumer preference for the trademark, reinforced by a distribution system no competitor matches. The company's share of world soft-drink sales has risen from under 38 percent to nearly 45 percent in eight years, more than double the next competitor. It has raised prices and widened margins abroad. Per-capita consumption outside the United States is about one-fifth the domestic level, so the runway is long. Total capital spending is under 5 percent of revenue, so growth costs little capital.

Owner earnings — reported earnings plus depreciation and deferred taxes, less capital spending — come to roughly $2.40 to $2.90 per share. That is close to reported profits.

Management has run the company as a team since 1981, and the record supports its claim to work for owners. Each dollar of earnings retained since 1981 has been matched by more than five dollars of added market value per share. About 69 million shares were repurchased between 1984 and 1988, the early ones at prices that now look clearly below value.

There are blemishes:
- Shares were issued in the early 1980s to buy into entertainment, a venture the company has since largely exited.
- Management is willing to borrow to boost returns.
- Its reports read at times like promotional material.

We judge these real but not disqualifying, partly because management itself reversed the worst of them.

Valuation is where we must be candid. By the old bargain standard of buying below net working capital, this stock fails completely: it sells for 44 times working capital and five times tangible book value. We do not buy great businesses on that test. The relevant question is whether the price makes sense if the future resembles the past.

Our method:
- Take core owner earnings of about $885 million.
- Grow them at 10 percent a year for a decade, close to the company's actual 1978–88 record, then at 5 percent.
- Discount the result at 12 percent, management's own estimate of its cost of capital.
- Add the affiliates at market value and subtract net debt and preferred stock.

This gives a value near $57 per share, with a reasonable range of $51 to $67. At a 10 percent discount rate the value is $73 to $96.

The present price implies growth of about 8 percent, below what the company has delivered. The margin of safety is therefore real but modest at a demanding discount rate, about 18 percent. It rests entirely on the franchise lasting. If growth slowed to 7 percent, the margin would vanish.

That is why we are not taking the full 40 percent maximum our rules permit when facts are clear and the risk of drastic impairment is low. We think both conditions hold here, but we lack three things: the long-term bond yield to compare against, a split of capital spending into maintenance and growth, and earnings growth stripped of currency effects. A quarter of the portfolio is our judgment of the right weight. No corporate event is needed for this to work. If the business compounds as it has, the market will eventually reflect it.

Several things would change our view:
- **Currency.** Evidence that the weak dollar, which flattered results in 1986–88, accounts for most of the recent growth.
- **Interest rates.** Long-term rates high enough that a 5.7 percent owner-earnings yield looks poor beside them.
- **Bottler support.** Proof that supporting bottlers is a permanent and growing cost of keeping the moat.
- **Management behavior.** A return to diversification or share issuance on poor terms.

We would sell if we found we had misread the economics, through sustained share loss or eroding margins, or if management turned to destroying capital. A rising price alone would not prompt a sale. A falling price with the business intact would prompt us to buy more.
