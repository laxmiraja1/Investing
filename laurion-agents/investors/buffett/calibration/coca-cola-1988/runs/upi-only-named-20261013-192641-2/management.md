# Stage 3: Management and capital allocation (Framework §4)

Management passes, with qualifications. Goizueta and Keough have run the core business very well. Capital kept in the business since 1981 has created several dollars of market value for each dollar retained. Since 1986 the direction has been good: refocusing on soft drinks, unwinding entertainment, buying back a lot of stock. Three things count against them:
- From 1982 to 1986 they diversified into entertainment, partly paid for with Coca-Cola shares, and it fell short of their own hurdle rate.
- Their buyback rule is "excess cash" rather than "price well below value."
- The reports mix candor with promotion.

---

## 4.1 Honest, able management in place (`brk-letter-1979`, `brk-letter-1987#18`)

**In place and long-tenured.** Goizueta (chairman and CEO) and Keough (president and COO) assembled the current team in 1981 (ar-1987 letter). Their record since then:

| | 1981 | 1988 | Annual rate |
|---|---|---|---|
| Income from continuing operations | $432M | $1,040M | about 13.4% |
| EPS (continuing) | $1.17 | $2.85 | about 13.6% |
| Return on average equity | 19.9% | 33% (reported) | |
| U.S. soft-drink share | — | over 40% (1988) | |
| Worldwide share | — | over 44% (1987) | |

**Operating ability.**
- Administrative and general expense held at about 7–8% of revenue throughout.
- Corporate expense fell from $224.5M to $175.3M in 1987.
- U.S. syrup plants were consolidated as bottlers switched to concentrate.
- The fragmented German bottling system was cut from 102 bottlers to 68.

This is the "widen the moat" behavior the framework praises at GEICO (`brk-letter-1986#16`).

**Responding to a mistake.** The 1985 change in formula was reversed within the same year, by bringing back the original as Coca-Cola classic. By 1986 classic was the No. 1 U.S. soft drink. Acting quickly is a plus. The reports never call the episode a mistake, though. The 1986 report presents it as a "megabrand" strategy, which is the opposite of the candor standard covered in §4.4.

## 4.2 Each $1 retained must create at least $1 of market value (`brk-letter-1983`)

Per share, end of 1981 to the as-of date (ar-1986/ar-1987 selected data; upi-1989-01-31; prices):

| | Per share |
|---|---|
| Net income per share, 1982–1988 total | $13.82 |
| Cash dividends, 1982–1988 | −$6.99 |
| CPE shares distributed in kind (1987, at carrying value) | −$0.90 |
| **Retained per share** (includes money used for buybacks) | **$5.93** |
| Market price: $11.58 (end 1981) → $46.56 (1989-02-24) | +$34.98 |
| **Market value per $1 retained** | **about $5.90** |

**Removing the higher P/E.** Part of that gain came from investors paying a higher multiple. At end-1981 the stock traded at about 9.9× continuing earnings. Applying that multiple to $2.85 gives $28.20, a gain of $16.60, or about **$2.80 per $1 retained**. That still passes comfortably.

**Business value, not just market price.** Continuing EPS rose $1.68 on $5.93 retained, a return of roughly 28% on retained capital. Some caveats:
- This ignores the added net debt.
- It ignores the currency tailwind.
- It ignores the shares issued for Columbia, discussed next.

Even with those caveats the test passes.

## 4.3 Acquisitions and reinvestment, judged by where the money went

### (a) Entertainment (1982–1987): fails management's own test

**What they did.**
- Bought Columbia Pictures in 1982. The packet does not give the price or form of payment. Average shares outstanding jumped from 372M (1981) to 390M (1982) and 408M (1983). That suggests roughly 36M shares, about 10% dilution, were issued around the acquisition. This is an inference, not a disclosed fact.
- Bought Embassy in 1985 with about 7.1M Coca-Cola shares worth $168.6M, about $23.83 a share. That is roughly 14× 1985 EPS. The same year, Coca-Cola repurchased 15M shares at about $25.33.
- Bought Tandem in 1985 for $178M cash and Merv Griffin in 1986 for about $200M.

**How it worked out.**
- Entertainment's 1986 operating income was $235M pretax on $2,170M of identifiable assets, about 11% pretax and perhaps 7% after tax.
- Management's own after-tax cost of capital for soft drinks is 12% (ar-1986, ar-1987). Entertainment carries more risk, so the right hurdle would be higher.
- At end-1987, the market value of all publicly traded investees (CPE, CCE, T.C.C.) exceeded carrying value by only $44M. CPE was therefore worth roughly its book value, not a premium.
- CPE booked a $100M restructuring in 1987 and write-downs in 1988 that cut Coca-Cola's equity income by 57%.

**Judged against `brk-letter-1982#17`.** That rule says shares should be issued only when "our owners receive in business value as much as we give." Shares of a business earning over 25% on capital were exchanged for a film and TV business that returned less than its cost of capital. The 7.1M Embassy shares alone are worth about $330M today.

**Mitigation.** Management recognized the problem. They changed studio leadership in 1986 and admitted Columbia's films were "not satisfied." In 1987 they combined the sector into CPE, distributed 51% to shareholders, and said they now prefer "investments within our existing lines of business" (ar-1987). The remaining 49% stake is carried at $989M, about $2.80 a share. In valuation I treat it as an asset at roughly book value, not as part of the franchise.

### (b) Foods: small and mediocre

- The 1985 acquisition of Nutri-Foods cost about $30M. There have been new products such as the Bacardi mixers.
- A $36M restructuring in 1987 coincided with a change in senior management. Frozen orange juice volume fell 22% in 1988.
- Foods is only about 5% of operating income, so the damage is limited. Management did not exit, though.

### (c) Bottlers: reasonable strategy, but hard to judge

**What they did.**
- Bought bottlers and transferred them to CCE: Lupton and BCI for about $2.25B, financed with CCE's own debt and not guaranteed by Coca-Cola.
- Bought Miami CCBC ($325M), the Rainwater bottlers ($211M) and Memphis, then resold them to CCE at carrying value.
- Took the CCE (1986) and T.C.C. (1987) stakes to 49% through public offerings, booking gains of $375M and $40M.

**Returns.**
- On its own, the CCE stake is poor. Equity income of $43.3M on a $749M carrying value is about 5.8%.
- The real return comes from the concentrate system. CCE bought $653M of syrup and concentrate in 1987, and the U.S. concentrate gross margin is about 63%. The packet does not provide what is needed to measure that system-wide return.

**Flags.**
- Coca-Cola deals with CCE, a 49%-owned company whose majority is held by outside shareholders. These dealings include sales of bottlers, pricing and $53M of net marketing payments in 1987, so there are related-party questions.
- Contingent liabilities: $394M of guarantees, about $300M of finance-subsidiary (CCFC) debt to be consolidated in 1988, and $325M of CPE receivables guaranteed with recourse.
- Much of the system's leverage now sits outside Coca-Cola's own balance sheet.

## 4.4 Buybacks only well below intrinsic value (`brk-letter-1984`)

Repurchase record (ar-1986, ar-1987, upi-1989-01-31):

| Year | Shares | Cost | Average price | P/E (on normalized EPS) | Earnings yield |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1984 | 18.4M | $335.7M | $18.24 | 11.6× ($1.57) | 8.6% |
| 1985 | 15.0M | $379.9M | $25.33 | 14.7× ($1.72) | 6.8% |
| 1986 | 2.9M | $110.5M | $38.37 | 18.5× ($2.07) | 5.4% |
| 1987 | 14.2M | $605.1M | $42.61 | 17.5× ($2.43) | 5.7% |
| 1988 | 18.9M | not in packet | ~$38–42 assumed (1988 range $35–45) | ~14× ($2.85) | ~7% |

**Scale.** About 69M shares were bought from 1984 to 1988, roughly 17% of the 1983 average of 408M. This effectively reversed the dilution from Columbia and Embassy and then went further. On scale and direction, this is what the framework wants.

**Outcome so far.** Every purchase is below today's $46.56. The 1984–85 purchases, at 11–15× earnings for a business earning more than 25% on capital, look clearly below value even without a formal valuation.

**Discipline: the framework's concern.**
- Management's stated trigger is capacity, not price. Repurchases "commenced after management determined that the Company's available cash and debt capacity exceeded the funds required" (ar-1987). And: "Often the best use of excess cash … has been to invest in our own stock."
- The 40-million-share, three-year program was announced in July 1987, when the stock was near a record. The Q3 1987 range was $42.88–53.13, and those buyback shares averaged about $42.12.
- Nowhere do they say they would stop buying above some estimate of value.

**Verdict.** The results are good and the 1986–88 prices look reasonable for a business compounding at about 15%. But the rule asks for purchases "well below" intrinsic value, and that can only be confirmed against the Stage 4 valuation. I am carrying it forward as an open question.

## 4.5 Dividends and leverage policy

**Payout.** Management is lowering the payout ratio toward 40% (cash payout was 46% in 1987) so more is kept "in opportunities offering returns in excess of the cost of capital." The §4.2 test supports retention in the core business. The risk is that retained cash drifts back into non-core ventures, as it did in 1982–86. The stated preference for existing lines of business is the safeguard, and it is only a statement.

**Debt.** Management raised its net debt-to-capital ceiling to 35% "aimed at reducing the weighted average cost of capital"; actual net debt is 15.3%.
- The framework favors little or no debt.
- It points out that highly profitable companies need little debt (`fortune-1977-inflation#6`). Coca-Cola doesn't need debt. It chooses it, mostly to fund buybacks.
- This choice is part of why ROE rose from about 20% to 33%.

This is a moderate yellow flag, not a disqualifier. Notably, management itself warns that inflation gains on monetary liabilities "do not necessarily imply that incurring more debt would be beneficial," which is unusually owner-minded.

## 4.6 Compensation and dilution

The packet gives the stock-based plans but not the cash pay.

| Item | Detail |
|---|---|
| Options outstanding | 5.6M at end-1987, priced $10–45 |
| Option grants | about 1.1–1.8M a year, roughly 0.3–0.5% of shares |
| Restricted stock plan | 3M shares |
| 1987 option plan | 8M shares |
| 1985 performance units | cash awards on 555,000 shares above a $20.63 base; worth about $14M at $46.56 |

**Dilution is small** next to buybacks of about 5% of shares a year.

**Weakness.** Every plan pays on the stock price. Price can rise because the P/E goes up, or because of leverage, not only because per-share business value improves. The packet does not show whether pay is linked to return on capital.

## 4.7 Candor in reporting (`brk-letter-1979`, `brk-letter-1983#2`)

| Candid | Promotional or flattering |
|---|---|
| States unusual items clearly ("Excluding 35 cents per share of unusual items in 1986 … EPS increased 17.4%") | The 1986 highlights lead with 41% EPS growth that includes the $375M CCE gain (footnoted) |
| Admits Columbia's films disappointed; "our foods business is obviously in transition" | "Potential appears limitless"; system sales "added an estimated $250 billion to the world economy" using a multiplier of five |
| Flags that Australia's 37% volume jump came from shipping patterns, and that retail case sales of 11% "more accurately reflect" demand; U.S. shipments up 1% versus retail sales up 6% because of bottler destocking | Gains from selling stock in subsidiaries are run through income, and restatements every year make the accounts hard to compare |
| Discloses its 12% cost-of-capital hurdle, that current-cost income would be lower, and how much currency helped | New Coke is never called a mistake; entertainment was praised as the "innovative leader" a year before the partial exit |
| | The CEO gives short-term EPS guidance ("well into the mid-teens" for 1989; upi-1989-01-31). The framework gives little weight to projections |

**Net.** The numbers needed to judge the business are disclosed and usually explained. The surrounding text often reads like the "public relations document" the framework dislikes. On candor I would rate them good, not exemplary.

## 4.8 What the investor would have relied on that I don't have

1. **Meeting Goizueta and Keough**, to judge integrity, temperament and whether they think about buybacks in terms of price versus value. The framework puts heavy weight on this ("high-grade, talented and likeable managers," `brk-letter-1987#18`; the reliance on Mrs. B's word, `brk-letter-1984#7`). It is the largest gap.
2. **The proxy statement**: executive pay amounts, performance metrics, and how much stock directors and officers own. "We eat our own cooking" (`brk-letter-1983#0`) cannot be checked.
3. **The 1988 annual report and the CEO's letter**: buyback cost and price, year-end equity and debt, and any discussion of CPE.
4. **The terms of the Columbia acquisition**: price, shares issued, and management's after-the-fact assessment.
5. **A stated price rule for buybacks**, or confirmation that none exists.
6. **Intentions for CPE and Foods**: hold, sell or reinvest.
7. **References from bottlers and partners**, and terms of the CCE related-party dealings.
8. **Whether a large holder would be welcome** and on what terms, which matters for position size.

## Stage 3 conclusion

| §4 test | Result |
|---|---|
| Honest, able management in place | Pass for operating ability; integrity not checkable without meeting them |
| $1 retained → ≥$1 market value | Strong pass (about $5.90 per $1, about $2.80 after removing the higher P/E) |
| Share issuance (`brk-letter-1982#17`) | Past failure (Columbia/Embassy for entertainment), largely reversed by buybacks |
| Buybacks well below intrinsic value | Large and so far profitable; price discipline unstated; to be confirmed in Stage 4 |
| Candor | Good, not exemplary |

Management is good enough to go on to valuation. In the valuation stage, I will treat the CPE and Foods holdings at modest value and value the soft-drink franchise on its own.
