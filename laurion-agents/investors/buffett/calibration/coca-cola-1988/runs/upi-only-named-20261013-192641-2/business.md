# Stage 2: Business analysis (Framework §3)

Coca-Cola passes all three tests in this section. The soft-drink concentrate business earns returns far above average with little capital, and it has a durable moat. Consolidated ROE overstates that edge somewhat, because recent years were helped by rising debt, shrinking equity and a weaker dollar. The packet does not give the true owner-earnings figure precisely, but the range is narrow enough to carry into valuation.

---

## 3.1 Return on equity, not EPS growth (`brk-letter-1979#2`)

The rule asks for a high return on equity "without undue leverage, accounting gimmickry, etc." There are three things to check.

**Reported return on average equity** (ar-1986 and ar-1987 selected data; upi-1989-01-31):

| Period | ROE | Total debt / total capital |
|---|---|---|
| 1976–1981 | 19.7–21.2% every year | 3.5–9.3% |
| 1982–1983 | 19.4–19.6% | 15.1% |
| 1984 | 21.8% | 30.7% (restated) |
| 1985 | 23.5% | 27.7% |
| 1986 | 28.8% | 31.4% |
| 1987 | 27.2% | 45.6% gross; 15.3% net |
| 1988 | 33% (reported) | not in packet |

**The clean baseline is about 20% ROE with almost no debt.** From 1976 to 1981 the company earned about 20% on equity with debt at or below 9% of capital. That includes the drag from Foods and other lower-return businesses, and the Columbia Pictures entertainment business (bought 1982) came later. By the framework's benchmark, this already beats nearly all large companies. Fortune found only six of 1,000 large companies averaged above 30% over a decade (`brk-letter-1987#1`). Coca-Cola's ten-year average of about 22% is below that 30% bar but well above the norm.

**The rise to 27–33% is partly financial.** Four things contributed:
- **Debt went up.** Gross debt rose from $520M (1983) to $2,702M (1987). Net of surplus cash, the CPE notes and temporary investments, net debt was still only 15.3% of capital.
- **Equity went down.** Treasury stock rose from about $12M (start of 1984) to $1,309M (end of 1987). Equity fell from $3,515M to $3,224M in 1987 alone, after $605M of buybacks and the $335M in-kind dividend of CPE shares. For 1988 I estimate year-end equity at roughly $3.1B: $3,224M + $1,040M earnings − about $438M dividends − about $760M buybacks. That buyback cost is my assumption (18.9M shares at about $38–44), not a reported figure. Average equity of about $3.15B is consistent with the reported 33%.
- **The dollar weakened.** Key hard currencies rose 27% (1986), 16% (1987) and 10% (1988) against the dollar. International income is about 74% of operating income.
- **Bottling was deconsolidated.** Selling down to 49% of CCE (1986) and T.C.C. Beverages (1987) moved capital-heavy, debt-heavy bottling off the balance sheet.

**Returns adjusted for debt are still very high.** I estimate 1987 earnings before net interest at about $963M: $916M net income plus about $47M after-tax net interest ($279M − $207M, taxed at 35%). Net operating capital was about $3.8B: $5,926M total capital less $1,268M surplus cash, $545M CPE notes and $308M temporary investments. That gives roughly 25% on capital employed with debt stripped out. On this measure the business still clears the bar without any help from leverage.

**Segment returns** (pretax operating income ÷ identifiable assets; ar-1987 note 16; 1986 restructuring provisions added back):

| Segment | 1986 | 1987 |
|---|---|---|
| International soft drinks | 47.7% | 52.1% |
| U.S. soft drinks | 20.6% | 15.8% |
| All soft drinks | ~36% | 34.3% |
| Foods (1987 provision added back) | 20.3% | 16.4% |
| Corporate expense | −$224.5M | −$175.3M |

- U.S. assets jumped from $1,425M to $2,047M in 1987. The packet does not explain why. Bottler holdings bought for later resale may be in this figure.
- These are returns on gross assets. Accounts payable of $1.43B fund part of the base, so returns on net operating assets are higher.
- International soft drinks are in the range of Berkshire's best owned businesses (57% pretax on equity for the seven units in `brk-letter-1987#1`).

**Accounting items.** One-time items now recur:
- 1986: a $375M gain on selling CCE stock, offset by $180M of provisions.
- 1987: a $40M gain on selling T.C.C. stock, offset by a $36M provision.
- 1988: CPE write-downs, which cut equity income by 57%.

Net over 1986–87, these items added about $199M pretax. I treat them as non-recurring and normalize per-share earnings:

| Year | Reported EPS | Normalized EPS | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1985 | $1.72 | $1.72 | continuing operations |
| 1986 | $2.42 | $2.07 | management's own figure, excluding $0.35 of unusual items |
| 1987 | $2.43 | about $2.43 | gain and provision roughly offset |
| 1988 | $2.85 | $2.85 | |

The annual restatements, especially the entertainment and T.C.C. deconsolidations, also make revenue and operating income impossible to compare across reports without care. Net income is unchanged by them. The framework would note this, but it is not gimmickry.

**EPS growth versus business growth.** EPS grew about 16.1% a year from 1983 to 1988 ($1.35 to $2.85). Net income grew about 13.5% a year ($553M to $1,040M). About 2.5 points a year came from a smaller share count (408M in 1983 to about 365M average in 1988). The rule says to judge the 13.5% and the ROE, not the 16%.

**Growth without capital.** This is the most important finding under §3.1. From 1984 to 1987:
- Coca-Cola earned $3,201M.
- It paid $1,577M in cash dividends, $1,431M in buybacks and $335M in kind, a total of $3,343M, more than 100% of earnings.
- Net income still rose from $629M to $916M, and soft-drink volume grew 6–10% a year.
- Equity has stayed roughly flat at about $2.8–3.2B since 1983 while earnings nearly doubled.

This is the pattern the framework prizes. Businesses that need little capital "can grow while concurrently making almost all of their earnings available for deployment in new opportunities" (`brk-letter-1987#1`). Some of the gap was filled with net borrowing, which partly flatters the result.

## 3.2 Durable competitive advantage: the moat (`brk-letter-1986#16`)

The packet gives evidence for the moat in four forms.

**Brand and market share, widening each year:**
- U.S. share was over 39% in 1986, 9 points ahead of the primary competitor, and over 40% in 1988.
- Worldwide share was 43% (1986) and over 44% (1987). 1987 volume was double the nearest competitor's.
- Coca-Cola classic is the No. 1 U.S. soft drink. In 1986 the company had five of the U.S. top ten.
- U.S. fountain share was 59% in 1986, and in 1987 the company outsold its nearest fountain rival by more than 2 to 1. In 1987 Coca-Cola USA's fountain growth in chain and fast-food accounts was more than three times its nearest competitor's.

The GEICO test is a moat that management "continually widens." Share has gone up every year shown.

**The brand survived management's own mistake.** The 1985 new-taste Coke was followed by the reintroduction of the original formula as "Coca-Cola classic." By 1986 classic was the best-selling U.S. soft drink. The trademark's hold on consumers did not depend on management getting the product right, and that is strong evidence of durability.

**The economics show pricing power and low costs:**
- Gross margin on U.S. concentrate was about 63% (1986). Consolidated gross margin was 52.6% (1987).
- Management says the company is the low-cost producer in most of its 155 countries and "is able to adjust prices to compensate for increasing costs" (ar-1986, ar-1987, inflation section).
- New contracts with CCE allow flexible pricing, and the company used them to raise prices in October 1986. International contracts "generally do not contain restrictions."

**Taxes and inflation.** By the framework's own reasoning (`brk-letter-1986#22`), a strong, unregulated franchise keeps the benefit of corporate tax cuts. Coca-Cola's effective tax rate fell from 39.7% (1984) to 35.0% (1987), and the U.S. statutory rate fell to 34% for 1988. Low capital intensity also limits the damage inflation does (`fortune-1977-inflation#6`). Management admits that current-cost income would be lower than reported, but does not give the figure.

**Room to grow.** International consumption per person was 16% of the U.S. level in 1986, so the moat has room to grow, not just to be defended. U.S. consumption per person rose from 203 to 274 servings between 1980 and 1987.

**Limits on the moat:**
- **It costs money to keep.** Selling expenses were $2.11B in 1987, 27.6% of revenue, including $720M of media advertising. Cooperative advertising for U.S. bottlers was about $234M in 1986. These costs are expensed, so they are already inside earnings, but they are a permanent cost of the castle.
- **U.S. concentrate pricing is partly fixed.** Most U.S. Coca-Cola syrup contracts outside CCE use formula pricing. This caps the U.S. margin, and the weak U.S. segment return reflects it.
- **The bottlers carry the debt.** CCE had $2.72B of liabilities against $1.53B of net assets and earned $88M on $3.3B of revenue in 1987. Coca-Cola also guarantees $394M of debt and $325M of CPE receivables sold with recourse. The concentrate business's high returns partly depend on pushing capital and debt into 49%-owned affiliates. The moat is real, but some of its cost sits off the balance sheet.
- **The non-core businesses have no moat.**
  - Foods (orange juice) is close to a commodity business. Coca-Cola is No. 2 in chilled juice, and 1988 frozen juice volume fell 22%. It is about 5% of operating income.
  - CPE, the film business, is carried at $989M and had 1988 write-downs. It is outside the circle of competence, so I give it no franchise value.

## 3.3 Owner earnings (`brk-letter-1986#16` era definition, `brk-letter-1986`)

The formula is (a) reported earnings plus (b) depreciation, amortization and other non-cash charges, minus (c) average capital spending needed to maintain the business.

**1987** (ar-1987 statement of changes in financial position):

| Item | $M |
|---|---|
| (a) Net income | 916.1 |
| (b) Depreciation + goodwill amortization | +153.5 |
| Non-cash provision | +36.4 |
| Non-cash gain on T.C.C. stock sale | −39.7 |
| Deferred taxes (a reversal, so subtracted) | −28.7 |
| (c) Capital expenditures, all of it | −299.6 |
| Owner earnings, before adjusting for equity income | about 738 |
| Equity income kept by affiliates, not received in cash | −79.7 |
| Owner earnings on a cash basis | about 658 |

- The equity-income line is mostly CCE and CPE earnings that stay with those companies. Whether to count them depends on whether those retained earnings create value, a §4 question. CPE gives me little confidence that they do.
- If maintenance capex were only about equal to depreciation (about $152M), owner earnings would be about $885M. Capex has run at about twice depreciation since 1983, and the reports describe much of it as capacity expansion and international infrastructure, which is growth spending.
- So 1987 owner earnings fall in a range of about $740–885M, or about $1.95–2.35 per share on 377M average shares. The low end subtracts all capex. The high end treats only depreciation as maintenance.
- Working capital released $273M in 1987. I do not count this as recurring.

**1988:** Only net income ($1,040M, $2.85 per share on about 365M shares) is in the packet. If capex and depreciation are about the same as in 1985–87 (capex about $300–330M, depreciation about $160M), owner earnings would be about $870–1,000M, or roughly $2.40–2.75 per share. This is an assumption, not a reported figure.

The key point is that owner earnings are close to reported earnings, at about 80–95%. Most other industrial companies cannot say this, and the gap is small because the capital needs are small.

## Facts the framework would want that the packet lacks

1. **The 1988 annual report and 10-Qs.** The packet has no 1988 balance sheet, cash-flow statement, capex, depreciation, segment returns, year-end equity or year-end debt. The 33% ROE is reported, but I cannot check it.
2. **Maintenance versus growth capex**, especially for international soft drinks. This is the largest open variable in owner earnings.
3. **Local-currency operating income and volume.** These would separate real growth from the 1986–88 currency tailwind. If the dollar turns, reported growth could stall for years.
4. **Price versus volume versus mix** in concentrate revenue, and the history of the concentrate price. These would show pricing power directly rather than relying on management's assertion.
5. **The terms of the U.S. formula-pricing contracts** and when they could be renegotiated.
6. **Competitor figures:** the primary competitor's margins, share by country and advertising spend. These would size the moat in relative terms.
7. **Bottler economics:** look-through earnings and debt for CCE, T.C.C. and the other affiliates. These would show how much of Coca-Cola's return depends on leverage held off its own books.
8. **Current-cost income**, which management mentions but does not quantify.
9. **CPE's real economics** and what the 49% stake is worth. Its 1988 write-down size is not given.

## Stage 2 conclusion

| Test | Result |
|---|---|
| ROE without undue leverage | Pass. About 20% with no debt for a decade, about 25% on capital with leverage stripped out, and the soft-drink core at about 34% pretax on gross assets |
| Moat | Strong and widening in soft drinks, but expensive to maintain and partly supported by leveraged affiliates; no moat in Foods or CPE |
| Owner earnings | About 80–95% of reported earnings; earnings nearly doubled since 1983 while equity stayed flat |

The core business looks like a large, worldwide version of what the framework calls an outstanding business. Two items need a closer look in Stage 3 (management and capital allocation): whether the debt-funded buybacks happen below intrinsic value, and whether the money retained in CPE and the bottlers has earned its keep under the $1 test.
