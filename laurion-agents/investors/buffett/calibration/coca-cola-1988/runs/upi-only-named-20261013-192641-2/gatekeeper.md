  "reason": "Coca-Cola passes the Section 2 gatekeeping rules, so it goes on to business analysis. One flag: I recognize the company and know Berkshire's later involvement with it. I have not used that here, and everything below comes from the packet as of 1989-02-27.\n\n**1. Turnarounds seldom turn (brk-letter-1979).** Coca-Cola is not a turnaround. Earnings per share rose every year in the ten-year record: $0.84 in 1977, $1.57 in 1984, $1.72 in 1985, $2.42 in 1986, $2.43 in 1987 and $2.85 in 1988 (ar-1987 selected data; upi-1989-01-31). Return on equity was above 19% in every year from 1976 to 1987, rose to 27\u201329% in 1986\u201387, and the company reports 33% for 1988. The weak spots are Foods, which is restructuring, and the old Entertainment sector. Both are small next to the core business. Soft drinks produced more than 95% of operating income in 1987 (ar-1987).\n\n**2. Little or no debt (brk-letter-1982).** This is the filter that needs the closest look. At the end of 1987, gross debt was $2.70B against equity of $3.22B, which management reports as 45.6% of total capital. That figure is inflated by temporary items. The company held $1.47B of cash and marketable securities. It was also owed $545M in notes by Columbia Pictures Entertainment (CPE), paid in cash in January 1988, and held $308M of temporary investments sold in January 1988. Net of those, debt was 15.3% of capital. Net interest expense fell 52% in 1988 because cash flow was higher (upi-1989-01-31). Operating cash flow was about $1.2B in 1987. The business does not need debt to run: management describes it as needing little capital and producing a lot of excess cash. The borrowing pays for share buybacks and bottler investments, and management has set a voluntary ceiling of 35% net debt to capital. This is not 'little or no debt' in the strictest sense, so it is a point to watch under management and capital allocation. It does not fail the rule, whose target is low-return businesses that cannot get enough capital (fortune-1977-inflation#6).\n\n**3. Consistent earning power and simple business / circle of competence.** The core business is selling soft-drink syrup and concentrate to bottlers. Gross margin on concentrate sold to U.S. bottlers is about 63%. The trademarks lead the market: more than 40% of U.S. carbonated soft drinks and more than 44% worldwide. There is no heavy technology. Bottling, which takes a lot of capital, has been moved into 49%-owned affiliates such as Coca-Cola Enterprises (CCE). Ten years out, the economics are understandable from the packet: rising consumption per person outside the U.S., where per-capita consumption was 16% of the U.S. rate in 1986, plus pricing power and very little reinvestment needed. Three things are harder to judge:\n- the 49% stake in CPE, the entertainment company;\n- the fact that about 74% of operating income comes from outside the U.S., so currency moves matter;\n- the restated accounts, which shift from one year's report to the next.\n\nThese make the analysis harder but do not put the business outside the circle of competence.\n\n**4. Management in place.** Goizueta and Keough have run the company since 1981 and have a stated framework for creating value: a 12% after-tax cost of capital, returns measured against it, and buybacks. The annual reports are partly promotional. For example, they multiply system retail sales by five to claim $250B of economic impact. Candor and buyback discipline belong in the management stage, not this filter.\n\n**5. Size.** Market value is about $17.3B at $46.56 a share (prices). After-tax earnings were about $1.04B in 1988, roughly 100 times Berkshire's $10M threshold. In 1987 Berkshire's largest holding was Capital Cities/ABC, worth $1.035B (brk-letter-1987#18). A position of $1B or more would be about 6% of Coca-Cola, and the stock trades easily. This is the kind of large opportunity the framework looks for.\n\n**6. Industry exclusions.** Coca-Cola is not a commodity business like textiles or farm equipment (brk-letter-1985#9, brk-letter-1987#18), not a regulated utility, and not a business that has to keep pouring in capital.\n\nNo hard rule rules the company out, so proceed to Stage 2. The price-to-earnings ratio of 16.3 times 1988 earnings is for the valuation stage to judge."
