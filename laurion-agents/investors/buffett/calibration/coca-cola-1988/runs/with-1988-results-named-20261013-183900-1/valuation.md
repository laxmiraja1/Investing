# Stage 4: Valuation (framework §5) and Catalyst/timing (§6)

## 5.1 The framework's valuation rule: buy below net working capital (`bpl-letter-1965`)

The only valuation rule listed in §5 is the net-working-capital test from the partnership years. Applied to the 1988 balance sheet (`ar-1988`):

| Item | $M |
|---|---|
| Current assets | 3,245.4 |
| Less current liabilities | (2,868.9) |
| **Net working capital** | **376.5**, or **$1.06 per share** on 354.8M shares |
| Current assets less *all* liabilities ($4,105.3M) and the preferred ($300M) | **−1,159.9**, which is negative |
| Book value per common share: ($3,345.3M − $300M) / 354.8M | $8.58 |

At the last close of $46.56 (`prices`), the stock trades at about 44 times net working capital and 5.4 times book. The strict net-net figure is negative.

**Result: the company fails the framework's stated valuation hurdle completely. Measured against that hurdle, there is no margin of safety; the price is about $45.50 a share above it.**

The method doesn't fit this kind of business, and the framework says so itself by 1988. The net-working-capital test was the method for "generals" bought "primarily by quantitative standards" (`bpl-letter-1963`). By the as-of date the same investor writes that the goal is "an outstanding business at a sensible price, not a mediocre business at a bargain price." He adds that his years spent searching for "bargains" taught him "the economics of short-line farm implement manufacturers, third-place department stores, and New England textile manufacturers" (`brk-letter-1987#18`). Coca-Cola's value is in its trademark and its distribution system, not in its balance sheet; book equity is almost all tangible, and the franchise sits off the books. So the net-working-capital test can only ever say no to a business like this, and it does.

The framework still gives a usable standard elsewhere:
- A price should "make sense, even should the future of the acquired enterprise develop much along the lines of its past" (`brk-letter-1981#3`).
- The relevant comparison is "value to a private owner," and a purchase needs a "substantial excess of value" to give "a comfortable margin of safety" (`bpl-letter-1962#4`).
- The earnings to value are owner earnings (§3, `brk-letter-1986`).

The test below uses those standards. The framework sets no numeric margin for a franchise purchase, and none is invented here.

## 5.2 The owner-earnings base

From Stage 2, 1988 owner earnings were about $0.95–1.05B, or about $2.70–2.95 per share. Reported EPS of $2.85 is at the top of that range and is used below as the base.

Splitting the business in two shows what the market is actually paying for the franchise:
- **Affiliates.** The carrying value is $1,912M. The publicly traded investees were worth $291M above carrying value at year-end 1988. That puts the affiliates at about **$2.2B, or about $6 a share**. In 1988 they produced only about $48M of equity income.
- **Core business implied by the price.**
  - Market value of $16.52B less about $2.2B for affiliates leaves about $14.3B for the core equity.
  - Adding net debt of about $0.9B gives a core enterprise value of about **$15.2B**.
  - Core operating income of $1,598M, taxed at the 34% effective rate, gives about $1,055M after tax.
  - **The core business is therefore priced at about 14.4 times after-tax operating earnings, an earnings yield of about 6.9%.**

Headline multiples: 16.3x 1988 EPS and 19.2x 1987 EPS (`prices`). Management expects EPS growth "well into the mid-teens" in 1989 (`upi-1989-01-31`). If that comes true, EPS would be about $3.28 and the stock would trade at about 14x 1989 earnings. That figure is a projection, not a reported result.

## 5.3 "Sensible price" test: will today's price make sense if the future looks like the past?

**What the past shows (`ar-1988` selected data):**
- 1978–88: net income went from $375M to $1,045M and EPS from $1.01 to $2.85, about **10.9% a year**. This covers ten years that include the weak, diversified early 1980s.
- 1984–88: EPS went from $1.59 to $2.85, about **15.7% a year**, helped by buybacks and a weaker dollar.
- Return on equity of about 33%, combined with the planned 40% payout, could fund growth of over 15% if new capital earns what existing capital earns. Stage 2 noted that the money retained recently has gone mainly into low-return bottler stakes and into buybacks, so this is an upper bound.

**Ten-year outcome for a buyer at $46.56.** Dividends start at the indicated $1.36 a share (a 2.9% yield) with a payout of about 42%. The terminal price-earnings ratio is an assumption in each row.

| Scenario | EPS growth | EPS in 1998 | Terminal P/E | 1998 price | Price CAGR | + dividends ≈ total return |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Pessimistic: franchise slows, P/E falls | 8% | $6.15 | 12x | $74 | 4.7% | **~8%** |
| Like the past decade | 11% | $8.09 | 15x | $121 | 10.0% | **~13%** |
| Past decade, current P/E | 11% | $8.09 | 16.3x | $132 | 11.0% | **~14%** |
| Management's mid-teens rate | 15% | $11.53 | 15x | $173 | 13.1% | **~16%** |

**The alternative investment.** The packet contains no long-term government bond yield, so it has to be assumed. The company's own dollar borrowings carry coupons of 9¼–12⅜% (`ar-1988`, note 7), including a 9.32% finance-subsidiary bond due in 1990. **I assume a long-term risk-free rate of about 9%.**

**How the alternatives compare:**
- At 9% with no growth, the bond beats Coca-Cola's starting owner-earnings yield of about 6.1% ($2.85 / $46.56) at first.
- At 11–12% growth, Coca-Cola's earnings on today's price pass 9% (about $4.19 a share) in roughly 3½ years.
- From then on the gap widens, and the growth comes from a business that needs little capital and can pass on cost increases. The 1977 inflation article says that combination is the only kind that protects an owner when inflation is high (`fortune-1977-inflation#6`).

**Intrinsic value range.** This is value to a private owner, discounting the table's cash flows at the assumed 9%:

| Scenario | PV of terminal value | PV of 10 yrs of dividends | Value per share | Margin of safety at $46.56 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Pessimistic (8%, 12x) | ~$31 | ~$11 | **~$42** | **Negative (price ~10% above value)** |
| Like the past (11%, 15x) | ~$51 | ~$13 | **~$64** | **~27%** |
| Mid-teens (15%, 15x) | ~$73 | ~$16 | **~$90** | **~48%** |

Two further points from the same arithmetic:
- **Implied expectations.** At 9%, today's price is consistent with growth of only about 8–9% a year for a decade, followed by a 12–15x multiple. The market is pricing in less than the franchise delivered over the last ten years, even though the last ten years included the diversified era that management has since unwound.
- **Price against business value since 1987.** The stock is below its 1987 peak of $53.13, when it traded at about 22x earnings. EPS has risen 17% since then. The business's value has grown faster than the price.

## 5.4 Margin of safety against the hurdle

| Hurdle | Result |
|---|---|
| **§5 net-working-capital rule** (`bpl-letter-1965`), the framework's only explicit valuation hurdle | **Fails.** Price $46.56 against NWC of $1.06 a share; strict net-net is negative. No margin of safety. |
| **"Sensible price" / "makes sense if the future resembles the past"** (`brk-letter-1981#3`, `brk-letter-1987#18`) | **Passes in the base case.** Value is about $64 against a $46.56 price, a margin of about 27% and an expected return of about 13–14% against a bond assumed at 9%. |
| **"Substantial excess of value" to a private owner** (`bpl-letter-1962#4`) | **Moderate, not large.** The margin depends on the franchise continuing to grow, not on assets. It disappears in the pessimistic case, where the price is about 10% above value. |

Unlike a net-net purchase, the margin of safety here rests on the Stage 2 judgment that the moat will last, not on the balance sheet. In the pessimistic case the expected return still roughly matches the assumed bond yield, about 8% against 9%, and capital is not permanently lost. That matters for the §9 rule on avoiding permanent loss. Whether a margin of about 27%, resting on durability rather than assets, is enough is the question for the decision stage.

## 6. Catalyst and timing (framework §6)

- **No catalyst needed** (`brk-letter-1987`): "The market may ignore business success for a while, but eventually will confirm it." The case rests on owner earnings compounding in the low-to-mid teens. No event is required. **Applies, and supports the case.**
- **Work-out** (`bpl-letter-1957`): No announced sale, merger, liquidation or tender exists. The buyback program, with 14.4M of its 40M shares still to buy through 1990, is not a work-out, though it raises a holder's percentage ownership over time. **Not applicable.**
- **Control** (`bpl-letter-1963`): At a market value of $16.5B, Berkshire cannot buy control, and the stock is neither neglected nor cheap by quantitative measures. Any position would be the kind of non-controlling partnership with good managers described in `brk-letter-1981#3`. **Not applicable.**
- **Timing and market forecasting** (`bpl-letter-1966-mid`, `brk-letter-1987`):
  - The stock is near the top of its 52-week range of $35.04–49.12, and the 1988 low came in the months after the crash.
  - This is not a moment of general fear (`brk-letter-1986`), so there is no contrarian discount on offer.
  - Nor is it greed: the multiple is lower than at the 1987 peak while earnings are higher.
  - The framework says to judge the price against value, not to wait for a better entry. "Mr. Market is there to serve you, not to guide you." The price matters only in relation to the value range above.

## Missing data the valuation would want

1. **The long-term government bond yield on 1989-02-27.** The 9% hurdle is my assumption, inferred from the company's own debt coupons.
2. **Berkshire's investable capital and other opportunities at this date.** This is needed for the opportunity-cost comparison and for sizing.
3. **Maintenance capital spending and constant-currency earnings.** These would narrow the owner-earnings range ($2.44–3.05) and remove the weak-dollar contribution to recent growth.
4. **Private-market prices paid for concentrate or brand franchises.** These would give an outside check on the private-owner value.
5. **Full market values of the affiliates, especially Columbia Pictures.**

## Stage 4 conclusion

Coca-Cola fails the framework's only stated valuation rule, the net-working-capital test, by a wide margin. It passes the "outstanding business at a sensible price" standard that the framework adopted by 1987.

On owner earnings of about $2.85 a share, if the next decade resembles the last, value is about $64 a share against a price of $46.56: a margin of about 27% and an expected return of about 13–14% a year against an assumed 9% bond. The price implies growth well below the company's own record. If management's mid-teens growth continues, value is about $90.

The margin of safety depends on the moat lasting, not on assets, and it disappears in a pessimistic case. No catalyst is needed under §6, and the framework rules out waiting for a better entry price.
