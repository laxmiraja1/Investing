# Memo: Purchase of The Coca-Cola Company common stock (as of February 27, 1989)

We recommend building a position in The Coca-Cola Company in the open market, aiming for about a quarter of the portfolio, at prices near the current $46.56. This memo explains what the business is, why it meets our standards, what we think it is worth, and what would make us stop buying or sell.

The business is simpler than its size suggests. Coca-Cola makes syrup and concentrate and sells them to more than 1,000 independent and partly owned bottlers in over 160 countries. The bottlers carry the heavy capital: plants, trucks and vending machines. The company owns the trademarks and runs the marketing. Its products account for about 45% of the world's carbonated soft drinks, more than twice the share of any competitor, and that share has risen from under 38% in 1980. In 1988 the system sold over 559 million servings a day. Soft drinks now produce more than 90% of operating income. The rest is a juice business and minority stakes in bottlers and in Columbia Pictures.

The company passes our filters. It is not a turnaround: earnings per share have risen every year for more than a decade, from $1.01 in 1978 to $2.85 in 1988, and net income has passed $1 billion. It needs no technology we cannot understand. It is large enough that a position can matter.

The one point of friction is debt. Management deliberately borrows up to 35% of net capital and has used borrowed money to buy back stock. We prefer businesses with little debt. Here the business itself doesn't need the borrowing: it generated $1.17 billion of operating cash in 1988 against $387 million of capital spending, and operating income covers interest about seven times. So we treated the debt as something to adjust for, not a reason to reject.

The test we care most about is return on capital without the help of leverage. Reported return on equity was 33% in 1988. Part of that comes from buybacks and debt, so we took it apart. On net capital the company earns about 25%. The soft-drink assets alone earn about 44% before tax. From 1978 to 1983, when debt was less than 15% of capital, the company still earned over 20% on equity, and that was while it carried lower-return food and film businesses.

The source of those returns is visible. Gross margin rose from about 50% to 56% between 1986 and 1988, even as advertising spending climbed. The company's contracts with its largest bottler let it set prices at its own discretion. The episode in 1985, when consumers forced the return of the original formula, shows how attached customers are to the product.

The advantage also has room to grow. Outside the United States, people drink only about a sixth as many of the company's soft drinks per person as Americans do. International volume has grown 7–12% a year.

Management has used retained earnings well. Since the current team took over in 1981, each dollar kept in the business has been followed by about $5 of market value. Even if we assume no change in the price-to-earnings multiple, the figure is about $2.60. The managers have sold or spun off businesses outside their core, including plastics, pasta, coffee and most of the film business. They measure investments against an explicit cost of capital. They have bought back about 69 million shares at an average near $31.50.

We do have reservations about management. The annual reports read more like promotion than we would like. Executive pay is tied to the share price rather than to returns on capital. And the company has never told shareholders what its venture into films actually earned. None of these outweighs the record.

On valuation, we should be clear that this purchase does not meet our old bargain standard of buying below net working capital. Working capital is about $1 a share against a $46.56 price. That standard was designed for cheap, ordinary companies. It cannot recognize value that sits in a trademark rather than on the balance sheet. The more relevant question is whether the price makes sense if the next decade resembles the last.

We estimate owner earnings at about $2.85 a share, roughly equal to reported earnings, because the business needs little reinvestment. Under the following assumptions, the shares are worth about $64:

- earnings grow 11% a year, the 1978–88 rate;
- the stock ends the decade at 15 times earnings;
- future cash flows are discounted at an assumed 9% long-term interest rate.

That is about 27% above today's price and implies a return of 13–14% a year. If management's expected mid-teens growth holds, the value is near $90. If growth slows to 8% and the multiple falls, the value drops to about $42. Even in that case we would earn about 8% a year, not suffer a permanent loss. The current price implies growth well below what the company has already delivered. The stock also trades below its 1987 peak even though earnings are 17% higher. We need no event or catalyst; if the business keeps compounding, the price should eventually follow.

We are stopping short of the maximum position size for three reasons:

- About three-quarters of operating income is earned abroad, and a weaker dollar has flattered recent growth.
- The company's use of debt exceeds our preference.
- Our margin of safety depends on the advantage lasting, not on assets we could sell.

We intend to hold the shares indefinitely. A higher price alone would not be a reason to sell. We would reconsider if:

- market share or concentrate margins erode for several years in a row;
- management returns to large acquisitions outside soft drinks, or issues or buys back stock at unfavorable prices;
- borrowing grows to fund non-core uses;
- we find management less candid than the record suggests.

We would stop adding shares if the price approached our estimate of value before the position is built, or if long-term interest rates turn out well above the 9% we assumed.
