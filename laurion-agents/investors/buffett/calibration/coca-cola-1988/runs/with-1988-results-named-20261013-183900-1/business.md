# Stage 2: Business analysis (framework §3)

The three rules in §3 are applied in order: return on equity first, then the moat, then owner earnings. All figures come from the packet. Per-share amounts are post-split.

## 3.1 Return on equity, not EPS growth, is the primary test (`brk-letter-1979`)

The rule asks for a high return on equity earned "without undue leverage, accounting gimmickry, etc." So the reported return has to be tested on each of those points.

**Reported ROE, from the 1988 AR selected data (restated basis):**

| | 1978 | 1980 | 1982 | 1984 | 1985 | 1986 | 1987 | 1988 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Net income / avg common equity | 22.6% | 21.1% | 20.3% | 22.2% | 25.3% | 29.1% | 27.5% | 33.3% |
| Total debt / total capital | 3.8% | 9.3% | 15.1% | 32.3% | 30.3% | 34.7% | 48.4% | 38.8% |

The 1988 figure checks out. Net income available to common was $1,038.3M. Average common equity was about $3,116M: year-end 1988 equity of $3,345.3M less the $300M preferred, averaged with restated 1987 equity of $3,187.2M. That gives 33.3%.

**Leverage test.** ROE rose by about 11 points over the decade. In the same period debt rose from about 4% to about 39% of capital, and buybacks reduced equity (treasury stock at cost went from $604M to $2,069M). Part of the rise is therefore financial engineering, not better business economics. Two ways to remove it:

- **History.** From 1978 to 1983 the company carried debt of only 4–15% of capital. It still earned roughly 20–23% on equity, and that was while it also held lower-return foods and, from 1982, entertainment businesses. The underlying business was already earning above 20% without leverage.
- **Unlevered 1988 return.** Net debt was about $893M: total debt of $2,124M less $1,231M of cash and securities. Net capital was therefore about $4,238M. Net interest expense was $31M ($230.5M paid less $199.3M received), or about $21M after tax at 34%. Adding that back to net income gives about $1,065M, a return of about **25% on net capital**. Leverage and buybacks therefore account for about 8 of the 33 points.

**Where the capital earns its return.** The 1988 segment data (`ar-1988`, note 15) splits the business sharply:

| 1988 | Operating income | Identifiable assets | Pre-tax return on assets |
|---|---|---|---|
| Soft drinks, international | $1,338.8M | $2,097.1M | ~64% |
| Soft drinks, USA | $351.9M | $1,711.9M | ~21% |
| Soft drinks combined | $1,690.7M | $3,809.0M | ~44% (~29% after a 34% tax) |
| Foods | $89.3M | $694.1M | ~13% |
| Investments in affiliates | $48.3M equity income | $1,912.0M | ~2.5% |

Soft-drink returns on gross assets have risen each year: about 36% in 1986 excluding the $180M provisions, about 34% in 1987, and about 44% in 1988. The consolidated ROE is a blend. A very high-return concentrate business sits alongside about $1.9B in bottlers and Columbia Pictures that earned little in 1988.

The bottlers are the capital-intensive layer. Coca-Cola Enterprises earned $142.7M on about $1.8B of net assets in 1988, roughly 8%, and only about 6% in 1987. The low returns on those investments are not a gimmick. They do mean the consolidated figure understates what the franchise itself earns. They also raise a question about where retained capital is going, which belongs to Stage 4.

**Accounting check.** Non-recurring items were material in 1986. That year included a $375M pre-tax gain on the CCE stock sale (+$0.68 per share) and $180M of provisions (−$0.33), so recurring EPS was about $2.07, not $2.42. In 1987 the $40M TCC gain and the $36M Foods provision roughly offset. 1988 had smaller items in both directions: a $25M BCI gain, a share of CCE's $104M gain, and CPE's $114M write-down and exchange losses passing through equity income. Goodwill is only $57M, so book equity is essentially tangible. The packet shows no sign of earnings manufactured through accounting. The repeated gains from selling stakes in bottlers should be treated as non-operating.

**EPS growth, recorded for completeness.** From 1985 to 1988 EPS rose 55% ($1.84 to $2.85) while net income rose 45%. Buybacks cut average shares from 393.4M to 364.6M, and that accounts for the gap. Under the rule this matters less than the ROE evidence above.

**Finding for 3.1:** The test is passed. The core business earns very high returns on the capital it actually uses. The 33% headline is somewhat inflated by debt and buybacks, but the figure after removing them (about 25% on net capital, and much higher in soft drinks) is still exceptional.

## 3.2 Durable competitive advantage: the moat (`brk-letter-1986#16`)

The GEICO example defines the moat as a measurable gap between this company's economics and its competitors'. The packet offers the following evidence.

1. **Scale and share.** The company has about 45% of world flavored carbonated soft-drink sales outside China and the USSR, and 47% internationally. In 1988 it sold over 200 billion servings, and "no other company sold even half as much" (`ar-1988`). World share rose from under 38% in 1980 to about 45%; the company says each share point is worth over $1B in retail sales. US share is over 40%, nine points ahead of the primary competitor in 1986 (`ar-1986`). US fountain share was 59% in 1986, more than twice the nearest rival (`ar-1987`).
2. **Pricing power and gross margin.** US concentrate earned about a 63% gross margin in 1986 (`ar-1986`). New contracts with CCE let the company "adjust prices at its discretion", and it used that right in October 1986. Consolidated gross margin rose from 50.5% (1985–86, restated) to 52.6% (1987) and 55.6% (1988). That happened while media advertising rose from $648M to $842M. Management states that it can "adjust prices to compensate for increasing costs" (`ar-1988`).
3. **Trademark.** The 1988 Landor surveys ranked Coca-Cola as the best-known and most admired brand, with the gap to #2 larger than the gap from #2 to #10. The 1985 reformulation is useful evidence. Consumers rejected the new formula and the company restored the original as Coca-Cola classic, which was the top-selling US soft drink in 1986–88. The customer's attachment is to the product as it was, which suits a business that should not need to change. This fits the 1987 letter's point that "severe change and exceptional returns usually don't mix" (`brk-letter-1987#3`).
4. **Distribution system.** More than 1,000 bottlers in more than 160 countries deliver over 559 million servings a day. The company says it is the "low-cost producer in most of the 155 countries" (`ar-1987`), but gives no cost comparison. This is the claim closest to the GEICO cost moat, and it is the one the packet supports least directly.
5. **Room to grow.** International per-capita consumption was 16% of the US rate in 1986 (`ar-1986`) and about 51 drinks a year in 1988, against 274 in the US in 1987. Mexico (219) and Norway (176) show that much higher levels are possible outside the US. Worldwide volume grew 10%, 6% and 7% in 1986–88, and world share rose while the industry grew. The moat is widening, not just holding.

**Limits on what the packet proves.**

- **Currency.** About 76% of 1988 operating income came from outside the US. Key foreign currencies rose against the dollar by an average of 27% (1986), 16% (1987) and 10% (1988). International operating income grew 21% in 1988 on 7% volume, so a meaningful part of recent growth is translation, not franchise strength. The packet gives no constant-currency figures.
- **Latin America.** Inflation cut volume in Brazil and Argentina in 1988.
- **The tax test from `brk-letter-1986#22`.** The prediction is that a strong, unregulated franchise keeps the benefit of a corporate tax cut. The packet cannot run this test cleanly. The US statutory rate fell from 46% to 34%, but most profit is taxed abroad, so the effective rate stayed around 33.5–35.1%.
- **History of diversification.** Earlier diversification into films and foods earned much lower returns: Foods made about 13% pre-tax on assets and had restructurings in 1987. The core franchise was strong enough to carry these businesses. The film business has since been spun off.

**Finding for 3.2:** The moat is real and visible in the numbers: share leadership, high and rising concentrate margins, and discretionary pricing. Its long-run durability rests mainly on the brand and the bottler system, not on a measured cost gap.

## 3.3 Owner earnings (`brk-letter-1986`)

Owner earnings are (a) reported earnings, plus (b) depreciation, amortization and other non-cash charges, less (c) the average capital spending needed to maintain the business.

1988 figures (`ar-1988`, cash-flow statement):

| Item | $M |
|---|---|
| (a) Net income | 1,044.7 |
| less preferred dividends | (6.4) |
| (b) Depreciation and amortization | 169.8 |
| (b) Deferred income taxes | 43.9 |
| Subtotal | ~1,252 |
| (c) Capital spending: 1988 / 1987 / 1986 | 387 / 304 / 346 (3-yr avg ~346) |

Item (c) is the problem. The packet does not separate maintenance capital spending from growth spending. It gives three facts: much of the spending is described as growth (capacity expansion, coolers and vending machines in Asia, new plants in China and France); management says current-cost income "would be less than reported" but gives no figure; and the LIFO reserve is only $30M.

- **Lower bound** (all capital spending counted as maintenance): about $1,252M − $387M = **~$865M**, or about $2.44 per share on 354.8M shares.
- **Upper bound** (maintenance equal to depreciation): about **~$1,080M**, or about $3.05 per share.
- **Cross-check.** Reported cash from operations was $1,166.8M. Less all $387M of capital spending, free cash flow after growth spending was about **$780M**, after working-capital changes and $36M of undistributed equity income.

A working estimate is owner earnings of about **$0.95–1.05B, close to reported net income** of roughly $2.70–2.95 per share. Reported earnings are a fair approximation of what owners can take out, which is the result the rule is meant to confirm. The soft-drink business is not using up cash to sustain itself. Two qualifications apply. First, about $1.9B of capital in affiliates contributes only $48M of equity income, so the earnings attributable to that capital are thin. Second, if the dollar strengthens, translation would reduce reported earnings.

## Facts the framework would want that the packet lacks

1. **Maintenance versus growth capital spending**, and the current-cost depreciation figure that management mentions but does not quantify. This determines whether owner earnings are nearer $0.87B or $1.08B.
2. **Constant-currency operating income** for the international business, to separate franchise growth from the weaker dollar.
3. **Price, mix and volume breakdown**: concentrate price per gallon over time by major market, to measure pricing power directly instead of inferring it from gross margin.
4. **Competitor economics**: the primary competitor's share, margins and cost per case, to confirm the "low-cost producer" claim, which would be the GEICO-style measure.
5. **Long-run soft-drink segment returns.** Clean segment data runs only from 1985 to 1988 because of restatements; earlier years mix in foods and entertainment.
6. **Bottler contract terms in full.** The packet says most non-CCE US bottlers are on formula pricing for Coca-Cola syrup, which limits US pricing discretion outside CCE.
7. **Fair value of the affiliate stakes.** Only the excess over carrying value of the publicly traded ones is disclosed (+$291M). The value of the Columbia stake is unclear.
8. **Interim 1988 detail.** The 10-Qs are absent; only the UPI summaries are available.

## Stage 2 conclusion

The business passes all three §3 tests.

- **ROE:** The core concentrate business earns exceptional returns on modest capital, roughly 44% pre-tax on soft-drink assets and about 25% on net capital for the whole company with leverage removed. This record goes back to the low-debt years before 1984.
- **Moat:** The moat is visible in share, gross-margin expansion and pricing discretion, and it is widening internationally.
- **Owner earnings:** Owner earnings are close to reported earnings, at about $1B.

Points to carry into Stage 3:

- The consolidated return is diluted by low-return bottler and film investments and inflated by debt-financed buybacks.
- Recent growth is overstated by the weak dollar.
- How management deploys retained earnings is the open question, to be examined under the $1-retained test in Stage 4.
