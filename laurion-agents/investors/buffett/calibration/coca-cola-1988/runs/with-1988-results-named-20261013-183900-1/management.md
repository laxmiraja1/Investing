# Stage 3: Management and capital allocation (framework §4)

The packet contains six years of reports from the Goizueta and Keough team (1981–1988, with full reports for 1986, 1987 and 1988). This stage applies the three §4 rules in order: candid management in place, buybacks only well below value, and $1 of market value for each $1 retained. It then covers the acquisition record and compensation, which the framework also weighs.

## 4.1 Honest, able management already in place; candid reporting (`brk-letter-1979`; standard in `brk-letter-1983#2`)

**In place.** Roberto Goizueta (Chairman and CEO) and Donald Keough (President and COO) assembled the team in 1981 and have run the company under a written "Strategy for the 1980s" ever since (`ar-1987`, `ar-1988`). M. Douglas Ivester is CFO. This meets the "management in place (we can't supply it)" criterion (`brk-letter-1986#10`). Succession is not discussed anywhere in the packet.

**Ability, judged by results.**
- Return on equity rose from about 21–22% (1980–81) to 33% (1988).
- World share rose from under 38% to about 45% (`ar-1988`).
- Soft-drink returns on assets rose each year (Stage 2).

**Ability, judged by stated discipline.** Management states its economics in terms close to the framework's:
- It holds investments to a hurdle: they must produce "long-term cash returns that exceed the Company's weighted average cost of capital", which it estimates at about 12% after tax (`ar-1986`, `ar-1987`, `ar-1988`).
- It describes the core business plainly: it "has a low capital requirement and offers a high return on investment" (`ar-1988`).
- It has acted on that. It has taken capital out of low-return businesses: plastics sold for $112M (1985), Ronco pasta sold (1984), the entertainment business spun into a 49% affiliate with shares distributed to holders (1987–88), and coffee sold (January 1989). More than 90% of operating income now comes from soft-drink concentrate.

**Candor. The test is whether the reports tell owners what they would want to know if the positions were reversed.**

Evidence for:
- One-time items are separated and quantified per share every year: the $375M CCE gain (+$0.68), the $180M provisions (−$0.33), the $40M TCC gain and the $36M Foods charge (`ar-1986`, `ar-1987`).
- Segment and geographic data are given, including identifiable assets, which let Stage 2 compute returns.
- Currency effects are disclosed every year, with percentage moves by country.
- The company admits weak results. The 1986 report says "we were not satisfied with the performance of Columbia Pictures' feature films", and the 1987 report says the foods business "is obviously in transition."
- It volunteers points against itself:
  - Purchasing-power gains from its net monetary liabilities "do not necessarily imply that incurring more debt would be beneficial."
  - Income measured on a current-cost basis "would be less than reported."
  - The new FASB 96 tax rule could cut equity by about $175M (`ar-1988`).

Evidence against:
- **Promotional tone.** Phrases such as "the potential appears limitless", "nearly unlimited potential", and the claim that the Coca-Cola system adds "$250 billion to the world economy" using a "multiplier factor of five" (`ar-1987`) are the kind of public-relations writing the framework says owners should not be given (`brk-letter-1979`).
- **Emphasis on the stock price.** The reports dwell on the stock price and total shareholder return: "the value of our stock has quadrupled", and the split put the price "in a range attractive to... investors" (`ar-1986`). They say less about intrinsic value per share, which the framework treats as the measure that counts (`brk-letter-1983#0`).
- **No accounting for the entertainment venture.** The 1986 report promoted entertainment as a growth sector. A year later it was folded into CPE and partly distributed. No report says what the 1982–87 entertainment investment earned on the capital put into it. CPE then wrote down $114M of film inventory and contributed equity losses in 1988.
- **Frequent restatements.** CCE (1986), CPE and TCC (1987) and CCFC (1988) were each restated. All are disclosed and audited, but the restatements make it hard to follow the core business across years.

**Finding for 4.1:** Management is able, economically literate and in place. Its reporting is accurate and fairly complete on figures. Its tone is more promotional than the framework prefers, and it has not openly accounted for the entertainment mistake.

## 4.2 Buybacks only well below intrinsic value (`brk-letter-1984`)

Repurchase record, with average prices computed from the cash cost and share counts in the equity statements (`ar-1986`, `ar-1987`, `ar-1988`):

| Year | Shares bought | Cost | Avg price | P/E on that year's EPS* |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1984 | 18.4M | $335.7M | ~$18.24 | ~11.6x ($1.57) |
| 1985 | 15.0M | $379.9M | ~$25.33 | ~14.7x ($1.72) |
| 1986 | 2.9M | $110.5M | ~$38.37 | ~18.5x (~$2.07 recurring) |
| 1987 | 14.2M | $605.1M | ~$42.70 | ~17.6x ($2.43) |
| 1988 | 19.0M | $758.2M | ~$39.94 | ~14.0x ($2.85) |
| **Total** | **~69.4M** | **~$2.19B** | **~$31.5** | |

*Continuing-operations EPS; 1986 excludes the CCE gain and the provisions.

Every lot is below today's price of $46.56. The buybacks cut average shares from 396.6M (1984) to 364.6M (1988), and year-end shares were 354.8M.

The 1988 buyback was bought at about a 7.1% earnings yield ($2.85 / $39.94). The figure is about 6.8–7.6% on the Stage 2 estimate of owner earnings. The underlying earnings were growing in the mid-teens, from a business that earns very high returns on any capital it adds. That is very likely well below intrinsic value. Whether it was *well* below is a valuation question for Stage 4.

Two qualifications under the framework:
1. **The stated reason is not the framework's reason.** Management says buybacks began after it "determined that the Company's available cash and debt capacity exceeded the funds required" and will continue "as long as investing in our own stock remains one of the best uses of our excess cash" (`ar-1987`, `ar-1988`). The 1988 wording implies a comparison with other uses of cash. But management never says it buys only when the price is well below per-share value. The 1987 purchases averaged about $42.70 and included the period before the October crash, when the stock traded at $48–53.
2. **The buybacks were partly borrowed.** Short-term debt rose about $1.2B in 1987 "due principally to the funding of the share repurchase program" and bottler stakes. The company also issued $300M of money-market preferred in 1988. Management believes it should "utilize prudent amounts of debt to lower its overall cost of capital" up to 35% net debt to net capital. That differs from Berkshire's preference for little or no debt (Stage 1). It is not dishonest, but it is a different philosophy, and it raises the reported ROE (Stage 2).

**Share issuance (`brk-letter-1982#17`).** In 1985 the company issued about 7.1M shares for Embassy, worth about $169M, or about $23.80 a share. That was roughly 13–14x earnings. Measured against what the stock later proved to be worth, the company paid in cheap currency for an entertainment business it later left. The amount was about 1.8% of shares. This is a small value leak, not a pattern; there has been no other equity issuance since.

## 4.3 Each $1 retained must produce at least $1 of market value (`brk-letter-1983`)

The test runs from the start of the current management's tenure: year-end 1980 to year-end 1988. Figures are from the selected financial data in `ar-1988`.

**Per share:**
- EPS for 1981–88 sums to $15.12: 1.30 + 1.32 + 1.37 + 1.59 + 1.84 + 2.42 + 2.43 + 2.85.
- Distributions sum to $8.66: cash dividends of $7.76 (0.77 + 0.83 + 0.89 + 0.92 + 0.99 + 1.04 + 1.12 + 1.20) plus the CPE in-kind dividend of $0.90.
- Earnings retained therefore total about **$6.46 per share**.
- The share price rose from $11.13 to $44.63, a gain of **$33.50**.
- That is about **$5.20 of market value per $1 retained.**

**Aggregate:** Market value rose from about $4.14B (372M × $11.13) to about $15.8B, a gain of roughly $11.7B. Retained earnings were about $2.5B: net income of $5.79B less roughly $3.3B of cash and in-kind dividends (1981–83 dividends estimated from the reported payout ratios). That is about **$4.70 per $1**, even though about $2.2B of the cash was spent shrinking the share count.

**Robustness check.** Part of the price gain is the market paying more per dollar of earnings: the P/E went from about 9.8x on 1980 EPS of $1.14 to about 15.7x on 1988's $2.85. Holding the 1980 P/E constant gives a 1988 price of about $27.90. The gain would then be about $16.80 against $6.46 retained, or about **$2.60 per $1**. EPS also rose by $1.71 on the $6.46 retained, about a 26% return on the retained capital, although debt, buybacks and the weaker dollar all helped.

**Finding for 4.3:** The test is passed by a wide margin under either measure. Lowering the payout target to 40% to retain more is justified by this record, as long as the incremental dollars keep going to uses like the past ones.

The pattern runs in the right direction. The diversifying moves of the early 1980s have been unwound, and the marginal dollar now goes to the core franchise and to buying back stock.

## Compensation (judged against the ROE test)

The notes in `ar-1988` show:
- Options outstanding rose to 8.66M shares, about 2.4% of shares, after a large grant of 4.18M in 1988 (only 1.1M in 1987).
- 511,500 restricted shares were granted in 1988.
- Under Performance Unit Agreements, officers receive cash based on the gain in 555,000 shares above $20.63. With the stock at $46.56, that is about $14M of gain.
- Incentive Units are tied to the value of 300,000 shares.

Dilution is small next to the buybacks. But the incentives reward the stock price, not return on equity or per-share value. The framework's primary test is ROE, not the stock price, so this is a mild flag. The packet has no salary or bonus figures and no proxy statement.

## What would have been relied on that the packet lacks

1. **Personal assessment of the managers.** Comparable judgments were built on long acquaintance: "Our admiration for the management of Capital Cities... has been expressed several times" (`brk-letter-1984#31`), and "she gave us her word" with Mrs. Blumkin (`brk-letter-1984#7`). Nothing in the packet substitutes for direct knowledge of Goizueta's and Keough's integrity, of how they would treat a large holder, or of their candor in private.
2. **The proxy statement:** management and director holdings ("we eat our own cooking", `brk-letter-1983#0`), full pay, bonus formulas, and option terms.
3. **The board's independence and relationships.** The board includes an investment banker and several large-company CEOs; their ties are not described.
4. **A full account of Columbia Pictures:** total cash invested, cash recovered, and the realized return.
5. **How management decides buyback prices.** Does it compare price to an internal estimate of intrinsic value, or only to excess cash and borrowing capacity?
6. **Reference checks** with bottlers and others who have dealt with management (`brk-letter-1986#10`).

## Stage 3 conclusion

Management passes §4, with reservations.

**The strongest evidence:** each $1 retained has produced about $5 of market value, or about $2.60 even with no change in the P/E. Capital has been moved out of low-return diversifications and back into the high-return franchise. Management states and uses a cost-of-capital discipline. Its reports quantify the bad along with the good.

**The reservations:**
- A leverage philosophy at odds with the framework's.
- Buybacks justified by "excess cash" rather than explicitly by a discount to value, and partly debt-funded.
- A promotional style and a focus on the stock price in both the reports and the pay plans.
- An entertainment venture that was never openly accounted for.
- Low-return bottler stakes that make sense mainly as protection for the moat.

None of these is disqualifying. Whether the buybacks, and a purchase today, are "well below intrinsic value" is the question for valuation in Stage 4.
