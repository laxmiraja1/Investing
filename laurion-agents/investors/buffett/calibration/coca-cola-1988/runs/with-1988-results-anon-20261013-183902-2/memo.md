**Memo: Purchase of Company A (AAA) common stock, February 1989**

We recommend buying Company A, the world's largest soft drink company, at about $46.56 a share. We would build the position to roughly a quarter of our portfolio through open-market purchases and expect to hold it indefinitely.

The core business is simple. Company A makes syrup and concentrate and sells them to bottlers, who bottle, can and distribute the drinks under its trademarks. Soft drinks produce more than 90% of operating income. Two smaller pieces remain. A juice business earns about 5% of operating income and is worse, because orange prices swing widely. Minority stakes in bottlers and in a film and television company are carried at about $1.9 billion and earned only 2.5% in 1988.

The company passes each of our screens.
- **Record.** It is not a turnaround: earnings per share rose every year from $0.84 in 1977 to $2.85 in 1988.
- **Debt.** It does not depend on debt to operate. In 1988 operating cash flow was $1.17 billion against $387 million of capital spending, so the business funds itself several times over.
- **Simplicity.** There is no technology to understand.
- **Size.** At a market value of $16.5 billion, a meaningful stake can be bought without moving the price.

The return on equity was impressive before any leverage. From 1978 to 1981 the company earned 21% to 23% on equity while debt was under 10% of capital and cash exceeded debt. Today it reports 33%. We discount that figure, because part comes from borrowing and from buybacks that have shrunk the equity base. Measured on equity plus net debt, the whole company earned about 24% in 1988. Excluding the low-yielding affiliates, the soft drink business earned roughly 40% after tax on the capital it uses. Very few large companies sustain anything close to that.

That return is protected by a brand and distribution system no competitor can reproduce, and the protection is widening.
- **Share.** World market share rose from under 38% in 1981 to nearly 45% in 1988, more than double the nearest rival. The company holds 59% of the US fountain business.
- **Margins and pricing.** Gross margin on concentrate is about 63%. In Latin America in 1988 the company raised operating income 17% on 1% volume growth, which shows it can raise prices.
- **Durability.** When management changed the flagship formula in 1985, consumers forced the original back. Within a year it was again the best-selling soft drink in the country. A franchise that survives its owner's mistakes is durable.
- **Room to grow.** Consumption outside the US is about 51 servings per person a year, against 274 in the US.

Management has used retained earnings well on the measure we care most about. From 1981 through 1988 the company kept about $6.46 a share of earnings. Over the same period the stock rose $33.50. Even after removing the rise in the price-earnings ratio over that period, the gain is about $2.60 of market value for each dollar retained. The company has also repurchased about 15% of its shares since 1984, at 12 to 18 times earnings, prices we consider well below value.

We have reservations about management:
- The move into films and television was a detour from a simple, superior business. It is now being unwound at modest cost.
- Buybacks have been justified as using "excess cash" rather than as buying below value, and some were funded with debt.
- Stock option grants rose sharply in 1988.
- The annual reports are more promotional than we would like, though the financial sections are candid.

None of these outweighs the record. We would still want to meet the managers before committing fully.

On valuation, net working capital is about $1.06 a share, so the stock is no bargain by that test. That test applies to mediocre companies selling below their assets, not to a business of this quality, so the relevant measure is what a private owner would pay for the earnings available to owners.
- **Owner earnings.** We estimate about $2.60 a share for 1988. That is reported earnings plus depreciation, less a range for the capital spending needed to maintain the business.
- **Value.** If owner earnings grow 8% to 15% a year for a decade, then 4% a year after that, and are discounted at about 9%, the business is worth roughly $70 to $125 a share. Our central estimate is about $100.
- **What the price implies.** Today's price assumes growth of only about 2% a year, against roughly 12% over the past eight years and management's guidance of mid-teens growth for 1989.
- **Margin of safety.** The central case gives a margin of about 50%. The margin disappears only if a 12% discount rate is combined with growth below about 8.5%. We think that unlikely.
- **Recent pricing.** In August 1987 the market valued this business at 21 times earnings. Today it pays 16 times, after earnings have risen 17%.

No catalyst is required; the value rests on the business continuing to compound.

We size the position at about 25% rather than the 40% we would accept in the clearest cases, for three reasons. We do not know how much of capital spending is maintenance. About three-quarters of operating income is earned abroad, and a stronger dollar would reduce it. Debt has risen by management's choice, though it is not yet troubling.

We would change our view on any of the following:
- evidence that owner earnings are well below $2.30 a share, or that recent growth came mostly from currency;
- a sustained loss of market share or concentrate margin;
- management redirecting capital into unrelated acquisitions, or issuing shares for less value than it receives;
- leverage beyond its own 35% ceiling for purposes other than cheap buybacks;
- long-term interest rates well above 10%.

A higher stock price alone would not be a reason to sell.
