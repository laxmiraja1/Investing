# Stage 3: Management and capital allocation

Management passes, with reservations. Since 1981 every dollar retained has produced well over a dollar of market value, even after removing the rise in the stock's P/E. The company has bought back about 15% of its shares at 12–18 times earnings, and it has refocused on the core business. The reservations are:
- the entertainment detour of 1982–87;
- buybacks justified as "excess cash" rather than price below value, and partly funded with debt;
- a sharp increase in stock option grants in 1988;
- annual reports whose front sections read like public relations.

None of these is disqualifying. The judgment of the managers' character, which the framework weighs heavily, needs information the packet lacks (listed at the end).

## 4a. Honest, able management already in place; candid reporting (`brk-letter-1979`, `brk-letter-1983#2`)

**In place and able.** The same team has run the company since 1981 (`ar-1987` letter: "When we assembled our present management team in 1981"). Over that time:
- return on equity rose from about 22% to 33%, and on unlevered net capital from about 21% to about 24% (Stage 2);
- world market share rose from under 38% to nearly 45%;
- earnings per share rose from $1.30 to $2.85.

Management also sets itself a stated hurdle: a 12% after-tax cost of capital for investments (`ar-1986`, `ar-1987`, `ar-1988`). That is the kind of yardstick the framework's retention test assumes.

**Candor, positives.** These are the business facts an owner would want, presented without spin:
- The 1986 letter puts the $375M CCE gain and the $180M of provisions in its opening paragraph. The 1987 letter strips out "35 cents per share of unusual items in 1986" to show underlying growth.
- The reports admit problems: "we were not satisfied with the performance of the film studio's feature films" (`ar-1986`), "Our foods business is obviously in transition" (`ar-1987`), and two years of orange juice volume declines (`ar-1988`).
- The financial review discloses:
  - the 63% gross margin on concentrate;
  - the cooperative advertising paid to CCE;
  - that current-cost income would be lower than reported;
  - that inflation gains on monetary liabilities "do not necessarily imply that incurring more debt would be beneficial."

**Candor, negatives.** The front sections are a "public relations document" of the kind `brk-letter-1979` disdains:
- a "$250 billion" contribution to the world economy built on a "multiplier factor of five" (`ar-1987`);
- hospital anecdotes and "off the charts" quotations (`ar-1988`);
- heavy use of "increasing shareholder value," the phrase `brk-letter-1984#4` calls "this season's favorite."

That passage says the market discounts such words unless they are "demonstrated." Here they are, through buybacks (below). On balance the reporting is candid where it matters, in the financial review, and promotional elsewhere.

## 4b. Buybacks only well below intrinsic value (`brk-letter-1984#4`)

From the treasury stock and share-repurchase lines in the equity statements:

| Year | Shares bought (M) | Cost ($M) | Average price | Price / EPS that year |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1984 | 18.4 | 335.7 | ~$18.2 | ~11.6x ($1.57) |
| 1985 | 15.0 | 379.9 | ~$25.3 | ~14.7x ($1.72) |
| 1986 | 2.9 | 110.5 | ~$38.4 | ~18.5x (underlying ~$2.07) |
| 1987 | 14.2 | 605.1 | ~$42.7 | ~17.6x ($2.43) |
| 1988 | 19.0 | 758.2 | ~$39.9 | ~14.0x ($2.85) |

- Treasury stock at end-1988 is 62.6M shares at a cost of $2,069M, about $33 each.
- Average shares outstanding fell from 396M (1984) to 365M (1988). Year-end shares are now 355M.
- The program has been continuous and large. Another 14.4M shares remain authorized through 1990.

**Were the purchases "well below" value?** The test is settled in Stage 4, but the preliminary answer is probably yes. Stage 2 found a franchise earning about 40% after tax on core capital, compounding earnings in the mid-teens, and needing little reinvestment. Buying that at 14x earnings (a 7% earnings yield rising roughly 15% a year) is the "$2 of present value for $1" arithmetic the 1984 letter describes. The most expensive purchases, in 1987 at about 17.6x, look least clear-cut.

**Reservations:**
- **Stated trigger.** Management's stated reason is cash surplus, not price versus value. Repurchases began "after management determined that the Company's available cash and debt capacity exceeded the funds required" (`ar-1986`, `ar-1987`, `ar-1988`). The 1988 report comes closer: it will continue "as long as investing in our own stock remains one of the best uses of our excess cash." Neither says shares are bought because they are cheap relative to intrinsic value.
- **Debt funding.**
  - Management raised its net debt ceiling to 35% of capital partly to fund buybacks (about $300M of the new capacity was earmarked for repurchases, `ar-1986`).
  - In 1987 short-term debt rose about $1.2B, mainly for buybacks and bottler stakes.
  - In 1988, buybacks ($760M) plus dividends ($443M) exceeded operating cash flow ($1,167M). The gap was covered by collecting the $545M CPE note and selling the Miami bottler.
  - Debt then fell from $2,995M to $2,124M. This is borrowing by choice, not need, and appears controlled. Still, it is more leverage than the framework's own practice ("a low amount of leverage," `brk-letter-1979#2`).
- **Issuing shares.** By the standard of `brk-letter-1982#17` (issue shares only for equal value received), the 1985 Embassy purchase used about 7.1M treasury shares worth about $169M at roughly 14x earnings. That swapped part of an exceptional franchise for a television producer. The amount is small but points the wrong way.

## 4c. Each $1 retained must create at least $1 of market value (`brk-letter-1983`)

This is per share, from the beginning of 1981 (when this team took over) to the end of 1988:

| Item | Per share |
|---|---|
| Net income per share, 1981–88 (1.30 + 1.32 + 1.37 + 1.59 + 1.84 + 2.42 + 2.43 + 2.85) | $15.12 |
| Cash dividends (0.77 + 0.83 + 0.89 + 0.92 + 0.99 + 1.04 + 1.12 + 1.20) | $7.76 |
| In-kind dividend of CPE shares | $0.90 |
| **Retained** | **about $6.46** |
| Price at end-1980 → end-1988 ($11.13 → $44.63) | **+$33.50** |

- That is about **$5.20 of market value for each $1 retained**.
- Part of the gain is a higher valuation: the P/E rose from about 9.8x to 15.7x. At the 1980 multiple, the 1988 price would be about $27.90, still a $16.80 gain, or **about $2.60 per $1 retained**.
- The aggregate view agrees. Market value rose from about $4.1B to $15.8B, and about $2.2B more went out through buybacks, against about $2.5B of retained earnings.
- The test passes by a wide margin, and that is the framework's primary evidence of capital-allocation skill.

**Where the retained dollars did poorly:**
- **Entertainment.**
  - The film studio was bought in 1982 (the price is not in the packet). Embassy, Tandem and Merv Griffin followed in 1985–86 (about $645M).
  - The film side then disappointed. It was merged into CPE and 51% was spun out in January 1988.
  - CPE took a $114M write-down; Company A's share of its loss over two and a half months was about $51M.
  - The carrying value has fallen to $598M.
  - Television syndication did earn well (operating income +30% or more for four years), so the result is a detour rather than a disaster. But it ran against the "simple business" discipline. The 1988 report's ">90% of operating income from soft drinks," the coffee sale and the narrower focus in foods suggest management has drawn that lesson.
- **Bottler stakes.**
  - About $1.9B sits in affiliates earning 2.5–6% in equity income (Stage 2).
  - The strategic case is that strong bottlers raise concentrate volume, and the affiliates' market value exceeded carrying value by $291M at end-1988.
  - Even so, these dollars have not earned the core business's returns. Whether they are a toll worth paying to protect the moat is a judgment the packet only partly supports.

**Dividend policy.** Management plans to cut the payout ratio to 40% to reinvest "in opportunities offering returns in excess of the cost of capital." Given the record above that is defensible, provided the extra retention goes into the soft drink core and buybacks rather than another diversification.

## Compensation (no specific rule in the framework; judged by the share-issuance test)

- Options outstanding rose from 5.6M to 8.66M in 1988, after **4.18M granted in one year**, about 1.2% of shares and roughly four times recent annual grants.
- Restricted stock grants rose to 511,500 shares in 1988 (from 178,500 in 1987). Unearned restricted stock now stands at $51.5M.
- Officers hold cash-settled performance units tied to 555,000 shares (base price $20.63) and, since 1988, incentive units tied to 300,000 shares.
- Exercises (about 0.9–1.3M shares a year) are small next to buybacks, so dilution is minor so far.
- The 1988 increase in grants is the trend to watch.
- A new 1988 clause lets restricted stock vest on a change of control.
- The packet has no salaries, bonus formulas or proxy statement, so it is impossible to say whether pay is tied to per-share value or merely to size.

## What the investor would have relied on that the packet lacks

1. **Meeting the managers.** The framework puts great weight on judging "high-grade, talented and likeable" managers (`brk-letter-1987#18`) and their integrity, as with the Blumkins (`brk-letter-1984#7`) and Murphy and Burke (`brk-letter-1984#31`). That judgment is personal, and nothing in an annual report substitutes for it.
2. **How management thinks about intrinsic value when buying back stock.** Whether they would stop at a high price, or keep buying because cash is "excess."
3. **Insider and director ownership.** The framework prizes directors who "eat our own cooking" (`brk-letter-1983#0`). The board list gives no shareholdings.
4. **The proxy statement:** salaries, bonus metrics, option terms and the reason for the 1988 jump in grants.
5. **The price paid for the film studio in 1982, and its full return.** Needed to score the entertainment episode exactly.
6. **Current market values of CCE and CPE.** Needed to test the bottler and entertainment stakes against their cost.
7. **Succession.** Ages and depth behind the two top officers (whose names are redacted in the packet).
8. **Whether the entertainment lesson is permanent.** Only conversation or a longer record would tell whether management has dropped diversification ambitions or merely paused them.
