# Stage 4: Valuation

**Conclusion.** The framework's literal valuation rule, buying below net working capital, rejects the stock by a factor of more than 40. But by 1989 the framework applies that test to cheap, ordinary "generals," not to an outstanding business. For a franchise like this one, the operative question is value to a private owner, built on the owner earnings measured in Stage 2.

On that basis:
- the $46.56 price assumes owner earnings grow only about 2% a year for a decade, against roughly 12% in 1981–88 and mid-teens guidance for 1989;
- intrinsic value is roughly **$70–$125 a share**, central estimate about **$100**;
- the margin of safety is about **35–55%** under reasonable assumptions;
- the margin disappears only if a 12% discount rate and growth below about 8.5% are assumed together.

No catalyst is needed.

## 5. Valuation: the rule as written (`bpl-letter-1965`)

From the end-1988 balance sheet:

| | $M |
|---|---|
| Current assets | 3,245.4 |
| Current liabilities | (2,868.9) |
| Net working capital | 376.5 → **$1.06 per share** on 354.8M shares |
| Current assets less all liabilities ($4,105.3M) and preferred ($300M) | **negative, −$1,160M** |

- The price is about 44 times net working capital per share, and about 5.4 times common book value of $8.58.
- **As a bargain "general," this fails outright.**

**Why the test is not decisive here.** The framework's own later statements limit its scope:
- `brk-letter-1987#18`: "Our goal is to find an outstanding business at a sensible price, not a mediocre business at a bargain price." It describes the earlier bargain-hunting as punishment, "an education in the economics of short-line farm implement manufacturers…"
- `brk-letter-1981#3`: "We may very well pay a fairly fancy price for a Category 1 business if we are reasonably confident of what we are getting."
- `brk-letter-1987#1`: paying "six times book value… does not change CCH's return on equity."
- The surviving yardstick from the partnership years is "value to a private owner" (`bpl-letter-1963#5`, `bpl-letter-1962#4`), and the 1986 letter defines owner earnings as the input for it.

So I value the business from owner earnings. This is a projection of owner earnings, but it is not an unconstrained DCF. It uses the framework's own earnings measure, the past record rather than management's projections (`brk-letter-1982`: "future projections are of little interest to us"), and deliberately conservative ending assumptions.

## Value to a private owner, built on owner earnings

**Inputs**

- **Owner earnings, 1988: $2.60 a share.** This is the middle of Stage 2's $2.45–$2.75 range, on average shares. On the 354.8M year-end shares it would be about $2.68, so $2.60 is conservative.
  - Net debt and the preferred are already reflected, because owner earnings are after interest and after preferred dividends.
- **Discount rate.**
  - The framework names none, and **the packet has no long-term Treasury yield.**
  - I assume about **9%**, as a proxy for the government bond yield. The packet supports roughly that level: the company's own notes carry 9.25–12.75% coupons, its preferred resets at about 6.5–7%, and its other long-term debt averages about 11.8%.
  - As a stress test I use management's own **12%** cost of capital.
- **Growth record**
  - Net income 1981–88: $482M to $1,045M, about 11.7% a year. EPS rose from $1.30 to $2.85, about 11.9% a year.
  - Underlying EPS 1985–88: about 17–18% a year, but helped by buybacks (about 2% a year) and a weak dollar (key currencies rose 10–27% a year).
  - Volume growth runs 6–7% a year. Non-US consumption per person is 51 servings a year against 274 in the US.
  - Management's 1989 guidance is EPS growth "well into the mid-teens."
- **After year 10:** the business is valued at owner earnings × 1.04 / (r − 4%). That is about 21x at a 9% discount rate. As a harsher floor, I also show zero growth after year 10.

**Value per share at a 9% discount rate**

| 10-year owner-earnings growth | PV of years 1–10 | PV of value after year 10 (4% growth) | **Total** | Total if zero growth after year 10 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 8% (below the 1981–88 record) | $24.7 | $49.3 | **$74** | $51 |
| 12% (about the 1981–88 record) | $30.3 | $70.9 | **$101** | $68 |
| 15% (the recent rate and guidance) | $35.3 | $92.4 | **$128** | — |

**Stress test at a 12% discount rate:** 8% growth gives about $45; 12% growth gives about $60.

**What the price implies.**
- At 9%, $46.56 is consistent with owner earnings growing about **2% a year** for ten years, then 4%.
- At 12%, it is consistent with about **8.5% a year**.
- Either way, the market is pricing growth well below the business's recorded rate. That holds even though the per-person figures show the international market is far from saturated, and even though growth needs little capital.

**Cross-check from an owner's yield.**
- At $46.56, the owner-earnings yield is about 5.6%.
- The company already returns about 7.5% of its market value each year: 2.9% in dividends (the $1.36 rate) plus about 4.6% in buybacks (1988: $759M against $16.5B).
- It does this while net income compounds at roughly 10–12% before buybacks.
- With no change in the P/E, an owner's return comes out around 15–17% a year, against a bond near 9% whose coupon never grows.

**Not counted (extra cushion).**
- Affiliate stakes are carried at $1.9B, and listed investees were worth $291M more than that at the end of 1988.
- Their equity income adds only about $0.13 a share to owner earnings. Valuing the stakes at market instead would add roughly $4 a share.

## Margin of safety against the framework's hurdle

**The hurdle.** The framework sets no percentage.
- For bargain generals it demands "a substantial excess of value" and "a comfortable margin of safety" (`bpl-letter-1961#3`).
- For an exceptional business it accepts "a fairly fancy price… if we are reasonably confident" and "a sensible price."
- So the hurdle depends on confidence. Here confidence is high: about 13 years of record, a widening moat, and simple economics (Stages 1–2).

| Case | Intrinsic value | Price as % of value | Margin of safety |
|---|---|---|---|
| Central (9%, 12% growth, 4% after) | ~$100 | 46% | **~54%** |
| Low growth (9%, 8%) | ~$74 | 63% | ~37% |
| Central, zero growth after year 10 | ~$68 | 68% | ~32% |
| Stress (12%, 12%) | ~$60 | 78% | ~22% |
| Double stress (12%, 8%) | ~$45 | 103% | none |

**Verdict.** In every case except pairing a high discount rate with growth well below the record, the price is substantially below private-owner value. That is a sensible price for a Category 1 business with a real margin of safety, and the margin is large in the central case.

**Main risks to the estimate:**
1. **Currency.** About 76% of operating income is earned outside the US, and 1988 was helped by a 10% weaker dollar. A stronger dollar would lower the starting owner earnings, though not the unit economics.
2. **Maintenance capital spending** is not disclosed. In the low case, owner earnings of $2.27 a share cut every value by about 13%.
3. **The rate assumption.** No bond yield is in the packet. The 12% stress test covers this.

## 6. Catalyst / timing

- **No catalyst is needed** (`brk-letter-1987`): "The market may ignore business success for a while, but eventually will confirm it." The value here rests on owner earnings compounding. No corporate event is required.
- **Not a work-out** (`bpl-letter-1957`). There is no pending sale, merger, liquidation or tender. The ongoing buyback (14.4M shares left, about $670M at today's price) adds to per-share value but is not a timed event.
- **Not a control situation** (`bpl-letter-1963`).
  - This is a $16.5B Dow company, not a neglected stock.
  - A stake of a few percent would make Berkshire an influential holder, the "non-voting partnership" model of `brk-letter-1981#3`, but not a controlling one.
- **Mr. Market and temperament** (`brk-letter-1986`, `brk-letter-1987`; no forecasting, `bpl-letter-1966-mid`).
  - In August 1987 the stock was $50.56 at about 21x earnings, near the market peak, when others were greedy.
  - Today it is $46.56 at 16.3x. Earnings per share have risen 17% since then and the price has not.
  - The October 1987 crash and the 1988 flat period (range $35–$45) left the price behind the business.
  - The stock is near its 52-week high. The framework ignores that and judges the price against value, not against recent prices.
- **Timing.** Buy when the price is sensible against value, which it is now. Buying in size takes time, and there is no reason to wait for a lower quote that may not come.

## Figures the framework would want that the packet lacks

- **The long-term government bond yield,** the rate to discount against. I assumed about 9%.
- **Maintenance capital spending,** which sets the starting owner earnings.
- **Constant-currency 1988 operating income,** to know how much of the starting point is currency.
- **Current market prices of CCE and CPE,** to value the affiliate stakes directly.
- **Berkshire's alternative uses of capital and its net worth on the as-of date,** to judge the opportunity cost. This feeds into sizing in the next stage.
