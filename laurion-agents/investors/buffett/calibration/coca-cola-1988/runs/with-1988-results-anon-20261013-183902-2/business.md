# Stage 2: Business analysis

The core concentrate business earns roughly 40% after tax on the capital it actually uses, and it has done so without needing debt. The moat is wide and has been widening. Owner earnings are close to reported earnings, about $2.30–$2.90 per share in 1988 against reported EPS of $2.85.

Two things to carry forward:
- Part of the headline 33% return on equity comes from leverage and buybacks.
- About $1.9 billion of shareholders' money sits in affiliates earning very little.

A note on sources: the 1988 segment detail comes from the 1988 annual report, which probably had not been mailed by the as-of date. The headline 1988 figures used here (net income, ROE, operating income growth, repurchases) were public in the 1989-01-31 release (`upi-1989-01-31`).

## 3a. Return on equity, not EPS growth, is the primary test (`brk-letter-1979`)

The test asks for a high return on equity "without undue leverage, accounting gimmickry, etc." So I look at the long record, then strip out leverage, then strip out the affiliates.

**The long record** (1988 AR selected data, restated):

| | 1978 | 1979 | 1980 | 1981 | 1982 | 1983 | 1984 | 1985 | 1986 | 1987 | 1988 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| ROE | 22.6% | 23.0% | 21.1% | 22.2% | 20.3% | 19.6% | 22.2% | 25.3% | 29.1% | 27.5% | 33.3% |
| Debt / total capital | 3.8% | 6.3% | 9.3% | 9.1% | 15.1% | 15.2% | 32.3% | 30.3% | 34.7% | 48.4% | 38.8% |

- The cleanest test is 1978–1981. Over those years the company earned 21–23% on equity with debt under 10% of capital. In 1981 it held more cash ($344M) than debt ($227M).
- That 21–23% was earned with essentially no leverage. It passes the framework's test on its own.

**Stripping out leverage in 1988:**
- Net debt was $893M at end-1988 ($2,124M debt less $1,231M cash) and $1,506M at end-1987.
- Average net capital (equity plus net debt) was about $4.47B.
- Net income plus after-tax net interest was $1,044.7M + ($230.5M − $199.3M) × 0.66, which is about $1,065M.
- That gives a return of about 24% on net capital.
- So roughly 9 of the 33 points of reported ROE come from leverage and from the $2.07B of treasury stock shrinking the equity base. The unlevered business improved from about 21% to about 24%.

**Stripping out the affiliates:**
- The affiliates (CCE, the entertainment affiliate CPE, TCC and others) carried at about $2.07B on average produced equity income of $48.3M in 1988, about 2.5%. That return was 5.1% in 1987 and 6.3% in 1986.
- Remove them and the rest of the business earned about $1,017M on about $2.4B of net capital, roughly 42% after tax.
- Soft drink operating income was $1,690.7M on $3,809M of identifiable assets, about 44% pretax. Those assets are stated before payables, so the return on capital actually committed is higher.
- For context, Fortune found only six of 1,000 large companies averaging over 30% ROE across a decade (`brk-letter-1987#1`). The soft drink core belongs in that group.

**Accounting quality:**
- **1986:** a $375M gain from the CCE share sale (+$0.68 EPS) and $180M of provisions (−$0.33). Underlying EPS was about $2.07, not $2.42.
- **1987:** a $40M gain on the TCC share sale and a $36M provision roughly cancel.
- **1988:** CCE's $104M pretax gain (counted through equity income) is offset by CPE's $114M write-down, plus a $25M gain and $13M of exchange losses. The $2.85 is roughly clean.
- Underlying EPS therefore ran about $1.72 → $2.07 → $2.43 → $2.85, roughly 17–18% a year. That growth was helped by:
  - the share count falling from 393M to 365M;
  - weaker dollar exchange rates: key currencies rose 27%, 16% and 10% in 1986–88, and 76% of operating income is earned outside the US.
- The "innovative business structures" deconsolidate the capital-intensive bottlers. CCE carries $2.86B of liabilities off Company A's balance sheet, and selling bottler shares to the public creates booked gains.
- This isn't gimmickry in the framework's sense: the debt is not guaranteed, and guarantees of others' debt fell to $133M. But it does make Company A's reported ROE look better than the system's.
- The bottlers themselves earn little. CCE earned about $143M on $1.8B of net assets in 1988, and that figure includes a gain.

**Verdict on 3a:** pass. The core's returns are exceptional and were proven before any leverage. The headline 33% overstates the improvement.

## 3b. Durable competitive advantage, the moat (`brk-letter-1986#16`)

The GEICO moat was a measurable cost gap that management kept widening. Here the moat is the trademark plus a distribution system that competitors can't replicate. The packet shows it widening:

- **Share is rising.** World share went from under 38% (1981) to nearly 45% (1988), more than double the nearest competitor. US share went from 39% (1986) to over 40% (1988), 9 points ahead of the primary competitor in 1986. Fountain share is 59%, more than double the nearest rival, and the company took about three-quarters of fountain growth over four years.
- **Volume is compounding.** Daily servings grew from 359M to 559M over 1981–88, +56%.
- **Margins and pricing power are high.**
  - US concentrate gross margin is about 63% (`ar-1986`).
  - Contracts allow flexible pricing; the October 1986 CCE price increase added about $10M a year.
  - In Latin America, 1988 operating income rose 17% on 1% volume growth.
  - Management states it can raise prices to offset rising costs.
- **The franchise survived management's own blunder.** The 1985 reformulation was reversed, and by 1986 the original formula ("classic") was the top-selling US soft drink. A franchise that outlasts its owner's mistake is durable.
- **There is room to grow.** International per-person consumption is about 51 servings a year against 274 in the US (16% of the US rate in 1986). Japan went from nothing to the largest profit contributor in three decades. China was supply-constrained in 1988.
- **The system's costs are falling.** The switch from syrup to concentrate lowers transport costs, bottler consolidation continues, and management claims to be the low-cost producer in most markets (unverified).

**Weak points in the moat:**
- **Advertising is heavy and rising:** $648M → $720M → $842M, about 10% of revenue. The moat has to be paid for every year.
- **The bottlers carry the system's capital at low returns.** That limits how hard concentrate prices can be pushed.
- **There is currency and political exposure.** Argentina and Brazil volumes fell under triple-digit inflation.
- **Foods is not a moat business.** Orange juice costs follow volatile orange prices; frozen volume fell 22% in 1988; operating income was $89M on $1.5B of revenue. It is small (about 5% of operating income).
- **Entertainment showed poor diversification judgement.** It now sits in CPE with a $114M write-down; this goes to the management stage.

**Verdict on 3b:** strong, durable, and widening.

## 3c. Owner earnings (`brk-letter-1986#16`)

Owner earnings are reported earnings plus depreciation and other non-cash charges, less the average capital spending needed to maintain the business. For 1988:

- Net income $1,044.7M + depreciation and amortization $169.8M + deferred taxes $43.9M − undistributed equity income $35.8M ≈ **$1,222.6M before capital spending**.

The packet doesn't split capital spending into maintenance and growth, so I bracket it:

| Case | Capital spending deducted | Owner earnings | Less preferred dividends ($6.4M), per share on 364.6M average shares |
|---|---|---|---|
| Low | All of it, $386.8M | $835.8M | about $2.27 |
| High | Maintenance equal to depreciation, $169.8M | $1,052.8M | about $2.87 |

**Where in that range:**
- The company describes its capital spending as mostly "facility construction, capacity expansion," coolers and new plants, which is growth spending.
- Soft drink capital spending was $239M against $110M of soft drink depreciation, consistent with an expanding system.
- Management also says current-cost income would be lower than reported, so depreciation at historical cost understates true maintenance needs somewhat.
- My estimate is that owner earnings are about 5–15% below reported EPS, roughly **$2.45–$2.75 per share**.
- Cash from operations of $1,167M against $1,045M of net income confirms that the reported earnings are backed by cash.

## Facts the framework wants that the packet lacks

1. **Maintenance vs. growth capital spending.** This is the key unknown in owner earnings. Related: whether investments in bottler equity ($339M in 1987, $129M in 1988) are effectively a cost of defending the franchise.
2. **Capital employed by segment, net of operating liabilities.** Only gross identifiable assets are given, so the core return is approximate.
3. **Results in constant currency.** How much of the 21% international operating income growth in 1988 survives a stronger dollar.
4. **Price versus volume, and margin per case over a long period.** Direct evidence of real pricing power.
5. **The primary competitor's costs, margins and country shares.** The GEICO-style comparison of cost ratios isn't possible from the packet.
6. **Bottler economics.** Only CCE and the aggregate other affiliates are shown, so it isn't possible to judge whether the system can absorb continued concentrate price increases.
7. **1988 10-Qs and the mailed 1988 report.** The segment detail rests on a report that probably wasn't in shareholders' hands yet. Also, FASB 96 could cut equity by about $175M, which is immaterial to the economics.
