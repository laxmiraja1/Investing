**Memo: Purchase of The Coca-Cola Company (KO), as of February 27, 1989**

We recommend building a position in The Coca-Cola Company. The target is roughly 25% of the portfolio, bought in the open market as a passive minority holder, and the intended holding period is indefinite.

Coca-Cola is, at its core, a seller of soft-drink syrup and concentrate. Independent and partly owned bottlers in more than 155 countries turn it into finished drinks. The company's job is to maintain the trademark, supply the concentrate, and keep the bottling system strong. The work is simple and involves no technology risk. It also needs very little capital. In 1987 the soft-drink business earned $1.43 billion of operating income and spent only $170 million on capital projects. Gross margin on US concentrate is about 63%. The company also owns Coca-Cola Foods (Minute Maid and Hi-C), which has no lasting advantage and whose profits follow orange-juice prices. It holds minority stakes in bottlers and in Columbia Pictures Entertainment. Soft drinks now account for more than 95% of operating income, and that is the business we are buying.

The company passes every gatekeeping test we apply. It is not a turnaround: earnings per share rose every year from 1976 to 1988, reaching $2.85. It does not depend on debt. Gross debt looks high at 45.6% of capital at the end of 1987, but net of excess cash and temporary items it was 15%. The core business funds itself many times over, and the borrowing paid for buybacks and bottler stakes, not operations. The company is far larger than our minimum, the business can be understood from its reports, and experienced management is in place.

The economics are what make the case. Return on equity was about 20% each year from 1976 to 1983 with almost no debt. It rose to 27% in 1987 and a reported 33% in 1988. Part of that increase reflects buybacks, a weaker dollar, and moving capital-heavy bottling into 49%-owned affiliates. The soft-drink business alone, however, earns about 34% pretax on its own assets. The moat is visible in the numbers. The company holds over 40% of the US market and more than 44% of the world market, outsells its nearest rival more than two to one in fountain sales, and raises prices without losing volume. The 1985 episode also tells us something: when the company changed the formula, consumers forced the original back, and it became the country's best-selling soft drink. International consumption per person is still only about a sixth of the US level, so there is a long runway.

Management, led by Goizueta and Keough since 1981, has run the core business very well. Its record of allocating capital is mixed. The move into entertainment and the Foods business earned low returns. The 1987 report is promotional in places, and executive pay is tied to the stock price rather than to returns on capital. The overall result is still strongly positive. From 1981 through 1988, each dollar of retained earnings per share was matched by about $5.50 of gain in the share price, or about $2.60 if we remove the rise in the market's multiple. Large buybacks in 1984 and 1985 at 11 to 15 times earnings plainly added value. Management is now concentrating on soft drinks and returning most of the company's earnings to shareholders. These are good operators to be partners with, provided we do not count on them to find businesses as good as the one they already have.

On valuation, Coca-Cola fails our oldest test completely. It trades nowhere near net working capital, which was about five cents a share. That test was designed for mediocre businesses sold at bargain prices, and our more recent experience is that buying a wonderful business at a sensible price works better. For a business like this we value owner earnings instead: reported earnings plus non-cash charges, minus capital spending. We estimate 1988 owner earnings conservatively at about $2.30 per share, deducting all capital spending, including spending for growth.

At $46.56, the market price implies that owner earnings will grow about 4 to 5% a year forever, using a 9 to 10% discount rate. Over the past twelve years, net income grew 11.6% a year. Suppose growth runs at 10% for a decade and 5% after that, well below the company's record. On that assumption the shares are worth roughly $71 to $90, which puts the price 35 to 48% below value. A more pessimistic case still gives a value of $50 to $61, above today's price. No catalyst is needed. As earnings keep growing, we expect the price to follow over time.

We are being careful about size because the margin of safety depends on assumptions. If the company stopped growing, the shares would be worth about $23, half the current price. At management's own 12% cost of capital, the margin nearly disappears. Currency also matters, since a weaker dollar flattered results in each of the last three years. These are the reasons for a 25% position rather than the 40% maximum we reserve for the most certain situations.

Several developments would change our view. We would reconsider if the 1988 accounts show maintenance spending far above depreciation, or if constant-currency figures show that most international growth came from the weak dollar. We would also reconsider if long-term interest rates are clearly above 10%, or if market share or pricing power erodes. We would sell if we found we had misjudged the franchise's economics, or if management made a large acquisition outside beverages or issued stock on terms unfavorable to owners. A higher share price is not, by itself, a reason to sell. A drop toward $35 with the business intact would be a reason to buy more.
