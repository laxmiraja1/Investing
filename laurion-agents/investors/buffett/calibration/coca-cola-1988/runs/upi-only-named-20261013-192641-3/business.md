## Stage 2: Business analysis

I apply the three rules of the "Business analysis" section in the order the framework lists them. The analysis covers 1987 in detail, using the latest full annual report (`ar-1987`), and brings in 1988 where the UPI releases allow.

---

### 3.1 Return on equity, not EPS growth (`brk-letter-1979`)

The test is "a high earnings rate on equity capital employed (without undue leverage, accounting gimmickry, etc.)." I start with the reported figures, then remove the gimmickry, then check how much leverage contributes.

**Reported ROE**

| Year | Net income / avg equity | Source |
|---|---|---|
| 1976–1983 | 19.4%–21.2% each year | `ar-1986` ten-year table |
| 1984 | 21.8% | `ar-1986` |
| 1985 | 23.5% | `ar-1986` |
| 1986 | 28.8% | `ar-1986` |
| 1987 | 27.2% | `ar-1987` |
| 1988 | 33% (company figure) | `upi-1989-01-31` |

**Removing one-time items**
- **1986** included a $375M pretax gain on the CCE stock sale (+$262M net) and $180M of provisions (–$128M net). Net income without either item is about $800M. On average equity of about $3,247M, that gives roughly **24.6%**, not 28.8%.
- **1987** had a $40M TCC stock gain and a $36M Foods provision, which roughly offset. Its 27% therefore stands as a fair figure.
- Both "gains on sale of stock by former subsidiaries" are non-cash accounting gains from deconsolidating bottlers. They don't reflect earning power, and I exclude them.

**How much of the rise is leverage or a smaller equity base?**
- Gross debt to capital went from 3.5% (1976–77) to 9% (1981), 15% (1983), 31% (1984), and **45.6%** at year-end 1987. Net of excess cash, CPE notes and temporary investments, it was 15.3% at year-end 1987 and 10.8% at year-end 1986. The recalculated 10.8% for 1986 is lower than the 14.2% originally reported because of the restatement.
- Treasury stock went from $604M to $1,309M during 1987. In 1988 the company bought back another 18.9M shares. At 1988 prices of $35–45, I assume that cost about $700–800M, since the packet gives no dollar figure. Year-end 1987 equity of $3,224M, plus about $1,040M of 1988 earnings, minus about $440M of dividends and those buybacks, leaves equity roughly flat, while earnings rose 13.5%. Part of the move from 27% to 33% therefore comes from a shrinking denominator, not from the business earning more on each dollar.
- The structure also flatters the figures. Most of the capital-intensive US bottling now sits in 49%-owned CCE, with $2.72B of liabilities against $1.53B of net assets and $88M of 1987 net income on $3.3B of revenue. That capital and debt are off Coca-Cola's balance sheet. The framework would judge the system as a whole, and the system's return is lower than the parent's.

**The other direction: book equity understates the core business**
- At year-end 1987, about **$2.26B** of the $3.22B book equity was tied up in affiliates: CPE $989M, CCE $749M, TCC $84M, and other $435M. Those affiliates produced only **$118.5M** of equity income, about 5%.
- The trademark carries no book value. Goodwill and intangibles total only $74M.
- On its own identifiable assets, the **soft-drink business** earned:

| 1987 | Operating income | Identifiable assets | Pretax return | Operating margin |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| USA | $323.6M | $2,047.4M | 15.8% | 15.3% |
| International | $1,108.9M | $2,126.7M | 52.1% | 27.0% |
| **Soft drinks total** | **$1,432.5M** | **$4,174.1M** | **34.3%** | **23.0%** |

  1986, with provisions added back, was similar: $1,181M on $3,287M, or 35.9%. From 1985 to 1987, soft-drink assets rose by about $1.12B and operating income by about $0.60B, an incremental pretax return of around 50%. A weaker dollar inflates that figure (see below).

**Conclusion on 3.1.** Earlier in this record, the company earned about 20% on equity for eight straight years with almost no debt, while also carrying diversifications. The core soft-drink business now earns over 30% pretax on its assets. Some of the improvement in headline ROE comes from buybacks, the bottler deconsolidation, and currency. The underlying rate is still high by any standard. For comparison, `brk-letter-1987#1` notes that only six of the Fortune 1,000 averaged more than 30% ROE over a decade. Coca-Cola passes the primary test. Reported EPS growth (1976 $0.79 to 1988 $2.85, with 1988 helped by a lower share count) is secondary, as the rule specifies.

### 3.2 Durable competitive advantage: the moat (`brk-letter-1986#16`)

GEICO's moat was a cost gap that competitors couldn't close. Here I look for the equivalent.

**Evidence of a moat**
1. **Share and leadership that keep growing.** US share was over 39% in 1986 and over 40% in 1988, nine points ahead of the "primary competitor" (`ar-1986`, `upi-1989-01-31`). World share is more than 44% outside the USSR and China, with volume "double that of our nearest competitor" (`ar-1987`). US fountain share is 59%, the company "outsell[s] its nearest rival by more than 2-to-1," and it took about three-quarters of the segment's growth over four years. It also holds 49% of vending and 46% of Canada.
2. **The trademark.** The company calls Coca-Cola "the world's best-known trademark" and reports record awareness, image and preference scores in 1986. The 1985 formula change also tells me something. The original formula came back as Coca-Cola classic and became the best-selling US soft drink by 1986. Consumers insisted on getting the original product back, which suggests their attachment is to the brand itself, not to whatever the company chooses to sell under it.
3. **Pricing power.** 1987 soft-drink operating income was "favorably affected by price increases." The new CCE contracts give the company "flexible pricing… at its discretion," and it used them in October 1986. In 1988, Latin America's volume rose 1% while its operating income rose 17% "because of emphasis on margin improvements." Management says it "is able to adjust prices to compensate for increasing costs."
4. **Distribution and scale.** The system reaches more than 155 countries, serves 524M servings a day, and generates about $50B in system retail sales. Management claims to be the "low-cost producer in most of the 155 countries." That claim is unverified in the packet.
5. **Low capital needs.** Soft-drink capex in 1987 was **$170M**, against $1,432M of soft-drink operating income and $103M of soft-drink depreciation. US concentrate gross margin is about 63% (`ar-1986`).
6. **Growth runway.** International per-capita consumption was 16% of the US rate in 1986. US per-capita consumption still rose from 203 servings in 1980 to 274 in 1987, so the gap reflects room to grow, not saturation. International operations supply 65% of volume and about 77% of soft-drink operating income.

**Limits and parts with no moat**
- **Foods has no moat.** Orange juice profits follow orange-solids prices. Operating income fell from $120M to $67M in 1987, and frozen OJ volume fell 22% in 1988. Minute Maid ranks only second in chilled juice.
- **Entertainment has no moat** that I can see. It is now a 49% CPE stake carried at $989M, and its 1988 equity income fell 57% on write-downs.
- **The US is competitive.** "Heavy competitive investment" appears in citrus, and efficiencies are being "passed along to consumers… at retail outlets." US soft-drink operating income grows only about 9–10% a year (1987 +10% excluding provisions, 1988 +9%).
- **US contract constraint.** Most US bottlers other than CCE buy Coca-Cola syrup under *formula pricing* (`ar-1986`). Pricing discretion is broad internationally and with CCE, but limited for part of the US system.
- **Customer concentration.** CCE handles about 45% of US volume. Coca-Cola's 49% stake and contract terms reduce the risk this creates, but don't remove it.

**Conclusion on 3.2.** The soft-drink franchise has the features the framework treats as a moat: dominant and rising share, a brand consumers won't allow to be changed, demonstrated pricing power, and very little capital required to grow. The moat protects soft drinks only. Foods and entertainment are outside it.

### 3.3 Owner earnings (`brk-letter-1986`)

The definition is reported earnings, plus depreciation, amortization and other non-cash charges, minus *average* annual capex for plant and equipment. I use 1987, the last year with a full cash-flow statement.

| 1987 ($M) | |
|---|---|
| Net income | 916.1 |
| + Depreciation | 151.9 |
| + Goodwill amortization | 1.6 |
| + Foods restructuring provision (non-cash) | 36.4 |
| – Gain on TCC stock sale (non-cash) | (39.7) |
| – Deferred taxes (reversal, i.e., cash taxes above book) | (28.7) |
| – Undistributed equity income | (79.7) |
| + Other | 0.8 |
| = Working capital provided by operations | **958.8** |
| – Average capex, 1985–87 restated ($412M, $346M, $300M) | (352.7) |
| **Owner earnings, conservative** | **≈ $606M, about $1.61/share** (377.4M avg shares) |

**Capex is the key judgment.** Total capex was about twice depreciation. The packet doesn't split maintenance capex from growth capex, and much of the spending is clearly for growth: international infrastructure, fountain dispensers, and building construction. If maintenance capex is roughly equal to depreciation (about $152M), owner earnings would be about **$807M, or $2.14/share**. A reasonable 1987 range is **$1.60–$2.15 per share against reported EPS of $2.43**. Much of the gap comes from undistributed equity income and growth capex, not from any weakness in the core business.

**Soft drinks alone** come out higher. $1,432.5M of operating income + $102.7M of D&A – $170.3M of capex leaves about $1,365M pretax, or about **$890M after tax** at the 35% effective rate. That is before corporate expense ($175M pretax), interest, Foods and affiliates. Most of the gap between this franchise figure and consolidated owner earnings comes from corporate overhead and capital placed in low-return activities. That gap is a capital-allocation question for Stage 4.

**Two further notes on candor:**
- The 1987 letter cites "a cash flow of approximately $1.2 billion." That is cash from operations, and it includes a one-time **$273M** release of working capital. It is not a repeatable owner-earnings figure.
- **1988 owner earnings can't be calculated.** The packet has no 1988 cash-flow statement, capex, or depreciation. As a rough assumption, if the 1987 ratios hold and equity income falls by 57%, which shrinks the undistributed-income deduction, 1988 owner earnings would be about **$700–950M, or $1.90–$2.60/share**. This is an estimate, not a packet figure.

### Facts the framework would want that the packet lacks

1. **The 1988 annual report and 10-Qs**: year-end 1988 balance sheet, debt, equity, cash flow, capex, depreciation, segment results, and the dollar cost of buybacks.
2. **Constant-currency results.** Key currencies strengthened 27% (1986), 16% (1987) and 10% (1988) against the dollar. Without local-currency figures I can't tell how much of international growth is real.
3. **The split between maintenance and growth capex**, which owner earnings depends on directly.
4. **Long-run concentrate pricing versus inflation**, and profit per unit case by country, to measure pricing power directly.
5. **Bottler-system economics and contract terms**: system-wide bottler returns, contract duration, and how much of the US system sits under formula pricing.
6. **Competitor data** (share trends, margins, marketing spend of the "primary competitor") to measure the gap the way the GEICO passage measures the cost gap.
7. **Current-cost earnings.** The company says they "would be less than reported" but gives no amount.
8. **Tax exposures**: the Puerto Rico exemption expiring in 1995 (it lowered the effective rate by about 4–6 points in 1984–86), and $280M of unremitted foreign earnings.
9. **The tax-cut pass-through test** from `brk-letter-1986#22`. The US statutory rate fell from 46% to 34% between 1986 and 1988. Whether Coca-Cola kept that benefit would be direct evidence of franchise strength. The packet lacks the 1988 tax detail needed to check.

---

### Stage 2 conclusion

The soft-drink franchise passes all three tests. It earns more than 30% pretax on its own assets, it has a moat built on brand, share, distribution and pricing power, and it generates high owner earnings with little reinvestment. The consolidated company looks somewhat weaker on each test. Reported ROE is flattered by buybacks, bottler deconsolidation and currency. It is also held down by about $2.3B of equity in low-return affiliates and by Foods. Owner earnings ($1.60–$2.15/share in 1987) are well below reported EPS. These are mainly questions about how management uses the franchise's cash (Stage 4) and what price is justified (Stage 5). They don't raise doubts about the quality of the core business.
