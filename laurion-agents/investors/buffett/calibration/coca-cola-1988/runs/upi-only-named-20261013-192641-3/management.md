## Stage 3: Management and capital allocation

I apply the three rules of the framework's "Management & capital allocation" section in order. Acquisitions and reinvestment are examined under the retention test (4.3), and compensation under owner-orientation (4.1).

---

### 4.1 Honest, able management already in place; candid reporting (`brk-letter-1979`, `brk-letter-1983#2`)

**In place.** Goizueta (Chairman and CEO) and Keough (President and COO) have run the company since 1981 ("When we assembled our present management team in 1981…", `ar-1987`). The CFO is Ivester. The framework's acquisition criteria say "we can't supply it" about management, and this requirement is met.

**Operating ability: strong.** Under this team the core business has done the following:
- US share rose to over 40% and world share to over 44% (`ar-1986`, `ar-1987`, `upi-1989-01-31`).
- ROE went from about 20% to 27–33% (Stage 2).
- The bottling system was reorganized: CCE and TCC were formed, German bottlers consolidated from 102 to 68, the UK joint venture created, and partnerships set up in the Philippines, Taiwan and Indonesia.
- US bottlers were moved from syrup to concentrate, and syrup plants were consolidated.
- After the 1985 formula change, they reversed course quickly and brought back Coca-Cola classic, which became the best-selling US soft drink by 1986.

These results come from management's actions. The franchise alone wouldn't explain them.

**Candor: mixed.**

Things done right:
- One-time items are disclosed clearly. The 1986 report quantifies the CCE gain (+$0.68/share) and the provisions (–$0.33/share). The 1987 letter compares results "excluding 35 cents per share of unusual items in 1986."
- Weak areas are admitted. On Columbia Pictures: "we were not satisfied with the performance… we took significant steps… including a change in leadership" (`ar-1986`). On Foods: "obviously in transition" (`ar-1987`).
- The MD&A says income on a current-cost basis "would be less than reported." It also says that purchasing-power gains "do not represent an increase in funds available for distribution." Those are statements that cut against the company.
- Management names ROE and a 12% after-tax cost of capital as its yardsticks.

Things the framework would object to:
- **Public-relations material.** The 1987 report says the system's $50B of retail sales "added an estimated $250 billion to the world economy" because of a "commonly accepted multiplier factor of five." This is the kind of document `brk-letter-1979` says it doesn't want.
- **Stock-price emphasis.** The letters lead with total shareholder return ("38 percent in 1986… stock has quadrupled since 1981") and justify the 1986 split as making the price "attractive to both individual and institutional investors." `brk-letter-1985#2` prefers a price that tracks business value. It is wary of market performance being presented as an accomplishment.
- **"Cash flow of approximately $1.2 billion"** (1987 letter). That figure includes a one-time $273M release of working capital. Stage 2 put owner earnings at roughly $606–807M.
- **Entertainment's change of story.** The 1986 report called the entertainment sector a "leading force" and the "industry's innovative leader." A year later it was folded into a 49%-owned affiliate and partly spun off. CPE took a $100M provision in 1987, and 1988 equity income fell 57% "due to… one-time write downs." The 1987 report describes this as creating "a formidable competitor." It never says plainly that the diversification earned low returns. `brk-letter-1983#2` asks management to tell owners "the business facts that we would want to know if our positions were reversed," and this account falls short.
- **"Innovative business structures."** Two restatements in two years made comparisons harder: 1986 revenue went from $8,669M as reported to $6,977M after restatement. The same structures produced $415M of non-cash gains from selling stock in subsidiaries. All of this was disclosed. It still points to a preference for financial engineering, something the ROE rule warns against when it mentions "accounting gimmickry."

**Compensation (owner-orientation).** The packet shows only plan-level information:
- The 1983 and 1987 stock option plans cover 6M and 8M shares. About 5.6M options were outstanding at the end of 1987, roughly 1.5% of shares. Stock appreciation rights are attached.
- The Restricted Stock Award Plan authorizes 3M shares. Grants were 883,500 shares in 1984 and about 180,000–280,000 in each later year. These shares are forfeited only if the employee leaves. They are not tied to any performance measure.
- Performance Unit Agreements from 1985 give certain officers cash equal to the gain on 555,000 shares above a $20.63 base. At today's $46.56 that would be about $14M, depending on measurement dates.

All of these reward the *stock price* or simply staying employed. None is tied to return on capital, and some pay out from a market rally even if the business doesn't improve. Dilution is modest, because option exercises of 1.1–1.3M shares a year are small next to the buybacks. The packet has no salaries, bonus formulas, or insider holdings.

**Conclusion on 4.1.** Management is in place and clearly able. On candor the record is acceptable, though promotional in places. Neither point fails the rule.

### 4.2 Buybacks only well below intrinsic value (`brk-letter-1984`)

| Year | Shares bought | Cost | Avg price | P/E on that year's EPS |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1984 | 18.4M | $335.7M | ~$18.24 | ~11.6× ($1.57) |
| 1985 | 15.0M | $379.9M | ~$25.33 | ~14.7× ($1.72) |
| 1986 | 2.9M | $110.5M | ~$38.37 | ~18.5× ($2.07 excl. one-time items) |
| 1987 | 14.2M | $605.1M | ~$42.61 | ~17.5× ($2.43) |
| 1988 | 18.9M | not given | est. $36–44 (1988 range $35.04–45.28) | ~13–15× ($2.85) |

Sources: equity statements and MD&A in `ar-1986` and `ar-1987`; `upi-1989-01-31`. The 1988 cost is my estimate.

What I observe:
- **The purchases are large.** Average shares outstanding fell from 396.6M (1984) to 377.4M (1987), and to about 365M in 1988 as implied by $1.04B ÷ $2.85. Repurchases clearly exceed option issuance.
- **The 1984–85 purchases were at 11–15× earnings for a business earning more than 20% on equity.** The stock has since roughly doubled or tripled. Those purchases were almost certainly well below value.
- **The 1987 purchases were near the pre-crash peak**, about $42.60 against an August 1987 high of $53. The stock is only modestly above that price today, so these were the least clearly valuable. The 1988 purchases came after the crash at lower prices, though I can't see the timing within the year.
- **Management's stated reason is excess cash, not price relative to value.** Repurchases began "after management determined that the Company's available cash and debt capacity exceeded the funds required…", and "Often the best use of excess cash… has been to invest in our own stock" (`ar-1987`). The framework's condition is "prices well below per-share intrinsic business value," and management never says it measures this.
- **Some purchases were funded with debt.** The 1987 increase in short-term debt was "due principally to the funding of the share repurchase program and the purchase of equity investments." Over 1984–87 as a whole, net debt stayed roughly flat. Using the restated figures, it was about $495M at year-end 1984 and about $380M at year-end 1987 after netting cash, CPE notes and temporary investments. Over the full cycle, the buybacks were paid for out of earnings.
- **There is one contradiction.** In 1985 the company issued about 7.1M treasury shares, worth about $169M (about $23.80 a share), to help pay for Embassy Communications. In the same year it bought back 15M shares at about $25.33. `brk-letter-1982#17` says to issue shares "only if our owners receive in business value as much as we give." Swapping undervalued Coca-Cola stock for a television producer while repurchasing the same stock at a higher price is hard to reconcile with that. Management later claimed it "recovered virtually all of the investment made in Embassy in 1985" (`ar-1986`), and I can't verify that.

**Conclusion on 4.2.** Taken together, the buybacks have very likely added to per-share value, because most of the shares were bought at 11–18× earnings for a 25–33% ROE franchise. Whether today's prices are "well below intrinsic value" will be settled in Stage 4. On the evidence, the discipline management states is about excess cash, not about value.

### 4.3 Each $1 retained must create at least $1 of market value (`brk-letter-1983`)

**Per-share test, 1981 to today.** I work per share so that buybacks are handled consistently. Over 1981–1988:
- Net income per share totals **$15.12**: $1.30, 1.32, 1.37, 1.59, 1.84, 2.42, 2.43 and 2.85.
- Cash dividends total **$7.76**, plus the 1987 in-kind CPE dividend of $0.90 at carrying value.
- That leaves **$6.46 per share** retained.
- Market price went from $11.13 at year-end 1980 to $46.56 today, a gain of **$35.43**.
- That is about **$5.50 of market value per $1 retained.** It easily passes.

Part of that gain comes from a re-rating. The P/E was 9.8× at year-end 1980 ($11.13 ÷ $1.14) and is 16.3× today. At an unchanged 9.8×, 1988 EPS of $2.85 would give a price of about $27.90. The gain would then be $16.80 against $6.46 retained, still about **$2.60 per $1**. The test passes even without the re-rating.

**Breaking the aggregate down.** The overall result is excellent because the soft-drink business needs so little capital. Over 1984–87, cash dividends ($1,578M), buybacks ($1,431M) and the in-kind dividend ($335M) together came to about $3,344M. After about $319M of stock issued, the net is about **94% of the $3,201M earned**. Very little was actually retained. The capital that was kept and put to work outside soft drinks did much worse:

| Use of capital | Evidence | Verdict under the $1 test |
|---|---|---|
| **Core soft drinks** (capex ~$170M in 1987) | ~34% pretax on identifiable assets (Stage 2) | Very good |
| **Entertainment** (Columbia 1982; Embassy and Tandem ~$445M in 1985; Merv Griffin ~$200M in 1986) | 1986 operating income of $235M on $2,170M of assets (~11% pretax); now a 49% CPE stake carried at $989M; 1987 provisions; 1988 equity income down 57% | Low return. Market value of publicly traded affiliates exceeded carrying value by only ~$44M in total at year-end 1987, so at best about $1 for $1 |
| **Bottler stakes** (CCE $749M, TCC $84M, Consolidated $63M, Johnston $54M, UK JV) | CCE earned $88M on $1.53B of equity in 1987 (~5.8%) | Below the company's own 12% hurdle when judged alone. Can be defended only as protecting concentrate sales: CCE bought $653M from Coca-Cola in 1987, at about 63% gross margin on US concentrate |
| **Foods** (Nutri-Foods $30M, citrus capex) | Operating income fell from $120M to $67M in 1987; OJ volumes down in 1988 | Low return, no moat |
| **Exits** (Presto/Winkler plastics $112M in 1985, Ronco pasta 1984, Embassy Home Entertainment $85M) | Sold near carrying value | Fits the stated policy of "divesting low return assets" |

Two forward-looking concerns:
- **The 12% hurdle is far below the core's return.** A 12% after-tax hurdle permits projects that earn a third of what soft drinks earn. Some investments that passed, notably the bottlers, appear to earn less than 12% on book.
- **The dividend payout is being cut to 40%**, from 65% in 1983 to 46% in 1987 and about 42% in 1988 ($1.20 ÷ $2.85), and the net debt target has been raised to 35%. Both increase the money management can reinvest. Its record outside soft drinks is weak, and both policies are framed as lowering "the weighted average cost of capital." That is financial-engineering language. The framework's emphasis is on what each retained dollar earns.

**Conclusion on 4.3.** It passes overall, and comfortably. The franchise generates so much return per dollar that it makes up for low-return diversification. The trend is in the right direction. Soft drinks are now more than 95% of operating income, entertainment has been reduced to a minority stake, and most earnings are returned through dividends and buybacks.

### Overall management judgment

| Rule | Result |
|---|---|
| 4.1 Able, honest, in place, candid | **Pass.** Strong operators. Candor acceptable but promotional, with stock-price-driven incentives |
| 4.2 Buybacks below intrinsic value | **Likely pass historically.** The stated discipline is excess cash, not value. One contradiction (Embassy share issue) |
| 4.3 $1 retained creates at least $1 market value | **Pass**, about $5.50 per $1 (about $2.60 without the re-rating). Weak results on capital placed outside soft drinks |

These operators have been tested in this business, they are refocusing on the franchise, and they return most of the cash. The risk to watch is their taste for "innovative structures," leverage targets, and a 12% hurdle that could pull capital away from a business earning three times that. None of this would disqualify them. It argues for owning the stock as a passive minority holder rather than relying on management to allocate retained earnings well.

### Information the investor would have relied on that the packet lacks

1. **Personal knowledge of the managers.** The framework's own record shows that judging character directly matters. It cites Mrs. B's handshake in `brk-letter-1984#7` and "people whom we strongly like and admire" in `brk-letter-1987#18`. The packet has no account of meetings with Goizueta or Keough, and I am not assuming any.
2. **The proxy statement:** salaries, bonus formulas, insider holdings, and whether management owns meaningful stock.
3. **The total amount invested in entertainment**, including the Columbia price in 1982, to measure the diversification's full result against what was received.
4. **How management values the company when it buys back stock**, including any statement linking repurchase prices to its estimate of intrinsic value.
5. **The 1988 annual report:** the dollar cost and timing of 1988 repurchases, the use of the $545M CPE note repayment, and capital commitments for 1989.
6. **How the 12% hurdle is set and applied**, especially to bottler investments, and the system-wide economics that justify them.
7. **Board composition and independence**, and succession plans, beyond the director list.
