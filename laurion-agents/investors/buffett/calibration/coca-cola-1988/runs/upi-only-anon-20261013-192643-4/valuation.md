# Stage 4: Valuation (framework §5) and Catalyst / timing (framework §6)

## 5.1 The framework's stated valuation rule: buy below net working capital (`bpl-letter-1965`)

This is the only rule listed under Valuation for this date, so I apply it first, using the year-end 1987 balance sheet. The 1988 balance sheet is not in the packet.

| Item ($M) | 31 Dec 1987 |
|---|---|
| Total current assets | 4,136.2 |
| Total current liabilities | 4,118.5 |
| **Net working capital** | **17.7**, about **$0.05 per share** |
| Total liabilities (8,355.6 − 3,223.8 equity) | 5,131.8 |
| Current assets − total liabilities | **−995.6** |

Adjustments don't rescue the result. The current assets include $545M of CPE notes, which were repaid in cash in January 1988. The current liabilities include the $335M CPE dividend payable in kind. Netting both out still leaves working capital under $0.6 per share.

Against a price of $46.56, the bargain test fails completely. The stock trades at about 5× 1987 book value of $8.66 per share. On this rule there is no margin of safety, and the rule says don't buy.

### Why this rule doesn't settle the case

The working-capital rule was written for the "generals" of the Partnership years (`bpl-letter-1961#3`, `bpl-letter-1963#5`). The investor's own writing in the packet shows that, by this date, he had moved away from it for businesses of this quality:

- "Our goal is to find an outstanding business at a sensible price, not a mediocre business at a bargain price … I searched for 'bargains' – and had the misfortune to find some" (`brk-letter-1987#18`).
- "We may very well pay a fairly fancy price for a Category 1 business if we are reasonably confident of what we are getting." Purchases should be "at prices that will make sense, even should the future of the acquired enterprise develop much along the lines of its past" (`brk-letter-1981#3`).
- "A good business purchased at a fair price" is preferred to "a poor business purchased at a bargain price" (`brk-letter-1979`).
- The acquisition criteria require "demonstrated consistent earning power (future projections are of little interest to us)" and a stated price (`brk-letter-1986#10`).

Stage 2 established that Company A is an outstanding business. The valuation test that fits that conclusion uses the framework's own measure of what an owner receives, owner earnings (§3, `brk-letter-1986`), and asks whether today's price makes sense if the future looks like the past. That is the test I apply below. I'm treating it as the framework's going-concern test for this date, not as a generic DCF built on management's projections.

## 5.2 Going-concern value from owner earnings

### Step 1: Owner earnings for 1988, core business only

I value the core business and the equity stakes separately so their earnings aren't counted twice.

| Item ($M) | 1988 |
|---|---|
| Net income (`upi-1989-01-31`) | 1,040 |
| Less equity income. 1987 was $118.5M; 1988 fell 57% (UPI). Estimated: | ≈ −51 |
| Core net income | ≈ 989 |
| Plus depreciation and amortization (not in packet; assumed, 1987 was $153.5M) | ≈ +165 |
| Less capex, if all of it is maintenance (not in packet; assumed, 1987 was $300M) | ≈ −330 |
| **Owner earnings, low** (all capex deducted) | **≈ 824** |
| **Owner earnings, high** (maintenance capex ≈ depreciation) | **≈ 989** |

Average 1988 shares were about 365M ($1,040M ÷ $2.85).

- Owner earnings per share fall in a range of **$2.26 to $2.71**.
- I use **$2.50** as the base.

Two assumptions sit underneath this:

1. **Shares outstanding now:** about 355M (372.4M at end-1987, less 18.9M repurchased, plus about 1.3M from option exercises).
2. **Currency:** 1986–88 profits were helped by a weaker dollar (key currencies up 27%, 16% and 10% in those years). If I cut the international contribution enough to lower owner earnings about 10%, the base falls to **$2.25**. I test that case below.

### Step 2: The equity stakes, valued separately

The company stakes are 49% of CCE, 49% of TCC, 49% of CPE, and stakes in Consolidated, Johnston and the UK joint venture.

- The company put the market value of its bottling and entertainment investments at "over $2.3 billion at the end of 1987" (`ar-1987`).
- CPE wrote down earnings in 1988, so I haircut the total to **$1.8–2.3B**.
- That is **about $5–6.5 per share**. I use **$6**.

The packet doesn't give market values for these stakes as of the as-of date.

### Step 3: What a price "makes sense" requires, if the future develops along the lines of the past

**The track record I'm extending:**

- **EPS growth:** about 11.6% a year over 1978–88 ($0.95 → $2.85) and about 16% a year over 1983–88.
- **Volume:**
  - international volume grew 7–12% a year in 1986–88;
  - US volume grew 5–7%;
  - international per-capita consumption was 16% of the US rate in 1986.
- **Capital needs:** low, so most growth doesn't require retaining earnings.

**The discount rate:** The framework gives no rate, and the packet has no government bond yield. I assume a long-term Treasury yield of about **9%**. I also run management's own stated after-tax cost of capital of **12%** (`ar-1987`) as a stricter case.

**Value per share** (owner earnings start at $2.50; equity stakes add $6):

| Case | Growth, years 1–10 | Growth after year 10 | Discount rate | Core value | + stakes | **Total** |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A. No growth at all | 0% | 0% | 9% | $27.8 | $6 | **≈ $34** |
| B. Growth well below the past, strict rate | 10% | 5% | 12% | $54.0 | $6 | **≈ $60** |
| C. Growth below the past, bond rate | 10% | 4% | 9% | $83.3 | $6 | **≈ $89** |
| D. Same as C | 10% | 5% | 9% | $98.2 | $6 | **≈ $104** |
| E. Past five-year rate continued | 15% | 5% | 9% | $146 | $6 | **≈ $152** |

**Mechanics, for case C:**

1. Years 1–10 are worth about $26.3 in present value.
2. Year-10 owner earnings are $6.48. At 4% growth and a 9% rate, the terminal value is $6.48 × 1.04 / 0.05 = $134.9. Discounted 10 years, that is about $57.0.
3. The core value is $26.3 + $57.0 = $83.3.

**Currency sensitivity:** With the lower $2.25 base, case C falls to about $81 and case B to about $55.

### Step 4: Read the result the other way, as the growth the price assumes

The framework distrusts projections (`brk-letter-1986#10`), so the more robust test is to ask how much growth today's price requires.

- Price $46.56, less $6 of stakes, leaves **$40.56** for the core business.
- $2.50 / $40.56 = an **owner-earnings yield of 6.2%** at today's price.
- Solving $2.50 / (r − g) = $40.56 for the perpetual growth rate the price assumes:

| Discount rate | Perpetual growth the price assumes |
|---|---|
| 9% | **≈ 2.8%** |
| 12% | **≈ 5.8%** |

So the market price assumes owner earnings will grow about 3–6% a year forever. The business's ten-year record is about 12% a year, it is gaining share at home, and its largest markets abroad consume a fraction of the US rate. The price makes sense even if the future is considerably worse than the past. That is the condition `brk-letter-1981#3` sets.

## 5.3 Margin of safety against the framework's hurdle

**The framework sets no numerical hurdle at this date.** Its tests are qualitative:

1. a "substantial excess of value" over price (`bpl-letter-1961#3`, `bpl-letter-1962#4`);
2. a price that makes sense if the past repeats (`brk-letter-1981#3`);
3. willingness to pay a "fairly fancy price" when confident of the business (`brk-letter-1981#3`).

| Basis | Value / share | Margin of safety at $46.56 |
|---|---|---|
| Net working capital (§5 bargain rule) | ≈ $0.05 | **none** |
| No growth (A) | ≈ $34 | **negative**; price is 37% above |
| Strict case (B, 12% rate) | ≈ $55–60 | **≈ 15–22%** |
| Below-the-past growth at the bond rate (C/D) | ≈ $81–104 | **≈ 43–55%** |
| Recent record continued (E) | ≈ $152 | **≈ 69%** |

**Assessment:**

- **On the working-capital rule:** fail.
- **On the owner-earnings test that fits an outstanding business:** pass. With central assumptions that sit well below the record, the stock trades at roughly half of value. Even the strictest reasonable case (12% rate, 10% growth falling to 5%, currency haircut) leaves a small cushion.

**The main risk to the estimate:** In cases B–E, 60–75% of the value comes after year 10. The margin of safety therefore depends on the franchise lasting, which Stage 2 judged strong. It does not depend on any one year's earnings.

**One more consideration (`brk-letter-1986#0` market temperament):** The stock is near its 52-week high ($49.12) and up from $35 in mid-1988. It is not being sold off in fear. The framework doesn't forecast the market (`bpl-letter-1966-mid`), and the price-to-value gap stands on its own. But the stock is not cheap the way a panic would make it cheap.

## §6 Catalyst / timing

1. **No catalyst needed** (`brk-letter-1987`: "The market may ignore business success for a while, but eventually will confirm it"). This is a holding in the "generals" sense: no timetable, and the return comes from the business compounding. The price assumes about 3–6% perpetual growth against a 12–16% record, so the gap closes as earnings show up. Nothing else has to happen.

2. **Work-out: does not apply.** There is no pending merger, tender, liquidation or sale. The CPE spin-off was completed in January 1988, before this decision.

3. **Control: does not apply as a route to profit.** At a market value of about $17B, Berkshire couldn't buy enough to influence corporate policy, and influence isn't needed with management in place (§4). One related effect: the remaining ~14.5M shares in the 40M buyback program, and any later programs, raise per-share value and passively raise a holder's percentage ownership (`brk-letter-1984#4`).

4. **Mr. Market** (`brk-letter-1987`: "there to serve you, not to guide you"). The current price is set by the business's value. Recent price strength is neither a reason to buy nor a reason to wait.

## What the valuation needed that the packet lacks

1. **1988 figures:** capex, depreciation, cash flow and share count, which are the direct inputs to owner earnings. I estimated all of them.
2. **The split of capex between maintenance and growth.** This sets the $2.26–2.71 range.
3. **The long-term government bond yield** on the as-of date. I assumed about 9%.
4. **Market values of the CCE, TCC, CPE and other stakes** at the as-of date.
5. **Currency-neutral earnings**, to set a normalized owner-earnings base.
6. **Berkshire's alternatives and available capital** at the as-of date, needed to judge opportunity cost and position size. This is for the next stage.
