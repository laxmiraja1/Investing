**Memo: Purchase of Company A (AAA), as of 27 February 1989**

We recommend buying a large position in Company A, about a quarter of the portfolio, to be held indefinitely. The case rests on four points: the quality of the business, the record of the people running it, a price that still makes sense if the future turns out considerably worse than the past, and the absence of any need for a catalyst. The main reservations are set out at the end.

Company A sells soft-drink syrups and concentrates to independent and partly owned bottlers in more than 155 countries. Soft drinks produce more than 95% of operating income. The company's brands account for 44% of soft drinks sold worldwide outside the Soviet Union and China, and its volume is twice that of its nearest competitor. In the United States its share passed 40% in 1988 and the gap to its main rival has been widening. It sells 59% of fountain volume, roughly twice the next company. The product hardly changes and needs no technology. When management altered the flagship formula in 1985, consumers forced the original back within the year, and it became the country's best-selling soft drink. We read that as unusually strong attachment to the brand.

The economics follow from that position. Gross margin on US concentrate is about 63%. The heavy capital of the system, in plants, trucks and coolers, sits largely with bottlers. In 1987 the soft-drink business spent about $170 million on capital against more than $1.4 billion of operating income. Return on equity ran at about 20% each year from 1976 to 1981, when debt was under 10% of capital, so those returns did not depend on leverage. It has since risen to 27% in 1987 and 33% in 1988. Some of the recent rise reflects share repurchases and more borrowing. Even measured against all capital, debt included, the company earned about 20% after tax in 1987. Roughly half of shareholders' equity is tied up in low-return bottling and entertainment affiliates, so the concentrate business on its own earns considerably more. International consumption per person is still a small fraction of the US level, which gives the business years of growth that require little additional capital.

Management has been in place since 1981 and passes our main test of retained earnings. Since then the company has kept roughly $5.90 per share after dividends, and the share price has risen by about $35. Even with no increase in the earnings multiple since 1981, each retained dollar has produced more than two dollars of market value. Management sold plastics and pasta operations and has moved entertainment into a separately capitalized affiliate. It now describes the company as first and foremost a soft-drink marketer. Since 1984 it has repurchased about 17% of the shares, much of that at 11 to 14 times earnings. The record is not clean. From 1982 to 1987 shareholder capital went into entertainment, which earned below management's own stated cost of capital, and shares appear to have been issued cheaply in 1982 and bought back at much higher prices. Management also favors more debt than we would like and reports in a promotional style, with "one-time" items appearing in consecutive years. All the facts we needed were disclosed, however, and the current direction is the right one.

On valuation, the stock fails the old test of buying below net working capital. Working capital is close to nothing, and the shares sell at about five times book value. For a business of this kind that test is not the relevant one. We estimate 1988 owner earnings at about $2.50 per share: earnings from the core business plus depreciation, less the capital spending needed to sustain it. The equity stakes in bottlers and the entertainment company add roughly $6 per share. At $46.56, the market is paying about 16 times reported earnings. That price is justified if owner earnings grow by only about 3% a year forever at a 9% discount rate, or about 6% at the company's own 12% cost of capital. Earnings per share have grown about 12% a year over the past decade and 16% a year over the past five. If growth falls to 10% for ten years and slows after that, the shares are worth roughly $80 to $105, nearly double the price. The strictest case we consider (a 12% discount rate and a haircut for currency) still leaves a modest cushion. No catalyst is needed. The value comes from the business compounding, and the price will reflect it in time.

The position is large because the facts are clear and the chance of serious damage to the franchise is low. It stops short of the maximum for four reasons. About three-quarters of operating income comes from outside the United States, and a stronger dollar would reverse part of recent growth. Management has a history of diversifying into businesses where it had no advantage. The bottlers it depends on are heavily indebted. And we have not met management or seen the 1988 annual report.

We would reconsider if the 1988 figures show capital needs well above our assumptions, or if growth excluding currency effects turns out to be mostly an artifact of exchange rates. A loss of market share in US food stores or fountain would also do it. So would a large acquisition outside soft drinks, share issuance on poor terms, or borrowing to fund low-return investments. We would sell only if the franchise erodes, returns fall durably, or management's integrity comes into question. A high share price alone would not be a reason to sell.
