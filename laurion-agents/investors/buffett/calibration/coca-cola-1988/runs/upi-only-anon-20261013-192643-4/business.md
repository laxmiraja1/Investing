# Stage 2: Business analysis (framework §3)

I apply the three rules in the order the framework gives them: return on equity, then durable competitive advantage, then owner earnings.

## 3.1 Return on equity, not EPS growth, is the primary test (`brk-letter-1979`)

The rule asks for "a high earnings rate on equity capital employed (without undue leverage, accounting gimmickry, etc.)." I take the headline ratio first, then test it against each of those two qualifications.

### Reported ROE (income from continuing operations to average equity)

| Year | ROE | Total debt / capital |
|---|---|---|
| 1976 | 20.3% | 3.5% |
| 1977 | 20.6% | 3.5% |
| 1978 | 21.2% | 3.8% |
| 1979 | 21.1% | 6.3% |
| 1980 | 19.7% | 9.3% |
| 1981 | 19.9% | 9.1% |
| 1982 | 19.6% | 15.1% |
| 1983 | 19.4% | 15.1% |
| 1984 | 21.8% | 30.7% |
| 1985 | 23.5% | 27.7% |
| 1986 | 28.8% | 31.4% |
| 1987 | 27.2% | 45.6% (gross) / 15.3% (net) |
| 1988 | 33% (UPI) | not in packet |

Sources: `ar-1986`, `ar-1987`, `upi-1989-01-31`.

### Test 1: Is the return earned without undue leverage?

**The low-debt years settle the question.** From 1976 to 1981, debt was 3.5–9.3% of capital and ROE held at about 20–21% every year. The company earned roughly 20% on equity with almost no debt, through the high-inflation years. That is the cleanest evidence the rule asks for.

**Since 1984, two things have pushed reported ROE up besides the business.**

- **Buybacks shrank the equity base.** Treasury stock grew from $11.6M at the start of 1984 to $1,309M at the end of 1987. Book value per share fell from $9.13 to $8.66 in 1987 even though the company earned $2.43 per share.
- **Debt rose.** Gross debt reached $2.70B against $3.22B of equity at the end of 1987. Net of $1.47B of cash, the $545M of CPE notes paid in January 1988, and $308M of temporary investments sold in January 1988, net debt was 15.3% of capital (`ar-1987`).

**Leverage-neutral measures for 1987:**

| Measure | Calculation | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Return on total capital | Net income $916M plus after-tax interest ($279M × 0.65 ≈ $181M), over average debt + equity ≈ $5.53B | ≈ 20% |
| Return on net capital | Net income plus after-tax net interest ($72M × 0.65 ≈ $47M), over average equity + debt − cash ≈ $4.36B | ≈ 22% |
| Pre-tax return on soft-drink identifiable assets, 1987 | ($323.6M + $1,108.9M) / ($2,047.4M + $2,126.7M) | 34% |
| Same, 1986, adding back the $180M of provisions | ($1,001M + $180M) / $3,287M | 36% |

On every leverage-neutral measure the business earns around 20% after tax or better. That is with the whole capital base counted, including assets that earn little.

**The consolidated figures understate the core business.** At the end of 1987, $1.82B of the $3.22B of equity was carried as equity-method investments: CPE $989M, CCE $749M, TCC $84M. Those investments produced $118.5M of equity income, about 6.5% on their carrying value.

- Removing them leaves roughly $1.4B of equity supporting roughly $800M of earnings.
- That is a very rough figure, because cash and debt are mixed into the remainder.
- Still, the concentrate business itself appears to earn returns far above the consolidated 27%.

By comparison, `brk-letter-1987#1` cites Fortune's finding that only six of the 1,000 largest companies averaged over 30% ROE across the prior decade. Company A's ten-year average was about 22%, with a strongly rising trend. The packet doesn't give the all-company average for comparison.

### Test 2: Is the return earned without accounting gimmickry?

Several items need adjusting before the earnings record can be read cleanly.

1. **Gains from selling subsidiaries' stock run through the income statement.** These were $375M pre-tax in 1986 (CCE, about +$262M net, +$0.68 per share) and $40M in 1987 (TCC). Bottler share sales added about $67M in 1985.

2. **"One-time" provisions recur.** They were $180M in 1986, $36M in 1987, and CPE write-downs in 1988 cut equity income by 57%.

3. **Normalized earnings are steadier than reported.** 1986 net income was about $800M once the gain and the provisions are removed. Underlying 1987 net income rose about 14.5% and EPS about 17%, as management states (`ar-1987`). Reported EPS of $2.42 then $2.43 hides that growth.

4. **Capital-heavy activities have been moved off the balance sheet.** Bottling (CCE: $2.72B of liabilities, 1987 net income of $88M on $1.53B of net assets, about 6%) and entertainment now sit in 49%-owned affiliates. This raises Company A's reported returns. The economics of the whole system are less attractive than the parent's ratios suggest.

5. **Pricing to a 49%-owned affiliate is discretionary.** The company sets concentrate prices to CCE at its discretion and pays cooperative advertising to CCE (net $53M in 1987). Profit can move between the parent and an affiliate it doesn't consolidate.

6. **Off-balance-sheet obligations exist.** These include an unconsolidated finance subsidiary (CCFC, guarantees of about $256M), $93M of guarantees of bottler debt, and recourse on $325M of CPE receivables. FASB 94 will bring CCFC on the balance sheet in 1988, adding roughly $300M of debt.

None of this changes the conclusion that the core business is highly profitable. It does mean that reported net income and reported ROE are noisier than the underlying business.

### On EPS growth, which the rule tells me to rank second

- **1976–1983:** continuing EPS grew from $0.79 to $1.35, about 8% a year, in a period of high inflation. That is a modest real result even with 20% ROE, because the payout was about 60% and part of the growth was eaten by inflation (`brk-letter-1979#2`).
- **1983–1988:** EPS grew from $1.35 to $2.85, about 16% a year. This came from rising margins, buybacks, and a weaker dollar together.
- **Management's emphasis:** management focuses on EPS and total return. The framework's test is the rate earned on capital, which I've used above.

## 3.2 Durable competitive advantage (`brk-letter-1986#16`)

The GEICO passage defines an advantage as something that keeps competitors from earning the same returns and that management keeps widening. In GEICO's case the source was cost. Here the evidence points to brand, distribution and scale.

### Evidence of a durable advantage

1. **Brand dominance**
   - "Brand A is the world's best-known trademark."
   - Company brands are 44% of soft drinks sold worldwide excluding the USSR and China.
   - Volume is double the nearest competitor's (`ar-1987`).

2. **Widening lead in the United States**
   - US share was over 39% in 1986, 9 points ahead of the main competitor.
   - It was over 40% in 1988 (`upi-1989-01-31`).
   - The company took more than 60% of US industry growth in 1986.
   - Fountain share is 59%, outselling the nearest rival more than 2-to-1, and the company captured nearly three-quarters of fountain growth over four years.
   - The cola food-store lead is the widest in more than 10 years.

3. **Pricing power and margin**
   - Gross margin on US concentrate was about 63%.
   - The company raised prices to CCE in October 1986 under flexible contracts.
   - Management says it can adjust prices to cover rising costs.
   - Soft-drink operating margins are rising:

| | 1985 | 1986 | 1987 |
|---|---|---|---|
| International | 22.9% | 24.5% | 27.0% |
| United States (revenue includes sweetener-laden syrup) | 11.6% | 14.5% | 15.3% |

   1986 margins are before the provisions.

4. **Who keeps the benefit of a tax cut.** By `brk-letter-1986#22`, a strong unregulated franchise keeps the benefit of a lower corporate tax rate rather than passing it to customers. The statutory rate fell from 46% to 40% and then 34%. The effective rate was 35% in 1987, against about 40% in 1984 on the old reporting basis.

5. **The product doesn't change.** The 1985 reformulation was in effect undone: the original formula returned as Brand A classic, and that brand became the US best-seller by 1986. Consumers refused to accept a change to the product, which is evidence of attachment to the brand itself. `brk-letter-1987#3`: "Severe change and exceptional returns usually don't mix."

6. **Room to grow without much capital**
   - International per-capita consumption was 16% of the US rate in 1986.
   - US consumption rose from 203 to 274 servings per person between 1980 and 1987.
   - International volume grew 12% in 1986, 9% in 1987 and 7% in 1988.
   - Bottlers, many of them separately capitalized, supply much of the plant and trucks.
   - Soft-drink capex was about $170M in 1987 against more than $1.4B of segment operating income.

### Points that cut against durability, or that I can't resolve from the packet

1. **The advantage requires heavy spending.** Media advertising was $720M in 1987 and total selling expense $2.11B. Cooperative advertising was 19–21% of related bottler revenues. Part of this spending is the cost of keeping the brand where it is.

2. **Currency inflates recent growth.** About 74% of operating income comes from outside the US. Key currencies strengthened against the dollar by 27% in 1986, 16% in 1987 and about 10% in 1988. Local-currency profit growth is real but smaller than reported, and a stronger dollar would reverse part of it. Latin American hyperinflation is a further risk: the company describes the region as "tremendous unit volume and low profit margins."

3. **The system depends on leveraged bottlers.** The concentrate franchise relies on bottlers like CCE that earn low returns on borrowed money.

4. **Foods is a weaker, commodity-exposed business.** Orange juice depends on solids prices. Operating income was flat at $118–124M in 1984–86, fell to $66.6M in 1987 after a $36M provision, and OJ volume fell in 1988. It is about 5% of operating income.

5. **Entertainment was a detour.** Its value now sits in a 49% stake whose earnings were written down in 1988. It shows no lasting advantage.

### Assessment

The soft-drink concentrate franchise shows the defining feature the framework looks for. Over ten-plus years it has earned high returns on modest capital, and its share lead has widened while its main competitor has fallen further behind. The foods and entertainment businesses don't share this advantage. They are small enough, or separated enough, not to decide the case.

## 3.3 Owner earnings (`brk-letter-1986`)

The definition: owner earnings are (a) reported earnings, plus (b) depreciation, amortization and other non-cash charges, minus (c) the average capital expenditure needed to maintain the business's competitive position and unit volume.

**1987 calculation:**

| Item | $M |
|---|---|
| Net income | 916.1 |
| Plus depreciation | 151.9 |
| Plus goodwill amortization | 1.6 |
| Less TCC gain, plus Foods provision (net non-cash) | −3.3 |
| Less **total** capex | −299.6 |
| **Lower bound** | ≈ 767, or about $2.03 per share on 377.4M average shares |

**If only maintenance capex is deducted:**

- Total capex of $300M includes growth spending: international production expansion, Taiwan, Indonesia and China, and $74M of corporate building and computers.
- The packet doesn't split maintenance from growth.
- If maintenance capex roughly equals depreciation (about $152M), owner earnings ≈ reported earnings of about $915M, or $2.43 per share.

**Range for 1987:** about $770–915M, or $2.03–$2.43 per share.

**Items I left out:**

1. **Working capital.** The 1987 working-capital release of $273M boosted operating cash flow to $1.23B. It isn't recurring, so I exclude it.

2. **Equity-method earnings.** About $80M of equity income above dividends received is non-cash. On the logic of `brk-letter-1981#3`, retained earnings of investees count only if they create value. CCE's and CPE's low returns make me count them at a discount. I've left them in reported earnings above, so the range is on the generous side by perhaps $40–80M.

3. **Deferred taxes.** These were −$28.7M in 1987, meaning cash taxes ran above book taxes, which slightly favors cash earnings. I've ignored it.

**Trend in capex intensity:**

| Year | Total capex / continuing earnings |
|---|---|
| 1985 (restated) | 61% |
| 1986 (normalized earnings) | 43% |
| 1987 | 33% |

The core business needs a falling share of its earnings to keep growing. That fits a franchise and conforms to `brk-letter-1987#1`: such a business "can grow while concurrently making almost all of their earnings available."

**1988 estimate.** Net income was $1.04B, or $2.85 per share. Depreciation and capex aren't in the packet. Assuming capex of $300–350M and depreciation of about $160M, as in 1987, 1988 owner earnings would be roughly $850M to $1.04B, about $2.35–$2.85 per share. This is an assumption, not a packet figure.

## Stage 2 conclusion

- **ROE:** High returns, earned for a decade with almost no debt and rising since. Leverage-neutral returns on capital are about 20–22% after tax for the whole company, and much higher for the concentrate business on its own. Reported figures need cleaning for gains, recurring "one-time" charges, and the move of capital-heavy units off the balance sheet.
- **Competitive advantage:** Strong and widening in soft drinks: a global brand, scale double the nearest rival, pricing power, low capital needs, and a long international growth runway. Foods and entertainment don't share it.
- **Owner earnings:** About $2.0–2.4 per share in 1987, and probably $2.35–2.85 in 1988. Most of the reported earnings are available to owners rather than consumed by reinvestment.

This is the kind of business the framework is built to find. Whether it is worth buying depends on management's capital allocation (Stage 4) and on price (Stage 5).

## Facts the framework would want that the packet lacks

1. **The 1988 annual report.** This means year-end equity, debt, cash, capex, depreciation, segment assets and income, average share count, and equity-investee carrying values. Only UPI summaries are available.
2. **The split of capex between maintenance and growth.** This is the key input for owner earnings.
3. **Currency-neutral volume and profit growth** by region, and an estimate of how much of 1986–88 profit growth was exchange-rate driven.
4. **Current-cost or inflation-adjusted earnings.** The MD&A says they would be lower but gives no figure.
5. **Bottler-system economics.** This means bottler returns, leverage and health beyond CCE, plus the terms of concentrate pricing and cooperative advertising with affiliates. Both bear on whether the parent's profits come at the bottlers' expense.
6. **International market shares** by country against the main competitor, and the competitor's own financials. The per-capita table in the 1987 report is garbled in the packet.
7. **Evidence of pricing power over time.** I'd want concentrate price changes against inflation and against volume.
8. **Current market value of the CPE, CCE and TCC stakes** as of the as-of date. The latest figure is about $44M above carrying value at the end of 1987.
9. **The average return on equity of large US companies** over the same decade, as a benchmark.
