# Stage 3: Management and capital allocation (framework §4)

I take the three rules in §4 in the framework's order: management in place and candor, then buybacks, then the retained-earnings test. Acquisitions, reinvestment and compensation are covered under the rule each bears on.

---

## 4.1 Honest, able management already in place; candid reporting (`brk-letter-1979`, `brk-letter-1983#2`)

### In place and able

1. **Continuity.** The current team was assembled in 1981 and still runs the company (`ar-1987`). The chairman and CEO and the president and COO sign both reports.

2. **Operating record since 1981**
   - ROE rose from 19.9% in 1981 to 33% in 1988.
   - US soft-drink share rose to over 40%.
   - International soft-drink margins rose from 22.9% in 1985 to 27.0% in 1987.
   - Soft-drink operating income nearly doubled from 1985 to 1987 on the restated basis, from $830M to $1,432M.

3. **Pruning of weak businesses**
   - Ronco pasta was sold in 1984.
   - The plastics businesses (Presto, Winkler) were sold in 1985 for $112M.
   - South Africa was exited in 1986.
   - Embassy Pictures and Embassy Home Entertainment were sold in 1986.

4. **Refocus on the core business**
   - By 1987 management describes the company as "first and foremost a marketer of soft drinks," with more than 95% of operating income from soft drinks.
   - It says it prefers "investments within our existing lines of business" (`ar-1987`).

5. **Correcting the 1985 reformulation**
   - In 1985 the company changed the flagship formula, then reintroduced the original as Brand A classic in the same year.
   - By 1986 the original was the best-selling US soft drink (`ar-1986`).
   - The packet doesn't describe the decision-making. What it shows is a large error at the core of the business followed by a fast reversal.

6. **The stated test of investments matches the framework.**
   - Management judges investments against a stated after-tax cost of capital of about 12%.
   - It names maximizing long-term cash flow and divesting low-return assets as goals.
   - That is close in spirit to the framework's return-on-capital test (`brk-letter-1979`).

### Candor

**Evidence of candor in the reports:**

1. Unusual items are separated out, and the 1987 letter states underlying growth excluding 35 cents of 1986 items.
2. The reports disclose:
   - the 12% cost of capital,
   - the roughly 63% gross margin on US concentrate,
   - cooperative advertising as a percentage of bottler revenues,
   - currency effects country by country,
   - contingent guarantees, and receivables sold with recourse.
3. The 1986 report admits the studio's 1986 films disappointed and that leadership was changed. The 1987 report says the foods business "is obviously in transition."
4. Both reports state that current-cost earnings would be lower than reported. They also say net purchasing-power gains on monetary liabilities "do not represent an increase in funds available for distribution to shareholders and do not necessarily imply that incurring more debt would be beneficial." That is an unusually honest statement about inflation accounting.

**Evidence of a public-relations orientation:**

1. **Promotional claims.** "The potential appears limitless." "A rich portfolio of opportunities." "Innovative business structures." A "multiplier factor of five" turning $50B of system sales into "$250 billion to the world economy."
2. **Emphasis on the stock price.** The 1986 letter leads its financial discussion with total stock return (38%, 40%, 30% compounded) and "the value of our Company's stock has quadrupled." Per-share business value gets less attention.
3. **Recurring "one-time" items.**
   - Gains from selling subsidiary stock appear in the highlights tables: $375M in 1986 and $40M in 1987.
   - "One-time" provisions appear in consecutive years: $180M in 1986, $36M in 1987, then CPE write-downs in 1988.
4. **Repeated restatements.** Each year a different unit moves to equity accounting (CCE, then TCC, then entertainment). Revenue comparisons don't line up from one report to the next: 1986 revenue is $8,669M as first reported and $6,977M as restated.

**Assessment:** The information an owner needs is mostly in the reports. A reader can strip out the gains and charges because management discloses them. But the narrative reads closer to the "public relations document" the framework says it does not want (`brk-letter-1979`). This is a reservation, not a disqualification.

## 4.2 Buybacks only well below intrinsic value (`brk-letter-1984#4`)

### Repurchase record

| Year | Shares bought | Cost | Average price | P/E at average price |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1984 | 18.4M | $336M | ≈ $18.24 | 11.6× ($1.57) |
| 1985 | 15.0M | $380M | ≈ $25.33 | 14.7× ($1.72) |
| 1986 | 2.9M | $110M | ≈ $38.37 | 15.9× reported, ≈ 18.5× normalized EPS of ≈ $2.07 |
| 1987 | 14.2M | $605M | ≈ $42.61 | 17.5× ($2.43) |
| 1988 | 18.9M | not in packet | assumed ≈ $38–40 (1988 trading range $35.04–45.28) | ≈ 13–14× ($2.85) |

Total retired 1984–88: about 69M shares, roughly 17% of the 1983 average count. The 40M-share program has 25.5M done and runs to 1990 (`upi-1989-01-31`).

### Against the rule

1. **The framework requires buying well below per-share intrinsic value.** I can't measure intrinsic value until Stage 5. Even so, the earlier purchases look well below value: 1984–85 at 11–15× earnings, for a business earning more than 20% on capital, growing per-share earnings in the mid-teens, and needing little capital. The 1986–87 purchases at 17–18× are less clearly bargains. The 1988 purchases, after the October 1987 fall, came back down to about 13–14×.

2. **Management doesn't say its test is price versus value.**
   - It says repurchases began "after management determined that the Company's available cash and debt capacity exceeded the funds required" (`ar-1986`, `ar-1987`).
   - It calls its own stock "often the best use of excess cash" (`ar-1987`).
   - That is an excess-cash rationale. It isn't the framework's rule, though the results have mostly met it.

3. **The 1987 purchases were funded with debt.** Short-term borrowings rose by about $1.2B in 1987, partly "due principally to the funding of the share repurchase program." The framework prefers businesses that don't need debt (`brk-letter-1982`). Borrowing to buy back stock in a highly profitable business is what `fortune-1977-inflation#6` notes such companies seldom need to do. The net-debt ratio stayed at 15%, so this is a matter of style rather than of danger.

4. **The less obvious benefit is present.** `brk-letter-1984#4` says sustained repurchases show management is "given to actions that enhance the wealth of shareholders, rather than to actions that expand management's domain." Five years of large repurchases, together with shrinking the company through the CCE, TCC and CPE deconsolidations, are that kind of demonstration.

### Related: issuing shares (`brk-letter-1982#17`, issue only when receiving as much value as given)

1. **The 1982 studio purchase.**
   - Average shares rose from 372M in 1981 to 390M in 1982 and 408M in 1983, the period of the 1982 studio purchase.
   - The packet doesn't state the consideration, but the timing suggests a large stock component.
   - If so, shares were issued at roughly $10–18 (the 1982–83 price range) and then bought back at $18–43 in 1984–87.
   - The 1984–87 buybacks largely undid that dilution, at higher prices.

2. **Embassy, 1985.** It was paid for partly with about 7.1M treasury shares (`ar-1986`, note 12).

On the framework's yardstick, issuing undervalued shares to buy a lower-return business and later buying them back at higher prices transferred value away from continuing owners. The amount can't be measured from the packet.

## 4.3 Each $1 retained must create at least $1 of market value (`brk-letter-1983`)

### Per-share test since management took over (end 1981 to 1989-02-24)

| | Per share |
|---|---|
| Earnings 1982–1988 (net income per share: 1.32, 1.37, 1.59, 1.84, 2.42, 2.43, 2.85) | $13.82 |
| Cash dividends 1982–1988 (0.83, 0.89, 0.92, 0.99, 1.04, 1.12, 1.20) | $6.99 |
| CPE dividend in kind, 1987 (carrying value) | $0.90 |
| **Retained per share** | **≈ $5.93** |
| Price at end 1981 → last close | $11.58 → $46.56 |
| **Market value gained per share** | **≈ $34.98** |

That is about $5.90 of market value per $1 retained, well above the required $1.

**Does the result depend on the market re-rating the stock?** The P/E rose from about 8.9× ($11.58 / $1.30) to 16.3×. At the 1981 multiple, today's $2.85 of EPS would be worth about $25.40. That is still a gain of $13.80 per share against $5.93 retained, more than $2 per $1. The test passes either way.

The 1982 and 1988 per-share dividends use my rounding of the reported figures. The conclusion doesn't depend on the exact numbers.

### Where the retained money went

1. **Core soft drinks: excellent.** Modest capital spending (soft-drink capex about $170M in 1987) supported more than $1.4B of segment operating income. This explains most of the test result.

2. **Entertainment, 1982–1987: poor.**
   - Acquisitions included the 1982 studio, Embassy and Tandem in 1985 (about $445M), Merv Griffin in 1986 ($200M) and Walter Reade.
   - 1986 operating income of $235M on $2.17B of identifiable assets is about 11% pre-tax, below management's own 12% after-tax hurdle. Soft drinks earned about 36% on the same basis.
   - The business has since been put into 49%-owned CPE. Shareholders received part of it as the in-kind dividend.
   - CPE's 1988 equity income fell 57% on write-downs.
   - In the framework's terms, this was a period of expanding management's domain into a business management did not have an advantage in.

3. **Foods: mediocre.**
   - Capex was $114M in 1985 and $72M in 1986.
   - Operating income was flat at $118–124M through 1984–86 and then fell.
   - It did no lasting harm but added little.

4. **Bottler stakes: low return.**
   - These are CCE, TCC, Consolidated ($63M), Johnston ($54M), the UK joint venture, and temporary holdings resold at carrying value.
   - CCE earned about 6% on equity in 1987.
   - The rationale is that strong bottlers protect the concentrate franchise. That is plausible, but the packet can't show the return.

5. **Going forward, the payout policy needs watching.** Management plans to cut the payout toward 40% to "reinvest more substantially." The core business can't absorb much more capital. Higher retention will therefore go to buybacks, bottler stakes, or new ventures. The test has been passed because the core business's earning power swamped the weaker uses. Whether retention keeps passing depends on management avoiding a repeat of the entertainment period.

## Compensation (no §4 rule at this date; relevant through share issuance)

What the packet shows:

1. **Stock options and SARs.** About 1.1–1.8M granted a year. A new 8M-share plan was adopted in 1987. Exercise prices are at market.
2. **Restricted stock.** A 3M-share plan, with dividends paid on unvested shares and vesting at retirement.
3. **Performance units.** Cash awards on 555,000 shares measured by the stock price above $20.63, the January 1985 price.
4. **Dilution.** About 1.2–1.3M shares a year from option exercises, roughly 0.35% a year. That is small against the buybacks.

These awards are tied to the stock price, not to business returns, which fits the stock-price emphasis in the letters. The packet doesn't contain the proxy statement, salaries, bonus formulas, or insider ownership, so I can't judge the level of pay or whether it lines up with owners' interests.

## Stage 3 conclusion

| Rule | Finding |
|---|---|
| Able management in place | Yes. Strong operating record since 1981, non-core assets pruned, the team now focused on the core business, and the 1985 error was corrected quickly. |
| Candor | Adequate on facts, promotional in tone. Recurring "one-time" items and yearly restatements make the reports harder to read than they should be. |
| Buybacks below value | Large and continuous. They look well below value in 1984–85 and 1988 and are less clearly so in 1986–87. Management's stated test is excess cash rather than price versus value, and some purchases were debt-funded. |
| $1 retained → ≥ $1 of market value | Passed by a wide margin, about 6:1, or more than 2:1 with no re-rating. This is driven by the core franchise, not the diversifications. |

**Overall:** Management passes. The main reservations are its record of moving into businesses it had no advantage in (entertainment, and to a lesser degree foods), a stated taste for leverage (the 35% net-debt ceiling and "using financial leverage"), and a promotional reporting style. The recent direction is favorable on all three: entertainment has been moved out, management has refocused on the core business, and buybacks are now the main use of excess cash.

## What the investor would have relied on that the packet lacks

1. **Personal knowledge of the chairman and president.** The framework's own examples of trusted managers were judged partly through long acquaintance and direct dealings (`brk-letter-1984#31`, `brk-letter-1984#7`). I can judge character and integrity only from documents.
2. **Management's own view of intrinsic value** and of the price limit for repurchases. In particular I'd want to know whether it would keep buying at higher multiples.
3. **The terms and returns of the entertainment acquisitions.** I'd want the consideration for the 1982 studio purchase (stock versus cash, and the price), the current market value of the CPE stake, and management's own assessment of the episode.
4. **The proxy statement:** executive pay, bonus criteria, insider share ownership, board independence, and any anti-takeover provisions.
5. **Management's plan for retained earnings** as the payout falls to 40%: how much goes to buybacks, how much to bottler investments, and any appetite for new non-soft-drink businesses.
6. **The 1988 annual report:** actual 1988 repurchase cost and average price, year-end debt, and any new acquisitions.
7. **Insider buying or selling** of the stock.
