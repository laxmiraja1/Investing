# Stage 2: Business analysis (Framework §3)

I apply the three measures in §3 in the order the framework gives them: return on equity, the moat, and owner earnings. All figures come from the packet. Where I adjust a number, I show how.

---

## 3a. Return on equity, not EPS growth

The test is "a high earnings rate on equity capital employed (without undue leverage, accounting gimmickry, etc.)" (`brk-letter-1979`). I take the reported figure first, then remove items that could flatter it.

**Reported return on average equity** (from `ar-1986` selected data, `ar-1987`, `upi-1989-01-31`):

| Year | ROE | Year | ROE |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1976–79 | 20.3–21.2% | 1984 | 21.8% |
| 1980–83 | 19.4–19.9% | 1985 | 23.5% |
| 1986 | 28.8% | 1987 | 27.2% |
| 1988 | 33% | | |

There are thirteen years at about 20% or better, and the trend has been rising since 1983.

**Adjustment 1: one-time items.**
- 1986 includes a +$262M after-tax gain on selling CCE stock and a −$128M provision.
- The 1987 letter states that net income excluding unusual items rose 14.5% to $916M. That implies a clean 1986 of about $800M.
- Clean 1986: $800M on average equity of $3,247M gives **about 24.6%**, not 28.8%.
- In 1987 the $40M T.C.C. gain and the $36M foods provision roughly cancel, so **about 27%** stands.
- 1988's 33% comes only from the press release. I can't check it for one-time items.
- **Flag:** gains on the sale of subsidiary stock were booked in two consecutive years ($375M, then $40M). These are recurring "non-recurring" items, and owners should exclude them from earning power.

**Adjustment 2: debt.**
- Gross total debt to capital was 45.6% at end-1987. Net of excess cash, the CPE notes and temporary investments, it was 15.3% (`ar-1987` MD&A).
- Net interest cost in 1987 was $279M − $207M = **$72M pretax**, against about $1.4B of pretax income. Net interest fell a further 52% in 1988.
- So borrowing does not produce these returns.

**Adjustment 3: buybacks shrinking the denominator.**
- Treasury stock rose from $604M (1985) to $1,309M (1987). 18.9M more shares were bought in 1988. The CPE dividend-in-kind removed another $335M of equity.
- Retiring equity raises ROE even when the business is unchanged, so I checked the operating ratios, which buybacks can't affect.

**Restated basis (`fin-summary`):**

| | 1985 | 1986 (ex-$180M provision) | 1987 (ex-$36M) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gross margin | 50.5% | 50.5% | 52.6% |
| Operating margin | 13.7% | 15.4% | 17.8% |

1988 gross margin is about 55% ($4.6B on $8.3B, reading the UPI typo as "billion").

**Soft-drink segment, pretax operating income ÷ identifiable assets (`ar-1987` Note 16):**

| | 1985 | 1986 (ex-provisions) | 1987 |
|---|---|---|---|
| International | 39% | 48% | 52% |
| USA | 15% | 21% | 16%* |
| Total soft drinks | 27% | 36% | 34% |

*US assets in 1987 likely include bottlers being held for resale (the Miami bottler was acquired for about $325M and later sold to CCE). Excluding those, the US return is roughly 19–20%.

Margins and segment returns both rose. The ROE gain is therefore real, not only a smaller denominator.

**What drags ROE down:** the 49%-owned affiliates. In 1987:
- CCE: carrying value $749M, equity income $43M (5.8%)
- CPE: carrying value $989M, equity income $49M (4.9%). Equity income fell 57% in 1988 because of CPE write-downs.

These are low-return, capital-heavy businesses inside a high-return company. The concentrate business alone earns well above the consolidated 27–33%.

**EPS growth, for reference only:** $1.17 (1981) to $2.85 (1988) is about 13.6% a year, helped by a share count falling from about 408M (1983) to about 365M (1988 average). The framework does not use this as its test.

**§3a verdict: passes strongly.** Underlying returns are high, rising, and achieved without meaningful leverage. Watch the gain-on-sale accounting.

## 3b. Durable competitive advantage (the moat)

The framework's model is GEICO, where the measurable moat was a cost gap against competitors (23.5% expense ratio versus 15 points higher elsewhere, `brk-letter-1986#16`). For Coca-Cola the packet points to a different moat: brand and distribution, which give it pricing power.

**Size and share:**
- 44% of all soft drinks sold worldwide, excluding the USSR and China. Volume is double that of the nearest competitor (`ar-1987`).
- US share above 40% in 1988 (`upi-1989-01-31`), and 9 points ahead of the "primary competitor" in 1986 (`ar-1986`).
- Fountain: 59% share. It outsells the nearest rival more than 2-to-1 and took about three-quarters of the segment's growth over four years. In chain and fast-food accounts its growth was more than triple the competitor's in 1987.

**Consumer attachment:** in 1985 the company changed the formula. The original formula had to be brought back as "Coca-Cola classic," which became the #1 US soft drink in 1986 (`ar-1986`). When management tried to change the product, consumers refused. That is direct evidence the franchise belongs to the customer's habit, not to management's marketing.

**Pricing power:**
- Margins expanded while volume grew (see 3a).
- New bottler contracts with CCE allow "flexible pricing … at its discretion," and the company raised prices in October 1986 (`ar-1986` MD&A).
- In Latin America in 1988, operating income rose 17% on 1% volume growth "because of emphasis on margin improvements."
- Management: "the Company is able to adjust prices to compensate for increasing costs."
- Gross margin on US concentrate is about 63%.

**Cost:** the company calls itself the "low-cost producer in most of the 155 countries" (`ar-1987`). It is also widening that advantage much as Bill Snyder did at GEICO:
- switching bottlers from syrup to concentrate to cut shipping costs,
- consolidating syrup plants,
- merging bottlers (Germany went from 102 bottling entities to 68).

**Room to grow:** international per-capita consumption was 16% of the US level in 1986. Even the US is still rising: 203 to 274 servings per person over 1980–87, with a target of 315 by 1990. A franchise that still has room inside its own moat is the best case the framework describes.

**Low capital needs:** the business "can grow while concurrently making almost all of their earnings available" (`brk-letter-1987#1`). In 1987, cash from operations was $1,231M against capex of $300M. On the 1986 tax passage (`brk-letter-1986#22`): an unregulated business with a strong franchise keeps tax cuts rather than passing them to customers. The packet's effective tax rates (32.2%, 33.4%, 35.0%) are muddied by gain items and can't confirm this cleanly, but the margin expansion is consistent with it.

**Weak spots outside the moat:**
- **Foods:** frozen orange juice volume fell 22% in 1988, and the business ranks second in chilled juice. It is a commodity-exposed business with no moat.
- **CPE:** a hit-driven film business. Now an equity stake carried at about $1B and earning poorly.
- **Bottling:** capital-intensive and leveraged (CCE: $2.72B liabilities against $1.53B net assets). Coca-Cola holds 49% stakes in this, plus about $394M of contingent guarantees.

**§3b verdict:** the concentrate franchise has a deep and widening moat, which is more than 95% of operating income. The side businesses do not, but they are now small and walled off.

## 3c. Owner earnings

The framework's formula: (a) reported earnings, plus (b) D&A and other non-cash charges, less (c) average capex (`brk-letter-1986`). I use 1987, the latest year with full statements, on the restated basis ($M):

| | |
|---|---|
| Net income | 916.1 |
| Less: non-cash gain on T.C.C. stock | (39.7) |
| Add: restructuring provision | 36.4 |
| Add: depreciation + goodwill amortization | 153.5 |
| Less: deferred tax benefit | (28.7) |
| Less: undistributed equity income | (79.7) |
| Less: average capex 1985–87 ($412, 346, 300) | (352.7) |
| **Owner earnings, conservative** | **≈ 605 → $1.60/share** (377M shares) |

The range depends on two assumptions:
- **Netting asset disposals against capex** (average 1985–87 is about $104M): **about $709M, or $1.88/share**.
- **Also crediting undistributed equity income in full:** **about $789M, or $2.09/share**. I would give little of that credit, because CCE and CPE earn about 5–6% on their carrying values. Undistributed earnings only produce full value if the economics are right (`brk-letter-1981#3`).

**Interpretation:** owner earnings run about 65–85% of reported earnings. The gap is capex exceeding depreciation by about $150–200M a year.
- Much of that capex is probably **growth** spending (international volume grew 9% in 1987, plus foreign bottling lines and fountain equipment). Maintenance capex alone would give a figure much closer to reported earnings.
- The framework would want maintenance and growth capex separated. The packet doesn't do that.
- Soft drinks on their own, in 1987: capex $170M against D&A of $103M. The franchise itself is close to self-funding.

**1988:** there is no cash flow statement. If D&A is about $160M, gross capex about $300–350M, and undistributed equity income small (equity income fell 57%), then owner earnings are about **$850–950M, roughly $2.35–2.60 a share**, against the reported $2.85. This is my estimate, not a packet figure.

**Inflation note** (`fortune-1977-inflation`): management says current-cost earnings "would be less than reported" but gives no number. The capital-light concentrate model is the kind of business that holds up under inflation. The capital-heavy bottlers are not.

## Summary of §3

| Test | Result |
|---|---|
| ROE without leverage or gimmicks | About 25–33% on a clean basis and rising; leverage immaterial; gain-on-sale items to exclude |
| Moat | Brand, distribution and pricing power in concentrate; widening; long runway abroad |
| Owner earnings | About $1.60–2.10/share (1987); about $2.35–2.60 estimated for 1988; capex above depreciation, mostly growth |

The core business passes §3 clearly. The weak parts (foods, the CPE stake, bottler stakes) are small enough not to change the conclusion. They do pull consolidated returns and owner earnings below what the concentrate business earns on its own.

## Facts the framework wants that the packet lacks

1. **The 1988 annual report:** balance sheet, year-end equity, cash flow statement, capex, D&A and segment data. Needed to verify the 33% ROE and compute 1988 owner earnings properly.
2. **Maintenance vs. growth capex,** the central uncertainty in owner earnings.
3. **Constant-currency operating income.** About 74–77% of operating income comes from outside the US, and the weak dollar helped results: key currencies rose 27% in 1986, 16% in 1987 and 10% in 1988. Growth stripped of currency may be noticeably lower.
4. **Competitors' figures** (the primary US competitor's margins, share and marketing cost per case). These would let me measure the moat the way GEICO's expense-ratio gap was measured.
5. **Why US soft-drink returns on assets (about 16–21%) are so far below international (about 52%).**
6. **Concentrate price history in real terms, and bottler contract terms** (how much of the US system is on formula pricing versus flexible pricing).
7. **Market values and returns of the equity investees in 1988,** and the size of the 1988 CPE write-downs.
8. **The size of the current-cost (inflation-adjusted) earnings gap,** and the effect of the Puerto Rico tax exemption expiring in 1995.
