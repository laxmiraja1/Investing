# Stage 4: Valuation (Framework §5) and Catalyst / timing (§6)

## 1. Applying §5 as written: the net-working-capital bargain test

The only rule in the framework's Valuation section is: "Bargains: buy below net working capital" (`bpl-letter-1965`). At the end of 1987, the last balance sheet in the packet (`ar-1987`):

| $M | |
|---|---|
| Current assets | 4,136.2 |
| Current liabilities | (4,118.5) |
| **Net working capital** | **17.7** (about **$0.05 per share**) |
| Less long-term debt and deferred taxes | (1,013.2) |
| **Current assets minus all liabilities** | **(995.6)** (about **−$2.67 per share**) |

Against a price of **$46.56**, the stock fails this test by any measure. Book value is about $8.66 a share at end-1987, so the price is roughly 5.4 times book. Coca-Cola is not a bargain of the partnership-era kind.

**Does this end the analysis?** It does not, for two reasons drawn from the framework's own evidence.

- **The investor's later writing replaced this test for good businesses.** The most recent passage in the packet says: "Our goal is to find an outstanding business at a sensible price, not a mediocre business at a bargain price … In the interim, I searched for 'bargains' — and had the misfortune to find some" (`brk-letter-1987#18`).
- **That writing supplies the standard to use instead.** Pay "a fairly fancy price for a Category 1 business if we are reasonably confident of what we are getting," at "prices that will make sense, even should the future of the acquired enterprise develop much along the lines of its past" (`brk-letter-1981#3`). The yardstick is owner earnings (§3, `brk-letter-1986`).

So the result of the working-capital test is that this would be a **good-business-at-a-sensible-price purchase, not a bargain purchase**. The test that decides it is the 1981 standard, applied to owner earnings.

## 2. The operative test: is the price sensible if the future looks like the past?

### Inputs

| Input | Value | Source / assumption |
|---|---|---|
| Price | $46.56 | `prices`, 1989-02-24 |
| Reported EPS 1988 | $2.85 (16.3× earnings, 6.1% earnings yield) | `upi-1989-01-31` |
| Owner earnings per share 1988 | **$2.35–2.60, midpoint $2.45** | Stage 2 estimate. The packet has no 1988 cash flow statement. |
| Past growth | EPS 1976→1988: 11.3% a year. Net income 1981→1988: 13.4% a year | `ar-1986`, `ar-1987`, UPI |
| Management's outlook for 1989 | EPS growth "well into the mid-teens" | `upi-1989-01-31` |
| Discount rate | **Not in the packet.** I use 9–10% and test 12%. | The company's own recent dollar notes yield 9⅞–11⅝%, and "other" debt about 10% (`ar-1987` Note 7). Management's cost of capital is 12%. The long Treasury yield on the as-of date is missing. |

**Correction for double counting.** Owner earnings are measured *after* all capital spending, and I treat them as fully available to owners. So the growth rate I apply should be growth in the business, not the extra per-share growth that buybacks add. Net income growth (13.4%) and per-share growth are close over 1981–88 because the share count barely changed over the period as a whole. Past growth was also helped by a weaker dollar (16% and 10% currency tailwinds in 1987 and 1988) and by wider margins, so I cut the "past repeats" case to 12%.

### Step A: What growth does the price already assume?

Solve price = OE × (1+g) / (r − g), with OE = $2.45:

| Discount rate | Perpetual owner-earnings growth implied by $46.56 |
|---|---|
| 9% | **3.5%** |
| 10% | **4.5%** |
| 12% (management's cost of capital) | **6.4%** |

At today's price the market assumes owner earnings grow at roughly the rate of inflation forever. The business has grown at 11–13% a year for 12 years, with international per-capita consumption at only 16% of the US level (`ar-1986`). By the 1981 standard ("even should the future … develop much along the lines of its past"), **the price asks for much less than the past has delivered.**

### Step B: Ten years of growth, then a 4% terminal rate

| Case | OE₀ | Growth yrs 1–10 | Discount rate | PV yrs 1–10 | PV of terminal | **Value / share** | **Margin vs $46.56** |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A: past roughly repeats | $2.45 | 12% | 9% | $28.5 | $66.9 | **≈ $95** | **≈ 51%** |
| B: base, slower | $2.35 | 10% | 10% | $23.5 | $40.7 | **≈ $64** | **≈ 27%** |
| C: harsh | $2.35 | 8% | 12% | $19.4 | $21.2 | **≈ $41** | **≈ −15%** |

How the base case is built: owner earnings grow to $2.35 × 1.10¹⁰ = $6.10 in year 10. The year-11 figure of $6.34 is capitalized at 6% (10% − 4%), giving $105.7, which is worth $40.7 today.

**Not counted in any case:** the 49% stakes in CCE, CPE, T.C.C. and other bottlers. They were worth about $1.9–2.3 billion at market at end-1987 (`ar-1987`: over $2.3 billion including others; publicly traded stakes $44 million above carrying value). That is roughly **$5–6 a share**, less whatever CPE's 1988 write-downs took off. I left out their undistributed earnings when estimating owner earnings, so adding their market value would not double count. Treat it as a cushion.

**Cross-check on reported earnings.** The earnings yield is 6.1% today. If earnings grow 12% a year, the yield on today's price passes 10% (the company's own borrowing cost) in about 4.3 years. On owner earnings (5.3% yield) it takes about 5.7 years. Compare a bond bought at 10%, whose coupon never grows.

### Margin of safety against the framework's hurdle

The framework gives **no numeric margin-of-safety percentage**. The partnership letters ask for a "substantial excess of value" (`bpl-letter-1962#4`). For a high-quality business, the bar is the 1981 test: the price makes sense if the future resembles the past.

- **Price is sensible on the 1981 test.** If the future resembles the past (Case A), value is about twice the price. In a case well below the past (Case B), the margin is about 27%, before the $5–6 a share of affiliate stakes.
- **Where the margin disappears.** Only when growth falls to 8% *and* the discount rate is 12% (Case C) does value fall below price. That combination means the business does much worse than its record and owners also demand a high return.
- **Implied growth is low.** The price requires only 3.5–6.4% perpetual growth.
- **It is not a bargain.** There is no asset-based margin of safety at all. The margin rests entirely on the durability of the moat found in Stage 2 and the management record found in Stage 3. That is exactly the bet the framework's later writing describes. Everything turns on being right about the moat; a 1964-style purchase would not have depended on that.

**Sensitivity to note.** Value moves a great deal with the discount rate and the terminal assumption. Case A's terminal value alone is 70% of its total. I would rest the decision on Step A (implied growth) and Case B, not on Case A.

## 3. Catalyst / timing (Framework §6)

- **No catalyst needed** ("The market may ignore business success for a while, but eventually will confirm it", `brk-letter-1987`). This applies directly, and the packet already shows it happening. The stock was flat at $35–40 through most of 1988 while the business earned 33% on equity and grew EPS 17%. The price then caught up to $46.56. The multiple is now 16.3× trailing, against 15.7× at end-1987 (38.13 / 2.43). The price has followed the business; it has not gotten ahead of it.
- **Mr. Market is there to serve, not to guide.** The price is near the top of its 52-week range ($35.04–49.12) and about 20% above the roughly $39 the company itself paid in 1988 buybacks. A patient buyer might get a better entry. But the framework rules out waiting on a market forecast ("I am not in the business of predicting general stock market or business fluctuations", `bpl-letter-1966-mid`). The question is whether today's price is sensible against value. On Steps A and B, it is.
- **Work-out: not applicable.** There is no announced sale, merger, liquidation or tender. The company's 40-million-share buyback program supports per-share value but is not a specific event with a payoff date.
- **Control: not applicable.** At about $16.5–17.3 billion of market value, control is out of reach. The closest analogue is the "non-voting partnership" stake described in `brk-letter-1981#3`.
- **One point for Stage 5:** the company is itself buying about 5% of its shares a year. Any large purchase by Berkshire would compete with the company's own buying for the available shares.

## Stage 4 conclusion

| Test | Result |
|---|---|
| §5 literal (below net working capital) | **Fails.** NWC about $0.05 a share against a $46.56 price. |
| Operative standard (good business at a sensible price, `brk-letter-1981#3`, `brk-letter-1987#18`) | **Passes.** The price implies 3.5–6.4% perpetual growth against an 11–13% record. Base-case value is about $64, a 27% margin; about $95 if the past repeats; plus about $5–6 a share of affiliate stakes. |
| Downside | Value falls below price only if growth drops to about 8% and the discount rate is 12%. |
| §6 Catalyst | None needed. The market is already following the business. The price is near its highs, but that is not a reason to wait under the framework. |

**Data the valuation needs but the packet lacks:**
- the long government bond yield on the as-of date (the discount rate)
- the 1988 cash flow statement, to replace the estimated owner earnings
- operating growth with currency effects removed
- end-1988 market values of the CCE, CPE and T.C.C. stakes
- the 1989 dividend rate
- the size of the gap between current-cost and reported earnings
- the effect of the Puerto Rico tax exemption ending in 1995
