# Stage 3: Management and capital allocation (Framework §4)

Management has done well on all three tests in §4: the businesses are run ably, buybacks were made at sensible prices, and retained earnings have produced far more than a dollar of market value per dollar kept. The main concerns are the detour into entertainment, low-return bottler stakes, some debt-funded buybacks, and annual reports written partly as promotion. None of these disqualifies the company. Taking the framework's three rules in order, followed by acquisitions, reinvestment and pay:

---

## 4a. Honest, able management in place; candid reporting

**Ability: strong record.** Goizueta (chairman) and Keough (president) have run the company since 1981. The 1987 letter says the "present management team" was assembled then (`ar-1987`). Under them:
- **Return on equity** rose from about 20% (1976–83) to 27% in 1987 and 33% in 1988 (`ar-1987`, `upi-1989-01-31`).
- **Operating margins** on the restated basis rose from 13.7% to 17.8% between 1985 and 1987. Worldwide share reached 44%.
- **Shareholder return** compounded at 25% a year from 1981 to 1987, against 16% for the S&P 500 (`ar-1987`).
- **Focus on the core:** they sold plastics (Presto/Winkler, 1985) and pasta (Ronco, 1984), moved bottling and entertainment into separately capitalized 49%-owned companies, and left soft drinks at more than 95% of operating income.

**A costly mistake, quickly reversed.** In 1985 the same team changed the formula of Coca-Cola. The original came back as "Coca-Cola classic" that same year, and 1985 marketing spending was "extraordinarily high" as a result (`ar-1986`). Classic became the number-one US soft drink by 1986. The packet shows the reversal but does not give the full cost.

**Owner orientation, in their own words.** Their stated aims match the framework's:
- "Management's primary objective is to increase shareholder value."
- They set an explicit after-tax cost-of-capital hurdle of about 12% for new investment.
- They promise to divest low-return assets.
- They chose to retain more earnings only because reinvestment returns exceed that hurdle (`ar-1986` and `ar-1987` MD&A).

This sounds like the "manager-owner" principles in `brk-letter-1983#0`. One phrase departs from the framework: using more debt to "reduce the weighted average cost of capital." Section 2 prefers little or no debt.

**Candor: mixed.** The framework does not want "a public relations document" (`brk-letter-1979`). It wants "the business facts that we would want to know if our positions were reversed" (`brk-letter-1983#2`).

*Against:*
- **Promotional framing.** Both reports are partly promotional: advertising slogans, "the potential appears limitless," and a claimed "$250 billion added to the world economy" built on a multiplier of five.
- **Upbeat on businesses that later stumbled.** In 1986 they called entertainment the industry's "innovative leader" and said foods was "setting the stage for future growth." Within a year foods took a $36M restructuring charge under new management, and entertainment had been moved out to CPE.

*For:* the reports disclose most of what an owner needs.
- Segment and geographic operating income and assets.
- How much currency helped: "aided by a 16 percent strengthening of key currencies."
- The 63% gross margin on US concentrate, the 12% cost of capital, and debt targets.
- Cooperative advertising payments to CCE ($53M net in 1987).
- Contingent guarantees of $394M, plus $325M of CPE receivables guaranteed.
- Receivables sold with recourse.
- Gains and provisions with their per-share effects. Management itself strips these out when describing performance: "Excluding 35 cents per share of unusual items in 1986 … net income per share increased 17.4 percent."
- Admitted shortfalls: "we were not satisfied with the performance of Columbia Pictures' feature films" (`ar-1986`); foods "obviously in transition" (`ar-1987`).

**Verdict on 4a:** able management with a clear owner-oriented framework. The financial reporting is substantive, but the reports are wrapped in public-relations material the framework dislikes. That is a mark against style, not evidence of dishonesty.

## 4b. Buybacks only well below intrinsic value

The rule: "major repurchases at prices well below per-share intrinsic business value immediately increase … that value" (`brk-letter-1984`).

**Repurchase record** (from `ar-1986` and `ar-1987` equity statements and MD&A; 1988 from `upi-1989-01-31`):

| Year | Shares (M) | Cost ($M) | Avg. price | P/E on that year's EPS |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1984 | 18.4 | 335.7 | $18.24 | 11.6x |
| 1985 | 15.0 | 379.9 | $25.33 | 14.7x |
| 1986 | 2.9 | 110.5 | $38.37 | 15.9x reported (about 18.5x excluding unusual items) |
| 1987 | 14.2 | 605.1 | $42.61 | 17.5x |
| 1988 | 18.9 | *not given* | *roughly $39, assumed (1988 range $35–45)* | about 13.7x |

- **Totals.** About 69M shares for about $2.2B, averaging around $31. Net of stock issued for Embassy (7.1M shares) and option exercises, shares outstanding fell from about 409M (end-1983) to about 355M (end-1988, estimated), roughly 13%.
- **Prices paid look sensible.** Every purchase was below today's $46.56, and earnings per share have since risen. Purchases at 12–17x earnings, in a business compounding per-share earnings in the mid-teens with returns on equity near 30%, look below value. Whether they were *well* below intrinsic value depends on the Stage 4 valuation.
- **Stated rationale is cash, not price.** Management says buybacks began "after management determined that the Company's available cash and debt capacity exceeded the funds required," and that "often the best use of excess cash … has been to invest in our own stock." That is a test of surplus cash, not of price against value. The framework would want management to say explicitly that it buys only below value.
- **Some purchases near the 1987 peak.** The 40M-share program was announced in July 1987, when the stock traded at $43–48. The first 6.6M shares cost about $42 each, close to the pre-crash high of $53.
- **Partly funded with debt.** About $300M of the new borrowing capacity was earmarked for buybacks in 1986, and short-term debt rose in 1987 "principally" to fund them. Net debt stayed modest at 15.3% of capital (`ar-1987`), so this is a yellow flag, not a red one.

**Verdict on 4b:** acceptable to good on the evidence. Final judgment depends on Stage 4.

## 4c. Each $1 retained must create at least $1 of market value

The rule is from `brk-letter-1983`. Per-share figures for 1982–88, split-adjusted, from `ar-1986` selected data, `ar-1987` and the UPI reports:

| | Per share |
|---|---|
| Net income, sum 1982–88 (1.32+1.37+1.59+1.84+2.42+2.43+2.85) | $13.82 |
| Cash dividends, sum (0.83+0.89+0.92+0.99+1.04+1.12+1.20) | $6.99 |
| **Retained** | **$6.83** |
| Market price, end-1981 to last close | $11.58 → $46.56 = **+$34.98** |

- **Raw result:** about **$5.10 of market value for each $1 retained**. This ignores the CPE shares distributed in kind ($0.90 a share at carrying value), so it understates the result.
- **Excluding re-rating:** part of the gain is a higher multiple, from about 8.9x 1981 earnings to 16.3x now. At the old multiple, $2.85 × 8.9 is about $25.4, a gain of $13.79, or about **$2.00 per $1 retained**.
- **Charging for borrowing:** net debt rose by about $1.35B (roughly $3.60 a share) between 1981 and 1987. Counting that as extra capital, total capital is about $10.40 a share. That still gives **about $1.30 per $1 even at the old multiple**, and more than $3 at today's price.
- **Incremental return:** earnings per share rose $1.55 on $6.83 retained, about 23%. Including the borrowed capital it is about 15%. Both exceed management's own 12% hurdle.

**Verdict on 4c: passes clearly, under every adjustment I can make with the packet.**

## Reinvestment and acquisitions, judged against management's own 12% hurdle

**Entertainment was a detour.** Columbia Pictures was bought in 1982; the purchase price is not in the packet.
- *Deals:* Tandem cost $178M cash and Embassy $267M, mostly paid in about 7.1M Coca-Cola shares at roughly $24, about 14x earnings, in 1985. Merv Griffin cost $200M in 1986.
- *Issuing shares:* under the share-issuance test in `brk-letter-1982#17`, the question is whether the Embassy deal gave holders as much value as the shares cost. Management says the sector "recovered virtually all of the investment it made in Embassy" by selling parts of it (`ar-1986`), which suggests at least no large loss.
- *Results:* by 1987 the whole sector had been folded into CPE, 51% of it distributed to shareholders, and Coca-Cola's remaining stake earned about 5% on a $989M carrying value. Write-downs cut equity income 57% in 1988.
- *Net assessment:* returns on this capital fell short of the hurdle. Management has now separated it from the core. The framework warns against managers who "scramble after foolish acquisitions" (`brk-letter-1983#3`), and this record calls for caution about future diversification.

**Bottler stakes fall below the hurdle on reported figures.** CCE earned 5.8% on equity in 1987 ($88M on $1.53B), and Coca-Cola's equity income from it matches that ratio. Management argues these stakes "enhanced the operating performance of our core business" by making the bottling system stronger. That may be true but cannot be measured from the packet.

The structure also puts Coca-Cola on both sides of its dealings with CCE:
- It sets concentrate prices under "flexible pricing" contracts.
- It pays cooperative advertising.
- It buys bottlers and resells them to CCE at carrying value (Rainwater, Miami, Memphis).
- It booked $415M of gains from selling stock in its own bottlers.

All of this is disclosed, but these are the kind of related-party arrangements the framework would examine for "accounting gimmickry" before trusting reported earnings.

**Foods:** a small business (Nutri-Foods cost $30M), restructured with new senior management in 1987. Not material to the overall judgment.

**Dividend policy is consistent with 4c.** Management is cutting the payout ratio toward 40% to retain more. With incremental returns around 23%, keeping more is justified, provided the extra money goes to the core business and below-value buybacks, not to more film or bottling stakes.

## Compensation (only what the notes show)

**Plans:**
- 1983 Restricted Stock Award Plan: 3M shares. Grants were 883,500 shares in 1984, then 178,500–282,000 a year.
- 1983 option plan: 6M shares. 1987 option plan: 8M shares. Holders can take the option gain in cash.
- Performance Unit Agreements: cash awards on 555,000 shares measured from a $20.63 base price (January 1985). At $46.56 that is worth about $14.4M.

**Scale:** options outstanding are about 5.6M (about 1.5% of shares). Annual grants of 1.1–1.8M are 0.3–0.5% of shares a year, which buybacks more than offset.

**Assessment:** pay is tied to the share price. That points management in the owners' direction, though it is not the same as tying pay to growth in per-share value or to return on equity. The packet does not show individual pay amounts or how much stock executives and directors own. The 1983 principle "we eat our own cooking" (`brk-letter-1983#0`) cannot be tested.

## What the investor would have relied on that the packet does not contain

1. **Direct judgment of Goizueta and Keough, in person and over years**, which is how the framework's "permanent" managers were assessed (Murphy and Burke at Capital Cities, `brk-letter-1984#31`, `brk-letter-1987#18`). The decision rests heavily on character, and the packet cannot show that.
2. **The proxy statement:** executive pay amounts, stock ownership by officers and directors, and board independence and holdings.
3. **The 1988 annual report:** the 1988 buyback cost, year-end debt and equity, and what management now says on capital allocation.
4. **What Columbia (1982) actually cost**, so the entertainment detour can be judged in full.
5. **How concentrate prices to CCE and other bottlers are set**, and whether the related-party arrangements move economics or only reported earnings.
6. **Management's stated test for buybacks.** Do they compare price to their own estimate of value, or simply buy with surplus cash?
7. **References from bottlers and counterparties** who have dealt with this management. The framework invites sellers to check Berkshire this way (`brk-letter-1986#10`), and the same check works in reverse.
