**Memo: Purchase of The Coca-Cola Company, as of February 27, 1989**

We recommend buying a large position in Coca-Cola and holding it permanently. We would build it over several months to about a quarter of the portfolio. Below we explain what the business is, why it meets our standards, what we think it is worth, and what would change our view.

Coca-Cola is, at heart, a seller of syrup and concentrate. It sells these to independent and partly owned bottlers and to fountain customers in more than 155 countries. This activity produces over 95 percent of operating income. The rest of the company is less attractive. The orange juice business is weak: frozen volume fell 22 percent last year. The company also owns 49 percent stakes in bottlers and in Columbia Pictures Entertainment, and these earn only about 5 to 6 percent on their carrying values. We regard these as side holdings. The concentrate business is what we are buying.

That business passes our first test, which is a high return on equity without heavy borrowing. Return on average equity was about 20 percent from 1976 through 1983. It then rose to 27 percent in 1987 and 33 percent in 1988. In 1986 the company booked a large gain on selling stock in its bottler, CCE. Stripping that gain and the year's special charges out, the 1986 figure is still about 25 percent. Debt does not explain these returns. Net of excess cash, debt was about 15 percent of capital at the end of 1987, and net interest cost was small compared with pretax income. Nor is the rise just a result of buybacks shrinking equity. Operating margins went from 13.7 percent to 17.8 percent between 1985 and 1987. Soft-drink operating income relative to the assets employed runs above 30 percent overall and above 50 percent internationally.

The advantage behind these returns appears durable and is growing. Company brands account for 44 percent of soft drinks sold worldwide, outside the Soviet Union and China, about double the nearest competitor. Coca-Cola holds over 40 percent of the U.S. market and 59 percent of fountain sales. The strongest evidence of loyalty comes from 1985. Management changed the formula, and consumers forced the original back as Coca-Cola Classic, which became the leading U.S. soft drink within a year. The company can also raise prices. U.S. concentrate carries roughly a 63 percent gross margin. In Latin America last year, operating income rose 17 percent on 1 percent volume growth. There is still plenty of room to grow. Consumption per person outside the United States is about a sixth of the U.S. rate, and even U.S. consumption rose a third between 1980 and 1987. Capital needs are modest. In 1987 operations generated $1.23 billion in cash against $300 million of capital spending.

Management has used its capital well. Between 1982 and 1988 the company kept about $6.83 per share of earnings after dividends. Over the same period the share price rose about $35. Even at the far lower 1981 earnings multiple, each retained dollar has produced about two dollars of market value. About 69 million shares were repurchased between 1984 and 1988, at an average near $31 and 12 to 17 times earnings at the time. All of those purchases look sensible against the value we estimate below. The 1980s move into film and television earned too little. Management has since moved that business out of the core and distributed most of it to shareholders.

We have three reservations about management. First, some buybacks were financed with debt. Second, the company deals with its 49-percent-owned bottlers on terms it partly controls, and it has twice booked gains from selling stock in its own bottlers. Third, the annual reports carry more promotion than we like, although the financial disclosure inside them is thorough.

We value the company on owner earnings: reported earnings plus depreciation, less capital spending and non-cash gains. We do not use asset value. By the old bargain measure the stock does not qualify at all. Working capital is close to zero, and the price is more than five times book value. We estimate 1988 owner earnings at $2.35 to $2.60 a share, below reported earnings of $2.85, because the company spends more on plant than it depreciates. At $46.56, the price assumes owner earnings grow only about 3.5 to 6.4 percent a year indefinitely, depending on the return investors require. The company has grown 11 to 13 percent a year for twelve years. Our base case is 10 percent growth for a decade, then 4 percent, discounted at 10 percent. That gives about $64 a share. If the past roughly repeats, the figure is closer to $95. Value falls below today's price only if growth slows to about 8 percent and investors also demand 12 percent returns. We have not counted the bottler and film stakes, which add perhaps $5 to $6 a share.

The protection here comes from the durability of the franchise, not from assets, and we have sized the position with that in mind. Two further cautions keep us below a maximum position. A weaker dollar flattered recent results, since about three-quarters of operating income is earned abroad. We also lack the 1988 cash flow statement and a current long-term bond yield.

We would reconsider on any of the following. If 1988 owner earnings come in well below our estimate. If growth excluding currency effects proves to be in single digits. If the company loses share in fountain or food stores. If management returns to large acquisitions outside soft drinks or issues shares on poor terms. A sharp fall in price with the business unchanged would lead us to buy more. We do not plan to sell because the price rises. We would sell only if the franchise's economics or management's treatment of owners deteriorated.
