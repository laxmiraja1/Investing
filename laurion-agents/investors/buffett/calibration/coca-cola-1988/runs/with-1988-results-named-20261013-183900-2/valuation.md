# Stage 4: Valuation

## 5. The framework's valuation rule: buy below net working capital (`bpl-letter-1965`)

The framework lists only one valuation rule for this date: the partnership-era test of buying below net working capital. I apply it first, as written.

**Net working capital at the end of 1988** (`ar-1988` balance sheet):

| | $M | Per share (354.8M shares) |
|---|---|---|
| Current assets | 3,245.4 | $9.15 |
| − Current liabilities | 2,868.9 | $8.09 |
| **Net working capital** | **376.5** | **$1.06** |
| Current assets − *all* liabilities ($4,105.3M) | (859.9) | **−$2.42** |
| Common book value ($3,345.3M − $300M preferred) | 3,045.3 | $8.58 |

At $46.56, the stock trades at **44 times net working capital** and **5.4 times book**. Current assets less all liabilities is negative. **Coke fails the bargain test decisively.** No version of the 1965 method produces a margin of safety here.

**Why this does not end the analysis.** By the as-of date, the investor's own writing had moved away from this method for businesses of this kind:
- "Our goal is to find an outstanding business at a sensible price, not a mediocre business at a bargain price… I searched for 'bargains' – and had the misfortune to find some" (`brk-letter-1987#18`).
- "We may very well pay a fairly fancy price for a Category 1 business if we are reasonably confident of what we are getting." Purchases should be at "prices that will make sense, even should the future of the acquired enterprise develop much along the lines of its past" (`brk-letter-1981#3`).
- On price to book: "If… you pay, say, six times book value, that does not change CCH's return on equity" (`brk-letter-1987#1`). A high multiple of book is therefore not disqualifying in itself.
- The earning-power measure the framework supplies is **owner earnings** (`brk-letter-1986`). Value is measured against "what careful analysis indicates its value to a private owner to be" (`bpl-letter-1963#5`).

So the net-working-capital rule is the right test for a "general" bought on asset value, and it rules Coke out on that basis. For an outstanding business, the applicable test at this date is different. It asks whether the price makes sense if owner earnings continue roughly along their past path.

## Valuation from owner earnings, judged against the past record

**Inputs (Stages 2 and 3):**
- 1988 owner earnings: $790M–$1,010M, or **$2.23–$2.85 per share**. The midpoint is **$2.54**.
- These figures are after interest and after deducting equity income not received in cash. Debt is therefore already reflected, and the stakes in bottlers and CPE must be added separately.
- Affiliate stakes are carried at $1,912M, about $5.40 a share. Listed stakes are worth $291M above book, and CPE's value is uncertain. I add a conservative **$5 a share**.
- Past growth: EPS rose 13.6% a year over 1981–88 and 16.1% a year over 1984–88. Soft-drink operating income rose about 27% a year over 1985–88, helped by currency. Daily servings grew 5.7% a year over 1980–88. Management guides to mid-teens EPS growth for 1989 (`upi-1989-01-31`).
- **Discount rate: not in the packet.** There is no long-term Treasury yield. As proxies I use the 9% pension discount rate (`ar-1988` note 11) and management's own 12% after-tax cost of capital. I show both.

### a) What the price itself assumes

At $46.56, an owner-earnings yield of 5.5% (4.8–6.1% across the range) is **below** the 9% proxy for bond yields. The purchase therefore makes sense only if earnings grow. The question is how much growth the price requires, if owner earnings grow at a constant rate forever:

| Discount rate | Owner earnings $2.23 | $2.54 | $2.85 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 9% | 4.0% | **3.4%** | 2.7% |
| 12% | 6.6% | **6.2%** | 5.6% |

The market is paying for roughly **3–6% perpetual growth**. That is less than half of the 13–16% per-share record, and at or below unit volume growth alone.

### b) Scenarios where "the future develops much along the lines of its past" (`brk-letter-1981#3`)

All cases start from $2.54 of owner earnings. Each assumes growth for ten years, **then zero growth thereafter**, which is deliberately harsh for a 100-year-old brand. The $5 for affiliates is added at the end.

| Case | 10-yr growth | Value at 9% | Value at 12% |
|---|---|---|---|
| No growth at all | 0% | $28 + $5 = $33 | $21 + $5 = $26 |
| **Fades: growth slows** | 10% | 57.6 + 5 = **$63** | 40.7 + 5 = **$46** |
| **Past continues** | 15% | 82.7 + 5 = **$88** | 57.1 + 5 = **$62** |
| (Not used: 15% for 10 years, then 5% forever, at 9%) | | about $153 | |

**Calculation for the "past continues" case at 9%:**
1. Present value of years 1–10 = 2.54 × Σ(1.15/1.09)^t = 2.54 × 13.59 = **$34.5**.
2. Year-10 owner earnings = 2.54 × 1.15^10 = $10.28.
3. Value at year 10 with no growth = 10.28 / 0.09 = $114.2.
4. Present value of that = 114.2 / 1.09^10 = **$48.2**.
5. Total = **$82.7**.

### c) Margin of safety

The framework gives **no numerical hurdle** at this date. In the partnership years it demanded "a substantial excess of value" (`bpl-letter-1962#4`). For an outstanding business, the 1981–87 standard is a "sensible price" that "make[s] sense" if the past continues. It also warns that two well-informed observers' estimates of value can differ by more than 10% (`brk-letter-1986#0`).

| Value basis | Value | Price below value |
|---|---|---|
| Past continues, 9% | $88 | **47%** |
| Fades, 9% | $63 | **26%** |
| Past continues, 12% | $62 | **25%** |
| Fades, 12% | $46 | **about 0%** |
| No growth, 9% | $33 | price 41% above value |

- **Against the "future like the past" test:** the price is about **25–47% below value**, depending on the discount rate. Only the combination of slowing growth *and* a 12% discount rate removes the margin. Even then, value equals the price; it does not fall below it.
- The margin of safety does **not** come from assets, as it did in 1965. It comes from (i) the large gap between the growth the price requires (3–6%) and the growth the business has delivered (13–16%), and (ii) the durability of the moat (Stage 2), which makes that record unusually predictable.
- **Consistency check with Stage 3:** management's 1987–88 buybacks averaged about $40–43. That is about one-third below the central values of $62–88. Those buybacks do appear to have been "well below" intrinsic value, which supports the earlier finding.
- **Risks that could break this:**
  - Currency: about 76% of operating income is foreign, and the weak dollar inflated 1986–88 growth.
  - Inflation in Latin America.
  - Low-return capital flowing back into stakes outside the core.
  - The fact that the zero-growth value ($33) is below the price, so **a large part of the price depends on growth.** Paying for growth is acceptable only because the business is predictable. A misjudgment of the franchise is a misjudgment of the kind most commonly made, "(2) the future economics of the business" (`brk-letter-1981#3`).

## 6. Catalyst and timing

- **No catalyst needed** (`brk-letter-1987`): "The market may ignore business success for a while, but eventually will confirm it." The value case rests on owner earnings compounding, not on a re-rating or an event. Each year of mid-teens growth at a constant multiple produces mid-teens returns.
- **Work-out** (`bpl-letter-1957`): **not applicable.** No merger, liquidation or tender offer is announced. The 14.4M shares left in the buyback authorization support per-share value but are not a work-out.
- **Control** (`bpl-letter-1963`): **not applicable.** At a $16.5B market value, Berkshire cannot get influence by buying a large stake, and the framework does not need it. Management is already in place and able (Stage 3).
- **Mr. Market's mood** (`brk-letter-1987`; `brk-letter-1986`, "be fearful when others are greedy"): the market is not offering Coke in a panic.
  - The stock sits near its 52-week high: $46.56 against a range of $35.04–$49.12.
  - It is up from $37–38 through most of 1988 and from the October 1987 low of $28.96.
  - It moved up after the January 31 earnings release.
  - The framework neither waits for a better price nor chases momentum: "Mr. Market is there to serve you, not to guide you." The decision turns on price against value, and on that test the price is sensible.

## Information the packet lacks for valuation

1. **The long-term Treasury yield**, the key discount-rate input. The 9% pension rate is only a proxy.
2. **Maintenance versus growth capex**, which sets where owner earnings fall within $2.23–$2.85.
3. **The market value of the CPE stake** and of each listed bottler stake. The packet gives only an aggregate $291M excess.
4. **Multiples for the S&P 500 and comparable consumer franchises**, to judge whether 16× earnings is unusual.
5. **Berkshire's own opportunity cost at this date**: cash available and the yields on its alternatives. These are needed for sizing in Stage 5.

---

## Stage 4 conclusion

- **Bargain test (net working capital): fails.** The price is 44× net working capital, and current assets less all liabilities is negative. By the investor's 1987 writing, this test no longer governs the purchase of an outstanding business.
- **Owner-earnings test, assuming the future resembles the past: passes.** Intrinsic value is about **$62–88 a share** in the main cases, against a price of **$46.56**. That is a margin of safety of about **25–47%**, and about nil only under the harshest combination of assumptions. The price requires only about 3–6% perpetual growth from a business that has compounded per-share earnings at 13–16%.
- **Catalyst:** none needed, and the business's own compounding does the work. There is no work-out or control angle. The market is not fearful, but the price is still sensible.
- The case for proceeding to sizing in Stage 5 is a wonderful business at a sensible price, not a bargain.
