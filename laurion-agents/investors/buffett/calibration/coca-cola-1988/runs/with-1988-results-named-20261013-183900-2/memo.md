# Memo: Purchase of The Coca-Cola Company, as of February 27, 1989

We recommend buying shares of The Coca-Cola Company in the open market, building toward roughly a quarter of the portfolio at prices near the current $46.56. The case is not that the stock is cheap by traditional measures; it isn't. The case is that this is one of the best businesses we have examined, its future is unusually predictable, and the price assumes far less than its record supports.

Coca-Cola's core business is selling soft-drink concentrate and syrup to more than a thousand independent bottlers in over 160 countries. Those bottlers carry the trucks, plants and debt; Coca-Cola supplies the formula, the trademark and the marketing. Soft drinks now produce more than 90% of operating income, after management spun its entertainment business into a 49%-owned affiliate and sold its coffee and plastics operations. What remains alongside the core is a modest orange-juice business and minority stakes in bottlers.

The business passes every screen we apply. It is not a turnaround: earnings per share have risen every year since at least 1976, reaching $2.85 in 1988. It does not need debt to operate. Capital spending was $387 million last year against $1.17 billion of operating cash flow, and the borrowing on the balance sheet was used for share repurchases and bottler investments rather than to keep the business running. Return on equity reached 33% in 1988. Part of that figure reflects buybacks that shrank the equity base, so we looked beneath it. The soft-drink operations earned 44% pre-tax on their identifiable assets, and roughly 40–45% after tax on the capital actually tied up in operations. Few large companies earn anything like that.

The returns are protected by advantages competitors cannot easily copy. Coca-Cola's share of world soft-drink sales rose from under 38% in 1980 to about 45% in 1988, more than twice its nearest rival. Gross margins widened from about 50% to nearly 56% over the last three years, which suggests real pricing power. The trademark is the most recognized in the world by a wide margin, and the bottling system is a distribution network built over a century. Outside the United States, consumption per person is still a small fraction of the American level, which leaves a long runway for growth that requires little capital.

Management has been in place since 1981 and has used retained earnings well. Over 1981–88 the company kept about $6.46 per share of earnings and the stock rose about $33.50 per share. Even if the market had paid no higher multiple than in 1980, earnings growth alone would have produced more than $2.50 of value for each dollar retained. Since 1984 it has repurchased nearly 70 million shares at an average cost of about $31.50. Management states its goals in terms of return on equity and a 12% cost of capital, and its financial disclosure is more candid than the glossy presentation suggests.

The record has blemishes. The entertainment venture of the early 1980s was partly paid for with stock issued at low prices, and it earned poor returns before being largely unwound. The bottler stakes and the juice business still earn less than management's own hurdle. Incentive pay is tied to the stock price rather than to business results. We have not yet met the managers ourselves, and we would want to before the position reaches full size.

On valuation, we estimate 1988 owner earnings at $2.23 to $2.85 per share. That is reported earnings plus depreciation, minus capital spending and minus affiliate income not received in cash. At today's price, the market is effectively assuming these earnings grow 3% to 6% a year indefinitely. Per-share earnings have actually compounded at 13% to 16%. If growth runs at 10% to 15% for the next decade and then stops entirely, the shares are worth roughly $62 to $88. The discount rates we used run from 9% to 12%, the stakes in bottlers are counted separately, and the price is therefore 25% to 47% below value. Only under the harshest combination of slower growth and a 12% discount rate does the margin disappear, and even then value roughly equals the price. Nothing in particular has to happen for us to be paid; continued growth in earnings should eventually be reflected in the share price.

We are sizing at about a quarter of the portfolio rather than the maximum we would ever commit, for three reasons. Much of the value depends on growth. About three-quarters of operating income is earned abroad, and a weaker dollar has flattered recent results. And our view of management is not yet confirmed in person. Purchases would be made over time without chasing the price far above current levels.

We intend to hold the position indefinitely, and a higher share price alone would not be a reason to sell. Our view would change if world or U.S. market share began to erode for several years, or if margins fell in a way that signaled lost pricing power. It would also change if management again channeled large sums into unrelated businesses or issued stock cheaply, if borrowing rose past its stated 35% ceiling for purposes outside the core, or if closer study showed that most capital spending is required just to stand still. Any of these would mean we had misjudged the business. Because the shares are marketable, correcting that mistake would be straightforward.
