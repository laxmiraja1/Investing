# Stage 3: Management and capital allocation

This stage applies the framework's three management rules in order: honest, able management with candid reporting; buybacks only well below intrinsic value; and each $1 retained creating at least $1 of market value. Acquisitions, reinvestment and compensation are assessed within those three rules.

---

## 4.1 Honest, able management in place; candid reporting (`brk-letter-1979`, `brk-letter-1983#2`)

**Who runs the company.** Roberto Goizueta (Chairman and CEO) and Donald Keough (President and COO) have run the company since 1981. That is long enough to judge them on results rather than promises. The CFO is M. Douglas Ivester (`ar-1986`, `ar-1988`).

**Ability, judged by results.**
- Underlying ROE rose from about 20% (1979–83) to about 33% in 1988 (Stage 2).
- World soft-drink share rose from under 38% to nearly 45%, and daily servings rose 56% (`ar-1988`).
- Net income per share rose from $1.30 in 1981 to $2.85 in 1988, about 12% a year compounded, with a faster pace since 1984.
- The core business was strengthened while weaker businesses were sold or spun off: plastics (1985), Ronco pasta (1984), Embassy Pictures and Home Entertainment (1986), entertainment into CPE (1987–88), and coffee (agreed in 1988).

**Stated goals.**
- "Management's primary objective is to increase shareholder value over time."
- Investments must beat an after-tax cost of capital of about 12%.
- The 1988 letter names ROE as "the most significant" number. That matches the framework's primary test (`brk-letter-1979`) rather than headline EPS growth.
- The stated goals are acceptable. The test is whether actions match them, which sections 4.2 and 4.3 examine.

**Evidence of candor:**
- Management states that current-cost income "would be less than reported." It also says that gains from being a net monetary debtor "do not represent an increase in funds available for distribution" and "do not necessarily imply that incurring more debt would be beneficial" (`ar-1986`, `ar-1987`, `ar-1988`).
- It flags its own inflated figures. Australia's 37% volume jump came from shipping patterns, and retail growth of 11% "more accurately reflects" the year. U.S. 1987 shipments rose only 1% against retail growth of 6% because bottlers reduced inventory.
- It discloses how much 1986 results were helped by unusual items ($0.35 a share) and gives growth figures excluding them.
- It says plainly that it was "not satisfied" with Columbia's 1986 films. It also calls Foods "obviously in transition."
- It discloses currency effects, guarantees, recourse on CPE receivables, and the possible FASB 96 charge of about $175M.

**Evidence against:**
- The reports are glossy and promotional. One example is the claim that the system added "$250 billion to the world economy" using "the commonly accepted multiplier factor of five" (`ar-1987`). That reads as public relations, which `brk-letter-1979` says owners should not receive.
- Entertainment's exit is presented as "creat[ing] a formidable competitor." Yet the 1986 report had called the sector a "leading force" with "a strategy… without significantly increasing capital risk." The reversal is never described as a reconsidered decision. The `brk-letter-1985#6` habit of studying one's own mistakes is absent.
- The basis of presentation changed three years running (CCE, then CPE and TCC, then the finance subsidiary). Gains from selling subsidiary shares appear inside "income before taxes." Each change is disclosed and the auditors concur, but together they make comparisons over time hard.

**Compensation and alignment:**
- Options outstanding were 8.66M at the end of 1988, about 2.4% of shares. 4.18M of these were granted in 1988 alone, up from 1.1M in 1987.
- The restricted stock plan has granted 0.2–0.9M shares a year. In 1988 a forfeiture exemption was added for a "change of control," a small step toward protecting management's position.
- Under the 1985 Performance Units, cash pays out on the rise of 555,000 shares above $20.63. At $46.56 that is about $14.4M.
- The 1988 Incentive Units pay the full market value of 300,000 shares, about $14M at today's price.
- **Assessment:** almost all incentives are tied to the **stock price**, not to returns on capital or to results by unit. The framework prefers judging managers "by the returns they achieve on the underlying assets" (`brk-letter-1987#1`). Dilution is modest: about 0.9–1.3M options are exercised a year against 355M shares, and buybacks more than offset it. Still, the 1988 jump in option grants is worth watching.

**Finding.** Management is able. Its stated priorities match the framework's (ROE, cost of capital, per-share results), and its financial disclosure is more candid than its marketing tone suggests. Reporting is promotional rather than misleading. The one weakness in candor is that nobody admits that entertainment was a misstep.

## 4.2 Buybacks only well below intrinsic value (`brk-letter-1984`)

**Record of repurchases (treasury cost, from the equity statements):**

| Year | Shares bought (M) | Cost ($M) | Average price | EPS that year | Earnings yield at purchase |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1984 | 18.4 | 336 | about $18.2 | $1.57 | 8.6% |
| 1985 | 15.0 | 380 | about $25.3 | $1.72 | 6.8% |
| 1986 | 2.9 | 110 | about $38.4 | $2.42* | 6.3% |
| 1987 | 14.2 | 605 | about $42.6 | $2.43 | 5.7% |
| 1988 | 19.0 | 758 | about $39.9 | $2.85 | 7.1% |
| **Total** | **69.5** | **2,189** | **about $31.5** | | |

*1986 EPS includes the $0.68 CCE gain. Excluding it and the charges, EPS was about $2.07 and the yield about 5.4%.

**Testing against the rule:**
- **Management's own criterion is about cash, not explicitly about value.** It buys "after management determined that the Company's available cash and debt capacity exceeded the funds required," and will continue "as long as investing in our own stock remains one of the best uses of our excess cash." That is a test of opportunity cost. The framework requires the price to be *well below intrinsic value*, which is a stricter test, and the packet does not show management estimating intrinsic value.
- **Do the purchases look below value?** Stage 2 found that the core earns about 40–45% after tax on operating capital and that owner earnings have grown in the mid-teens. Buying at an owner-earnings yield of about 6–7%, on earnings growing about 15%, implies a long-run return in the low 20s if that growth holds. That is well above the company's 12% hurdle and its after-tax borrowing cost. Purchases from 1984–85, at $18–25, look clearly below value. Whether the 1987–88 purchases at about $40–43 were "well below" value depends on Stage 4's estimate. They were at least below today's $46.56 and backed by rising per-share earnings.
- **How the buybacks were paid for.** In 1987, short-term debt rose about $1.2B, "due principally to the funding of the share repurchase program" and bottler investments. Debt to capital reached 48%. Management then cut it to 38.8% in 1988, using $545M of collected CPE notes, the Miami bottler sale and $300M of money-market preferred. The framework prefers "little or no debt." Using temporary borrowing to buy back stock is acceptable only because the core business does not need the capital, but it is a flag.
- **Issuing and buying back stock at the same time (`brk-letter-1982#17`).**
  - Average shares rose from 372M (1981) to 408M (1983), at the time of the 1982 Columbia purchase. The packet does not give the terms, but the timing suggests about 10% dilution at prices of roughly $10–18.
  - In 1985, about 7.1M treasury shares were issued for Embassy at roughly $24 a share, while the company was buying back shares at about $25.
  - The framework's yardstick is to issue shares only when owners receive equal value. Issuing stock cheaply for entertainment and later buying it back at $40 fails that yardstick. Since 1985, however, the flow has run only one way: net shares fell from about 408M to 355M.

**Finding.** The current buyback policy is shareholder-friendly and probably value-creating, but it is driven by surplus cash rather than an explicit discount to value. Earlier share issuance for acquisitions was not disciplined. The judgment on "well below" depends on Stage 4.

## 4.3 Each $1 retained must create at least $1 of market value (`brk-letter-1983`)

**Per share, end-1980 to end-1988 (all figures in the packet):**
- Net income per share earned, 1981–88: **$15.12**
- Cash dividends paid: **$7.76**, plus the CPE dividend in kind of **$0.90**
- **Retained per share: about $6.46**
- Market price: $11.13 → $44.63, a gain of **$33.50**
- **Gain per $1 retained: about $5.20**

The test passes clearly, even after allowing for the market paying a higher multiple. At the 1980 multiple of about 10× earnings, 1988 earnings of $2.85 would support about $28.5–30. That is still roughly $2.60–2.90 of value per $1 retained, from earnings growth alone.

**Company level:** market value rose from about $4.14B (372M × $11.13) to $15.83B, against about $2.47B retained (net income $5.80B less cash dividends of about $2.99B and the $0.34B in-kind dividend). That is about $4.70 per $1. This figure understates the result per share, because buybacks shrank the share count.

**Where the retained capital went, by use:**

| Use | Evidence | Return | Assessment |
|---|---|---|---|
| Core concentrate business | 1988 soft-drink capex $239M; advertising $842M (expensed) | About 44% pre-tax on identifiable assets | Excellent; this is the source of value |
| Share buybacks | $2.19B since 1984 | Earnings yield 6–9% at purchase, growing | Good |
| Entertainment (Columbia 1982, Embassy and Tandem 1985 ~$445M, Merv Griffin 1986 ~$200M) | 1986 operating income $235M on $2.17B of assets, about 11% pre-tax; now a 49% CPE stake carried at $598M; Coke's share of CPE's 1988 result was a loss of about $44M after a $114M write-down | Below the 12% hurdle | **A mistake of the kind in `brk-letter-1981#3` (misjudged economics).** Much of it was recovered: $545M of CPE notes collected in cash, and the dividend in kind returned 34.1M CPE shares to Coke owners. The packet does not show the final value created or lost. |
| Bottler stakes (CCE, TCC, Johnston, Consolidated, U.K. joint venture, about 30 companies) | $1.31B carried in bottler affiliates; 1988 equity income from CCE about $64M on $733M (includes a gain share), others about $29M on $485M | About 5–9% | Low direct returns. The stated purpose is to strengthen the system that supports the moat. U.S. share gains and CCE's efficiency support that purpose, but it is unproven at Coke's own hurdle. Listed stakes are worth $291M more than book. The structure also moves bottling debt off Coke's balance sheet. |
| Foods | 1988 capex $82M; identifiable assets $694M; operating income $89M | About 13% pre-tax, roughly 8.5% after tax | Below the 12% hurdle. Small enough that it is not a Burlington-style trap (`brk-letter-1985#9`), but management keeps reinvesting in it. |
| Dividends | Payout cut from about 65% (1983) to 42% (1988), targeting about 40% | n/a | Retaining more is justified only because it funds the core and buybacks. The 13.3% increase in the 1989 dividend shows payouts still growing. |

**Finding.** The retained-earnings test passes by a wide margin. That success comes from the core franchise and the buybacks. Most of the side investments (entertainment, foods, bottler stakes) have earned less than management's own 12% hurdle. The trend is favorable: entertainment has been exited, coffee sold, and more than 90% of operating income now comes from soft drinks.

## What the investor would have relied on that the packet lacks

1. **Meeting the managers.** The framework puts great weight on "people we like and admire" (`brk-letter-1987#18`). It also stresses judging "the quality of the people we will be joining" (`brk-letter-1986#10`). The packet has no conversations, no assessment of Goizueta's and Keough's character, and no indication of any personal acquaintance. That is the central gap at this stage.
2. **The proxy statement:** individual pay, director and officer ownership (the "eat our own cooking" test in `brk-letter-1983#0`), and board independence.
3. **Terms and later returns of the 1982 Columbia purchase,** including how many shares were issued and at what price. Without these, the entertainment episode cannot be measured fully.
4. **How management decides the price of buybacks,** and whether it has an internal estimate of intrinsic value.
5. **Market value of the CPE stake and of the CPE shares distributed,** to score the entertainment exit.
6. **Plans for the remaining $727M of debt capacity** and the 14.4M shares left in the buyback authorization. In particular: would surplus cash go to buybacks, or to new diversification?
7. **Bottler-system economics** (repeated from Stage 2), to judge whether the $1.3B in bottler equity is truly protecting the moat.
8. **Who owns large blocks of Coke stock,** and whether any holder or foundation influences policy.

---

## Stage 3 conclusion

- **Management is able and shareholder-oriented.** The record since 1981 easily passes the $1-retained test, at about $5 of market value per $1 retained. That rests on a strengthened core franchise and $2.2B of repurchases at earnings yields of 6–9%.
- **Capital allocation has improved.** The entertainment diversification of 1982–86, partly paid for with cheaply issued stock, was a mistake. It has largely been unwound without a fire sale, and capital is now concentrated on soft drinks.
- **Remaining cautions:**
  - Buybacks are justified by surplus cash rather than an explicit discount to value, and some were paid for with temporary debt.
  - The 35% debt-to-capital ceiling is more leverage than the framework favors.
  - Bottler stakes and Foods earn below management's own hurdle.
  - Pay is tied to the stock price.
  - Reporting is promotional in tone, though candid in its financial disclosure.
- **What depends on Stage 4:** whether today's buybacks and today's purchase price are "well below" intrinsic value.
- **Overall:** management passes the framework's test, subject to the in-person character assessment the packet cannot supply.
