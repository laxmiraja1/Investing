# Stage 2: Business analysis

The framework's business-analysis section has three steps, applied here in order:
1. Return on equity, judged without undue leverage or accounting gimmickry.
2. The moat.
3. Owner earnings.

Valuation and management judgments are left for later stages.

---

## 3a. Return on equity, not EPS growth (`brk-letter-1979`)

The test is "a high earnings rate on equity capital employed (without undue leverage, accounting gimmickry, etc.)". There are three questions: what the reported ROE is, how much of it comes from one-time items, and how much comes from leverage and a shrunken equity base.

**Reported ROE (income from continuing operations to average equity), from the selected financial data in `ar-1986`, `ar-1987` and `ar-1988`:**

| Year | ROE | Total debt / total capital | Comment |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1979 | 21.1% | 6.3% | Almost no debt |
| 1980 | 19.7% | 9.3% | |
| 1981 | 19.9% | 9.1% | Current management takes over |
| 1982 | 19.6% | 15.1% | Columbia Pictures bought |
| 1983 | 19.4% | 17.3% | |
| 1984 | 21.8% | 32.4% | |
| 1985 | 23.5% | 30.2% | |
| 1986 | 28.8% | 33.1% | Includes the $375M CCE share-sale gain and $180M of charges |
| 1987 | 27.2% | 48.4% (restated) | Includes the $40M TCC gain and a $36M charge |
| 1988 | 33.3% | 38.8% | No unusual operating items |

**Removing one-time items.**
- In 1986, the CCE gain added $262M after tax and the provisions cost about $128M after tax (`ar-1986`). Without them, net income is about $800M and ROE about **24.6%**, not 28.8%.
- In 1987 the gain and the charge roughly cancel, so ROE stays at about **27%**.
- 1988 is close to clean. Operating income of $1,598M less $31M of net interest expense, taxed at 34%, gives about $1,034M, against reported net income of $1,045M.
- **Underlying trend:** about 20% for 1979–83, rising to roughly 23.5%, 24.6%, 27% and 33% over 1985–88. The ten-year average of reported ROE is about 23.4%.

**Accounting signals to note.**
- The financial statements have been restated three years running: entertainment and TCC moved to the equity method, then the finance subsidiary was consolidated.
- Gains from selling shares of subsidiaries were booked as income: $375M in 1986, $40M in 1987, plus the share of CCE's $104M gain in 1988.
- Creating CCE and TCC moved the bottlers' debt off Coke's balance sheet. CCE carried $2.86B of liabilities against $4.67B of assets at the end of 1988.

None of this is fraudulent. It does mean the reported ROE should not be accepted at face value.

**Leverage and buybacks.**
- Treasury stock grew from $11.6M at the start of 1984 to $2,069M at the end of 1988. Separately, $335M of equity left as the CPE dividend in kind.
- As a result, average common equity in 1988 ($3,116M) was *below* 1986's, even though earnings rose about 30%.
- Part of the rise from 24% to 33% therefore comes from a smaller denominator rather than from better business economics.

**Returns with leverage and idle capital removed (my calculations, 1988):**
- *Return on net capital.* Add back after-tax net interest: $1,044.7M + $31.2M × 0.66 = about $1,065M. Divide by average debt plus equity less cash: ($4,238M + $4,693M) / 2 = $4,466M. The result is about **24% unlevered**, still double the 12% after-tax cost of capital management cites.
- *Return on operating capital.* That 24% is held down by about $1.9–2.2B of stakes in bottlers and CPE. In 1988 these produced only $48M of equity income, and CPE lost money after a $114M write-down.
- After-tax operating income (about $1,055M) divided by net capital excluding cash and affiliate stakes (about $2.4B average) gives roughly **40–45% after tax**.
- **Segment check** (operating income divided by identifiable assets, pre-tax, from `ar-1988` note 15):

| | 1986 (before charges) | 1987 | 1988 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Soft drinks, U.S. | 20.6% | 15.8% | 20.6% |
| Soft drinks, international | 47.7% | 52.1% | **63.8%** |
| Soft drinks, combined | about 36% | 34.3% | **44.4%** |
| Foods | 20.3% | 10.6% (12.4% before the charge) | 12.9% |

**Finding.** The concentrate business earns returns as high as any the framework cites. `brk-letter-1987#1` notes that only 6 of the 1,000 largest companies averaged more than 30% ROE, with the best at 40.2%. Leverage did not create those returns. Consolidated ROE *understates* the core business, because capital has gone into low-return bottling, film and orange-juice businesses. It also *overstates* it in the short run, because buybacks have shrunk the equity base. The economics of the core are clearly excellent. Whether capital allocation lives up to them is a question for Stage 3.

## 3b. Durable competitive advantage: the moat (`brk-letter-1986#16`)

The question is what protects these returns from competitors, and whether that protection will still hold in ten or more years.

**Evidence of a moat:**
1. **Share, and its direction.**
   - About 45% of world flavored carbonated soft drinks (excluding China and the USSR), up from under 38% in 1980 (`ar-1988`). That is more than twice the nearest competitor.
   - 47% of international volume.
   - More than 40% of the U.S. market, 9 share points ahead of the main competitor (`ar-1986`).
   - In U.S. fountain sales: 59% share, more than twice the next competitor's sales, and about three-quarters of the segment's growth over four years (`ar-1987`).
   - This is a share that is still widening after a century.
2. **Pricing power, measured by gross margin.**
   - Concentrate sold to U.S. bottlers carries about a 63% gross margin (`ar-1986`).
   - Consolidated gross margin (restated basis) rose from 50.5% in 1985 to 52.6% in 1987 and **55.6%** in 1988.
   - Operating margin before charges rose from about 15.4% in 1986 to 17.8% in 1987 and 19.2% in 1988.
   - The new CCE contracts give Coke "flexible pricing" at its own discretion, and it used that to raise prices in October 1986.
   - Management states that it "is able to adjust prices to compensate for increasing costs." That matters given the framework's inflation worries (`fortune-1977-inflation`, `brk-letter-1979#2`).
3. **Brand.**
   - The Landor surveys of 1988 put the gap between Coca-Cola and the second brand wider than the gap between the second and tenth.
   - Servings grew from 359M a day in 1980 to 559M (+56%).
   - The 1985 new-taste episode, covered as "stories of social significance", ended with the original formula restored as Coca-Cola classic. Classic became the best-selling U.S. soft drink in 1986 and grew 7% more in 1988.
   - This is the "girl next door" business of `brk-letter-1987#3`: little change, exceptional returns.
4. **Low cost and distribution.**
   - The company describes itself as the "low-cost producer in most of the 155 countries" (`ar-1987`).
   - It has more than 1,000 bottlers in more than 160 countries, 700,000+ vending machines in Japan alone, and a fountain network.
   - Like GEICO's cost gap, this scale is something a competitor cannot cheaply copy.
5. **Room to grow.**
   - International consumption per person is about 51 servings a year (`ar-1988`) against 274 in the U.S. (`ar-1987`); the 1986 report put it at 16% of the U.S. rate.
   - U.S. consumption per person itself grew 35% from 1980 to 1987.
6. **Low capital needs.**
   - 1988 soft-drink capex was $239M against $1,691M of soft-drink operating income, about 14%.
   - Soft-drink revenue was about 1.8 times its identifiable assets.
   - Growth therefore does not consume capital, which is the trait praised in Berkshire's own units (`brk-letter-1987#1`).

**Limits of the moat:**
- **U.S.:** "intensely competitive". The U.S. operating margin is 15.4%, against 29.7% internationally, and U.S. returns on assets are only about 16–21% pre-tax. The moat is widest abroad.
- **Currency:** about 76% of operating income is earned outside the U.S. Key hard currencies strengthened 27% (1986), 16% (1987) and 10% (1988) against the dollar. A good part of the 1985–88 soft-drink operating income growth (from $830M to $1,691M, about 27% a year) is translation gain, and it could reverse.
- **Latin America:** triple-digit inflation in Brazil and Argentina caused volume and operating income to fall there in 1988.
- **Outside the moat:** Foods is commodity-like. Frozen and chilled orange juice volume fell 22% and 6% in 1988, its margin is about 6%, and it ranks second in chilled. CPE is a film business that has just written down $114M. Neither has the franchise.
- **Tax test** (`brk-letter-1986#22`): the statutory rate fell from 46% to 34% while operating and net margins rose. That fits a franchise that keeps tax cuts for itself, but currency and buybacks make the test inconclusive.

**Finding.** The concentrate franchise has a wide moat that is getting wider, with consumer preference, cost and distribution all reinforcing one another. Of all the parts of Coke's economics, this one is the most predictable ten years out. The main uncertainties are currency translation and how much capital management diverts outside the core.

## 3c. Owner earnings (`brk-letter-1986`)

The formula is reported earnings, plus depreciation and amortization and other non-cash charges, less the average capital spending needed to maintain competitive position and volume.

| $M | 1986 (adjusted) | 1987 | 1988 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Net income | 934 → about 800 after removing the gain and charges | 916 | 1,045 |
| + Depreciation and amortization | 155 | 155 | 170 |
| − Equity income not received in cash | (132) | (80) | (36) |
| − Total capital spending | (346) | (304) | (387) |
| **Owner earnings if all capex is maintenance (floor)** | **about 480** | **about 690** | **about 790** |
| Owner earnings if maintenance capex ≈ depreciation (ceiling) | about 670 | about 835 | about 1,010 |

- 1988 range: **about $790M to $1,010M**. That is about **$2.25–$2.85 per share** on 354.8M year-end shares, or $2.17–$2.77 on 364.6M average shares.
- Working capital is not a drain. It released cash in 1987, and 1988's operating cash flow of $1,167M exceeded net income.
- The true figure is probably near the top of the range. 1988 capex explicitly included "capacity expansion" and new plants in China, France and elsewhere, which is growth spending rather than maintenance.
- Advertising rose from $648M (1986) to $720M (1987) to $842M (1988) and is expensed. It is effectively the cost of keeping the moat and is already deducted from earnings.
- Owner earnings for the *system* are lower than Coke's own. Bottlers carry heavy capital and debt (CCE), and Coke supports them with $68M of cooperative marketing paid to CCE and about $167M of finance-subsidiary loans to Johnston. How much of the system's capital burden flows back to Coke over time is an open question.

## Facts the framework wants that the packet does not contain

1. **Maintenance versus growth capex**, by segment and especially for international soft drinks. This is the largest gap in the owner-earnings estimate.
2. **Breakdown of operating income growth into volume, price, mix and currency**, by region. Only the overall currency movement is given.
3. **Competitor data**: Pepsi's U.S. and international share, margins and concentrate pricing, needed to judge whether the U.S. gap is stable.
4. **Long-run real price per serving** and pricing history, to confirm pricing power holds beyond 1986–88.
5. **Bottler-system economics**: debt, returns and capex across the 30 or so equity-held bottlers, Coke's support payments, and whether the system's capital needs eventually fall back on Coke.
6. **Fair value of affiliate stakes.** The packet gives only a $291M aggregate excess over carrying value, with no separate CPE or CCE market values.
7. **Option and restricted-stock dilution economics**: 8.66M options outstanding and price-linked incentive units.
8. **1988 interim 10-Qs** and longer segment histories before 1984 on a consistent basis.
9. **Off-balance-sheet items** in full: $133M of guarantees, $101M of recourse on CPE receivables, a pending FASB 96 charge of about $175M to equity, and post-retirement health costs (described only as "not significant").

## Stage 2 conclusion

- Coke's core business earns about 40–45% after tax on operating capital, with little need for reinvestment.
- That return rests on a moat that is growing: rising world share, rising gross margin, a unique brand, low-cost distribution, and international consumption per person still at a small fraction of the U.S. level.
- Consolidated ROE (33%) is a mixed signal. Buybacks and moderate debt inflate it, while low-return stakes in bottlers, films and orange juice depress it. Underlying, unlevered returns are still about twice the cost of capital.
- 1988 owner earnings are about $0.8–1.0B, or $2.25–$2.85 per share.
- What to carry forward:
  - currency dependence;
  - the capital that has gone outside the core (a Stage 3 question about management's capital allocation);
  - using owner earnings rather than headline EPS when valuing at $46.56.
