# Stage 3: Management and capital allocation (framework §4)

The framework has three rules in this section:
- **4.1** Honest, able management already in place, with candid reporting (`brk-letter-1979`, `brk-letter-1983#2`).
- **4.2** Buy back stock only when the price is well below intrinsic value (`brk-letter-1984`).
- **4.3** Each $1 retained must create at least $1 of market value (`brk-letter-1983`).

I take the measurable tests first, 4.3 and then 4.2. The judgment about people, 4.1, comes last because it rests on them.

## 4.3 The $1-retained test, 1981–1988

The current team took charge in 1981 (`ar-1987`: "When we assembled our present management team in 1981…"), so the test window starts at end-1980.

**Earnings retained, 1981–88** (selected data in `ar-1988`):

| Item | $M |
|---|---|
| Net income, 1981–88 | ≈ 5,799 |
| − Cash dividends (per-share dividend × average shares) | ≈ (2,988) |
| − CPE in-kind dividend | (335) |
| − Preferred dividends | (6) |
| **Earnings retained** | **≈ 2,470** |

**Change in market value:**
- End-1980: $11.13 × 372M shares ≈ **$4.1B**
- End-1988: $44.63 × 354.8M shares ≈ **$15.8B** (`ar-1988` gives "more than $15.8 billion")
- Increase: **≈ $11.7B**

The increase came even though about $2.2B of cash left the company through buybacks between 1984 and 1988, and the CPE shares were spun out.

**Result: about $4.70 of market value for each $1 retained.** The test asks for at least $1.

**Checking for re-rating.** Part of the gain comes from a higher price-to-earnings multiple, which rose from about 10x 1980 EPS to about 16x 1988 EPS. I strip that out by holding the multiple at 1980's roughly 10x:
- 9.8 × $2.85 ≈ $28 per share, or about $9.9B in market value
- That is still a gain of about $5.8B on $2.5B retained, more than **2:1**.

The test passes clearly. It does so because the core business needs little capital (Stage 2), so most retained cash ended up in buybacks and bottler stakes, not in low-return projects.

### Reinvestment and acquisitions: the record is mixed

The packet shows four groups of deals.

**1. Entertainment: the clear misallocation.**
- Deals: Columbia Pictures (1982, purchase price not in packet), Embassy and Tandem (1985: $178M cash plus $267M, part of it about $169M in Coke stock), and Merv Griffin (1986, about $200M).
- Results: by 1986 the sector had $2.17B of identifiable assets earning $235M of pretax operating income, about 11%. Soft drinks earned about 29% on $3.46B in the same year (`ar-1986` note 17).
- Share issuance: the Embassy deal paid for a lower-quality business partly with stock in an excellent one. `brk-letter-1982#17` warns about exactly this. The packet does not give Embassy's value, so I cannot tell whether the issue diluted value.
- Exit: in 1987–88 entertainment was merged into CPE, 51% was distributed to shareholders, and Coke's 49% stake was then written down. CPE took a $114M inventory charge, and Coke booked a 1988 equity loss of about $44M (`ar-1988`).
- Mitigant: the publicly traded equity investees, CPE among them, were worth about $291M more than carrying value at end-1988 (`ar-1988` note 3). So the loss was contained, not catastrophic.

**2. Foods.** Small add-ons: Nutri-Foods for $30M and the Hood citrus foodservice business for $45M. Earnings were weak, with restructuring in 1987. Coffee was sold in January 1989.

**3. Divestitures of low-return businesses.** Ronco pasta (1984), Presto and Winkler plastics (1985, $112M), Embassy Home Entertainment and Embassy Pictures (1986), coffee (1989). Management does sell what fails its cost-of-capital test, and it does not cling to unprofitable businesses.

**4. Bottler restructuring.** CCE, TCC and the British joint venture moved capital-heavy operations off Coke's balance sheet and produced gains. Equity stakes in Johnston, Consolidated and roughly 30 other bottlers support the core franchise.

**Overall.** The early-1980s drift into entertainment and diversified foods is the kind of empire-building `brk-letter-1981#3` cautions against ("The empire would have been larger, but the citizenry would have been poorer"). From 1986 to 1988, however, management reversed course. By 1988 more than 90% of operating income came from soft drinks, and the stated rule is to invest only above an estimated 12% after-tax cost of capital (`ar-1986`, `ar-1987`, `ar-1988` MD&A). Having a measuring standard, and visibly applying it, is a good sign.

## 4.2 Buybacks only well below intrinsic value

Average prices paid, from the shares-repurchased and cost lines in the statements of equity and cash flows:

| Year | Shares bought (M) | Cost ($M) | Avg. price | P/E on that year's EPS |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1984 | 18.4 | 336 | ~$18.25 | ~11.6x |
| 1985 | 15.0 | 380 | ~$25.33 | ~14.7x |
| 1986 | 2.9 | 110 | ~$38.37 | ~18x (on ~$2.09 excluding the CCE gain and provisions) |
| 1987 | 14.2 | 605 | ~$42.70 | ~17.6x |
| 1988 | 19.0 | 758 | ~$39.93 | ~14.0x |

**Judgment.**
- Stage 2 put owner earnings at about $2.50 a share. That figure grows mid-teens with little added capital, and roughly all of it can be paid out.
- On that basis, purchases at 11–15x earnings (1984, 1985, 1988) were very likely well below intrinsic value.
- The 1986–87 purchases at about 18x are less clearly "well below." The packet does not show their timing within the year: 1987's range ran from $29 to $53, so whether purchases fell before or after the October drop matters.
- Firmly calling these "well below" requires the intrinsic value estimate, which belongs to Stage 4. I flag it here as provisional.

**Is the rationale owner-oriented?** Yes. Management says it buys back stock "as long as investing in our own stock remains one of the best uses of our excess cash" and only after "available cash and debt capacity exceeded the funds required" (`ar-1988` MD&A). That is a returns test, not propping up EPS. One caution: the 1987 buying was partly debt-funded (short-term debt rose about $1.2B), and in 1988 the company issued $300M of preferred. So the buybacks are partly leverage rather than surplus cash.

**Dilution from stock issued to employees is small.** About 0.9–1.3M shares a year are issued to employees, against 14–19M bought back. Share count fell from 386.8M (1986 average) to 354.8M at end-1988.

## 4.1 Honest, able management in place, and candid reporting

### Ability
- Under Goizueta and Keough (CEO and President since 1981), ROE rose from about 20% to 33%.
- World share rose from under 38% to about 45%.
- The business was refocused on its franchise.
- The company now explicitly measures returns against a cost of capital and sets its goal per share: "Management's primary objective is to increase shareholder value over time."
- This is closer to the owner orientation of `brk-letter-1983#0` than most large-company managements show.
- Management is clearly in place, which satisfies "We can't supply it." A visible second tier is listed in the officer rosters: Ivester (CFO), Herbert, Halle.

### Candor: on balance good, with some promotional tone

What counts in favor:
- **Admits problems.** "We were not satisfied with the performance of Columbia Pictures' feature films" (`ar-1986`). "Our foods business is obviously in transition" (`ar-1987`).
- **Flags unusual items.** Every one-time item (the $375M and $40M gains, the $180M and $36M provisions) is separately identified, with comparisons that exclude them.
- **Discloses currency.** The currency tailwind is quantified every year.
- **Concedes uncomfortable points.** Current-cost income "would be less than reported." Monetary-liability gains "do not necessarily imply that incurring more debt would be beneficial." These are statements a promoter would leave out.

What counts against:
- **Promotional language.** The reports carry "$250 billion added to the world economy" multiplier claims, a strong emphasis on stock-price total return, and modern-finance vocabulary ("using financial leverage," "more aggressive, efficient use of the financial resources").
- **Frequent restatements.** Repeated deconsolidations make long-term comparison harder for an outside reader, and they flatter reported returns, as noted in Stage 2.

On the standard of `brk-letter-1983#2` ("tell you the business facts that we would want to know if our positions were reversed"), the reports mostly pass. They are not as plain-spoken as Berkshire's own.

### Appetite for leverage: a caution, not a disqualifier
- In 1986 management raised its net debt-to-capital ceiling to 35% specifically to lower the cost of capital.
- The framework's preference is for little or no debt (`brk-letter-1982`), and the franchise does not need any.
- Interest coverage is about 7x, and the borrowing funds buybacks rather than operations, so it is tolerable.
- Still, it shows managers who will apply financial engineering to a business that doesn't require it.

### Compensation (limited data)
- **Restricted stock:** grants rose from 178,500 shares (1987) to 511,500 (1988), about $20M at around $40 a share.
- **Options:** grants jumped from 1.1M to 4.18M; 8.66M are outstanding, about 2.4% of shares.
- **Cash awards:** cash "Performance Unit" awards on 555,000 shares above a $20.63 base, and new 1988 "Incentive Unit" awards on the full value of 300,000 shares.
- **Reading:** pay is tied to the stock price, which aligns management with shareholders. Fixed-price options, however, reward retained earnings that pile up even if returns on them are poor, and the 1988 step-up in grants is large. I cannot judge whether pay is reasonable without the proxy statement.

## What the investor would have relied on that the packet lacks

1. **Direct personal judgment of Goizueta and Keough.** The framework puts heavy weight on liking and trusting the people (`brk-letter-1987#18` on Murphy and Burke, `brk-letter-1984#7` on the Blumkins). The packet provides only their written reports. Meetings or conversations with them, and the testimony of people who have dealt with them, would have been the decisive evidence for 4.1.
2. **The proxy statement:** executive pay totals, insider shareholdings, board independence, and option terms.
3. **The total capital put into entertainment** (the Columbia purchase price, later additions) and the realized value on exit, to score the largest capital-allocation error precisely.
4. **When within each year the buybacks were made**, especially in 1987.
5. **Management's own view of intrinsic value** and how it decides between buying back stock and investing in bottlers.

## Stage 3 verdict

Management passes.

- **4.3:** Retained earnings have produced roughly $2–4.70 of market value per $1 retained, a clear pass.
- **4.2:** Most buybacks were made at modest multiples of a high-return, compounding business. Whether they were "well below" intrinsic value is provisional until Stage 4.
- **4.1:** Management is able, in place, owner-oriented in its stated goals, and reasonably candid. It corrected its main mistake, the entertainment and foods diversification of the early 1980s, rather than defending it.

**Cautions carried forward:** a taste for voluntary leverage, a promotional style, and a sharp increase in equity compensation in 1988. None of these is disqualifying, but the investor would want a personal read on the two principals before committing a large share of capital.
