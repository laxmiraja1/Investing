# Stage 4: Valuation (framework §5) and Catalyst / timing (§6)

## 5.1 The framework's valuation rule as written: buy below net working capital

The only rule in the Valuation section is the partnership-era bargain test: net working capital alone, before any value for plant, should exceed the price (`bpl-letter-1965`). Applied to Coca-Cola's balance sheet at 1988 year-end (`ar-1988`, $M):

| Item | $M |
|---|---|
| Current assets | 3,245.4 |
| − Current liabilities | (2,868.9) |
| **Net working capital** | **376.5**, about **$1.06/share** |
| − Long-term debt, other liabilities, deferred taxes | (1,236.4) |
| − Preferred stock | (300.0) |
| **Net-net (current assets less all liabilities and preferred)** | **≈ −$1.16B, negative** |

Common equity per share is about $8.58 ($3,045M / 354.8M shares). Against a price of $46.56, the stock trades at about **5.4x book** and about **44x net working capital**. **Coca-Cola fails the bargain test by a wide margin.**

This rule does not decide the case. The rule set in force on the as-of date has moved past it. The 1965 test was a tool for "generals" bought on quantitative cheapness. By 1988 the investor's own documents set a different standard:

- "Our goal is to find an outstanding business at a sensible price, not a mediocre business at a bargain price." He calls the years spent searching for "bargains" a mistake that cost him an education in farm implements, department stores and textiles (`brk-letter-1987#18`).
- Businesses should be bought "at prices that will make sense, even should the future of the acquired enterprise develop much along the lines of its past." Marketable stakes are taken in "companies with excellent business franchises and competent, honest managements" (`brk-letter-1981#3`).
- Owner earnings, not book value or working capital, is the relevant earnings figure (`brk-letter-1986`). Value is judged per share as "intrinsic business value" (`brk-letter-1983#0`).

So I apply the operative standard: **capitalize owner earnings, assume the future resembles the past, and test whether the price makes sense.** The packet sets no numeric hurdle. The partnership letters ask for "a comfortable margin of safety" (`bpl-letter-1961#3`, `bpl-letter-1962#4`). I interpret that as requiring the price to sit below value even under conservative assumptions.

## 5.2 Inputs

**Owner earnings (from Stage 2).** About $2.50/share is the working figure, with a range of $2.34–2.95. The low end subtracts all capex and recurring bottler support. The high end treats capex above depreciation as growth spending.

**Growth if the future resembles the past.** These figures come from the packet:
- EPS from continuing operations grew about 13.6% a year from 1981 to 1988. Net income grew about 13.4% a year.
- Worldwide unit volume grew 6–10% a year from 1985 to 1988.
- Margins rose and the share count fell.
- Management guides 1989 EPS growth "well into the mid-teens" (`upi-1989-01-31`). Following `brk-letter-1982`, I give projections no weight.

Part of the 1986–88 growth came from a weakening dollar, so I haircut the record.

**Discount rate.** The packet does not give the long-term US Treasury yield on the as-of date, so this is an assumption. The packet's interest-rate evidence:
- Coca-Cola's dollar notes carry coupons of 9.4–12.75%.
- Its finance unit's 1990 bond pays 9.32%.
- Management puts its after-tax cost of capital at about 12%.

I use **9%** as an approximate government-bond yield and **10%** as a conservative case. I use the bond yield instead of management's 12% figure because the framework compares a business to the risk-free alternative and handles risk through conservative assumptions, not through a higher rate.

## 5.3 Intrinsic value per share

Each case runs two stages: owner earnings grow at g₁ for 10 years, then at g₂ forever, discounted at r.

| Case | OE base | g₁ (yrs 1–10) | g₂ (after) | r | PV yrs 1–10 | PV of terminal | **Value/share** | Price as % of value |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A. Past repeats, haircut | $2.50 | 12% | 5% | 9% | $29.1 | $86.1 | **≈ $115** | 40% |
| B. Conservative | $2.50 | 10% | 4% | 10% | $25.0 | $43.3 | **≈ $68** | 68% |
| C. What the price implies | $2.34 | 7% | 3% | 10% | $20.2 | $26.1 | **≈ $46** | ~100% |
| No growth at all | $2.50 | 0% | 0% | 9% | | | **≈ $28** | 167% |

**What the price assumes.** At $46.56 the market prices Coca-Cola as if owner earnings, taken at the low estimate, grow only about 7% a year for a decade and about 3% after, discounted at 10%. That is roughly half the record of the past eight years, for a business with:
- about 40% after-tax returns on the capital it uses in the core business,
- international per-capita consumption at about one-fifth of the US level,
- capital needs low enough to fund that growth internally (Stage 2).

### Cross-checks

**1. Sum of the parts (from `ar-1988`).** I separate the non-core pieces to isolate what the market pays for the core operation.

| Per share | |
|---|---|
| Price | $46.56 |
| − Affiliates (carrying $1,912M + $291M excess of market value over carrying) | −$6.20 |
| + Net debt ($2,124M − $1,231M cash) | +$2.52 |
| + Preferred | +$0.85 |
| **Implied price of the core operating business** | **≈ $43.70** |

The core earned about $1,017M after tax in 1988, roughly $2.87/share (net income excluding equity income, with net interest added back). That puts the core at **about 15x earnings**, for a franchise of the quality described in Stage 2.

**2. Equity coupon versus bond coupon.** The owner-earnings yield today is 2.50 / 46.56 = **5.4%**. A 9% bond pays a fixed 9%. If owner earnings grow 12%, the yield on today's cost reaches about 9.5% in year 5 and about 16.7% in year 10. If they grow 10%, it passes 9% in about year 6. The bond's coupon never rises. With only a few years of growth, the stock comes out ahead.

**3. Earlier multiples.** At 16.3x 1988 EPS the stock trades below the roughly 18.5x paid in early 1987, when the price was about $45 on $2.43. It is well above the roughly 10x of 1980–81, but the business today is clearly better: return on equity of 33% against 22%, and a market share of 45% against under 38%.

## 5.4 Margin of safety

- **Conservative case (B): about 32% below value** ($46.56 against about $68).
- **Base case (A): about 60% below value.**
- **Most pessimistic case (C):** the price is about equal to value.

The margin of safety here is a different kind from the one in the 1965 rule. Assets give no protection: the net-net figure is negative and the price is 5.4x book. The protection comes from the durability and predictability of the earning power. Paying a fair price for that is an accepted trade under the post-1979 framework ("a good business purchased at a fair price," `brk-letter-1979`). The risk this exposes is a misjudgment of the business's economics, which `brk-letter-1981#3` lists as the most common mistake. Stage 2 found little sign of that risk in the core business.

**Threats to the value estimate:**
1. **Currency.** Seventy-six percent of operating income is earned abroad, and three years of dollar weakness flattered the record. A stronger dollar would slow reported growth, though not the underlying volume.
2. **Interest rates.** If long rates are well above 9%, every case falls. Case B at r = 11% is about $55, which still exceeds the price.
3. **Management raising leverage or diversifying again** (Stage 3 cautions).
4. **Non-core drags.** The CPE stake and Foods are small, but they are not franchise-quality.

**Stage 3 follow-up (§4.2).** Against a conservative value of about $68, the 1988 buybacks at about $40 and the 1987 buybacks at about $42.70 were made at roughly 60% of value. That fits "well below intrinsic value." The repurchase record passes.

## Catalyst / timing (framework §6)

- **This is not a work-out or a control position** (`bpl-letter-1957`, `bpl-letter-1963`). No corporate event is pending, and a control stake in a $16.5B company is neither possible nor wanted.
- **No catalyst is needed.** "The market may ignore business success for a while, but eventually will confirm it" (`brk-letter-1987`). Volume growth, margin gains and the reduction in share count each compound per-share value without any market event.
- **No market forecasting** (`bpl-letter-1966-mid`, `fortune-1977-inflation`). The stock is up about 25% from its 1988 low of roughly $37 and sits near its 52-week high of $49.12. That says nothing about value. Mr. Market "is there to serve you, not to guide you" (`brk-letter-1987`). The price is not at a peak multiple, and nothing in the packet suggests crowd euphoria about this stock that would call for fear (`brk-letter-1986`). Waiting for a lower price would be a forecast, and the framework does not make forecasts.
- **Practical timing.** A position large enough to matter to Berkshire would take months to build through open-market buying. The packet has no volume data, so I can't estimate how far buying would move the price. The disclosure policy in `brk-letter-1983#2` (no comment on purchases) would matter while the position is built.

## Facts the valuation needs that the packet lacks

1. **The long-term Treasury yield on 1989-02-27** (assumed about 9%) and the inflation rate.
2. **The split of capex into maintenance and growth**, which sets the owner-earnings base (Stage 2).
3. **Growth at constant currency**, to separate business progress from the dollar's moves.
4. **Trading volume and float**, to judge how fast a large position can be built.
5. **The market value of the CPE stake on its own** (the packet gives only the total excess over carrying value for all listed affiliates).

## Stage 4 verdict

Under the 1965 net-working-capital rule, Coca-Cola is not a bargain and is not close to one. Under the owner-earnings standard the framework uses in 1989, the picture is different. A price of $46.56 against a conservative intrinsic value of about $68 (base case about $115) gives a margin of safety of roughly a third or more. That margin rests on an exceptionally durable franchise, not on assets. The market price assumes only about half the company's demonstrated growth rate. No catalyst is required, and timing considerations do not argue for waiting.
