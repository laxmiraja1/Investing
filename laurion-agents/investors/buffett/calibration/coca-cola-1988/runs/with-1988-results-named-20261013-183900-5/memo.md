**Memo: Purchase of The Coca-Cola Company, February 27, 1989**

We recommend buying shares of The Coca-Cola Company at about $46.56 and building the position over several months to roughly a quarter of the portfolio. We intend to hold it indefinitely. This memo explains what the business is, why it passes our tests, what we think it is worth, and what would change our view.

Coca-Cola is mainly a seller of soft-drink concentrate and syrup. More than 90 percent of 1988 operating income came from that activity. The company makes the flavoring base and markets the brands. More than a thousand independent and partly owned bottlers do the capital-heavy work of bottling, canning and delivery in over 160 countries. The company's products account for about 45 percent of the world's flavored carbonated soft drinks, more than twice any competitor, and for over 40 percent of the US market. The business is easy to understand, and nothing in it depends on technology we cannot follow. What matters over the next decade is whether people outside the United States drink more soft drinks per person, and whether the company can keep pricing above its costs. Americans consume about 274 servings of the company's drinks a year. The average elsewhere is about 51.

The first question was whether this is a good business independent of price. The record says it is exceptional. Return on equity has stayed above roughly 20 percent in every year since 1978, and it reached 33 percent in 1988. Part of the recent rise comes from borrowing and from buying back stock, so we do not lean on it. The important evidence is earlier: from 1978 through 1981, debt was under a tenth of capital and the company still earned 21 to 23 percent on equity. The franchise produced those returns before management added any leverage. If we strip out cash, debt, and the company's stakes in bottlers and in Columbia Pictures, the core business earns roughly 40 percent after tax on the capital it actually uses. Gross margins are about 56 percent. Capital spending of $387 million in 1988 was small next to $1.7 billion of soft-drink operating income, and much of that spending went to coolers, vending machines and new plants abroad, which are for growth. Because so little capital is needed to maintain the business, the cash that owners could take out each year is close to reported net income, about $2.50 a share on a conservative measure.

The second question was management. Roberto Goizueta and Donald Keough have run the company since 1981. Over that period the company kept about $2.5 billion of earnings, and its market value rose by about $11.7 billion, even after $2.2 billion was spent buying back shares. Even with no credit for the higher price-earnings multiple, each retained dollar produced more than two dollars of market value. The 1987 and 1988 buybacks were made at around $40 to $43 a share, well below what we think the shares are worth. Management did make a serious error in the early 1980s by moving into film and television. It admitted the problem in its reports and has mostly undone it: the entertainment business now sits in a 49 percent-owned affiliate, and weaker food and plastics businesses have been sold. We have three reservations. Management uses more debt than the business needs. Its reports are sometimes promotional. And it sharply increased stock awards in 1988. None of these disqualifies the company, but we want to meet both executives before the position reaches full size.

On price, this is not a bargain in the old statistical sense. The stock trades at more than five times book value, and working capital covers almost none of the price. What we are paying for is earning power that is predictable and growing. We estimated value with three assumptions: owner earnings of $2.50 a share growing 10 percent a year for a decade and 4 percent after, discounted at 10 percent. That gives roughly $68 a share, a margin of about a third below value. If the company comes close to repeating its record of the 1980s, which was about 13 to 14 percent annual growth in earnings per share, the value is well above $100. To justify today's price, earnings only need to grow about 7 percent for ten years and 3 percent afterward. Seen another way, the market is paying about 15 times after-tax earnings for the core business once the investments and net debt are separated out. We do not need any event to unlock the value. Each year of growth, wider margins and fewer shares outstanding adds to what every share is worth.

The main risks are known. About three-quarters of operating income is earned abroad, and a weaker dollar flattered results from 1986 to 1988. Our discount rate is an assumption, because we did not have the long-term bond yield as of the date of this memo. The bottlers carry heavy capital needs and debt, and Coca-Cola may keep having to support them. We would reconsider the position if any of four things happened: the costs of maintaining the business turn out to be much higher than they appear, growth at constant currency turns out to be weak, interest rates are well above our assumption, or our meetings with management raise doubts about their integrity or discipline. Once we own the shares, a higher stock price alone will not be a reason to sell. We would sell only if the economics of the franchise deteriorate or if management starts putting large amounts of capital into businesses that earn far less than this one.
