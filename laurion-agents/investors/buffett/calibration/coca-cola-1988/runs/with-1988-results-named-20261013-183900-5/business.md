# Stage 2: Business analysis (framework §3)

I recognize the company as Coca-Cola. Everything below comes from the packet.

## 3.1 Return on equity, not EPS growth, is the primary test

The test in `brk-letter-1979` is a high return on equity achieved "without undue leverage, accounting gimmickry, etc." I take each part in turn.

**Reported ROE.** The figures are net income on average common equity, from the selected data in the 1988 report, restated (`ar-1988`).

| 1978 | 1979 | 1980 | 1981 | 1982 | 1983 | 1984 | 1985 | 1986 | 1987 | 1988 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 22.6% | 23.0% | 21.1% | 22.2% | 20.3% | 19.6% | 22.2% | 25.3% | 29.1% | 27.5% | 33.3% |

The return never fell below about 20% in 11 years. That alone would rank among the best large companies; `brk-letter-1987#1` notes that only six of the Fortune 1,000 averaged over 30% for a decade.

**Leverage check.** In 1978–1981, debt was only 3.8–9.3% of total capital, and ROE was still 21–23%. The franchise earned more than 20% before leverage was added. Leverage did not create the return.

The jump from about 22% to 33% since 1984 has three main causes:
- **Smaller equity base from buybacks.** Treasury stock grew from $604M in 1985 to $2,069M in 1988.
- **The $335M in-kind dividend of CPE shares.**
- **Debt.** Total debt to total capital was 38.8% at the end of 1988, or 24.3% net of excess cash. Management's ceiling is 35% (`ar-1988` MD&A).

**A cleaner measure: return on total capital.** I add back after-tax interest at the 34% effective rate.
- On total capital: (1,044.7 + 230.5 × 0.66) / 5,469 ≈ **21.9%**.
- On net capital (net debt 1,074 plus equity 3,345 = 4,419), with interest income netted against interest expense: about **24%**.

**Judging the core business on its own assets.** `brk-letter-1987#1` says managers should be judged on the assets they actually work with. The net-capital figure includes about $1.9B of investments in affiliates (CPE $598M, CCE $733M, and others), which earned only $48M of equity income in 1988. Removing those investments and that income:

> (1,065 − 48) / (4,419 − 1,912) ≈ **40% after tax** on the capital the operating business actually uses.

**Segment returns.** These are pretax operating income on identifiable operating assets in 1988 (`ar-1988` note 15):

| Segment | Pretax return | Operating margin |
|---|---|---|
| International soft drinks | 1,338.8 / 2,097.1 = **64%** | 29.7% |
| US soft drinks | 351.9 / 1,711.9 = **21%** | 15.4% |
| Foods | 89.3 / 694.1 = **13%** | 5.9% |

About 95% of segment operating income comes from soft drinks.

**Accounting check.** The packet shows several items that would distort a simple reading of earnings:

- **One-time items in earlier years.**
  - 1986 earnings include a $375M pretax gain on the CCE stock sale, worth +$0.68 per share. They also include $180M of provisions, worth −$0.33.
  - 1987 includes a $40M gain on the TCC sale and a $36M Foods provision, which roughly offset each other.
  - 1988 has no gains on subsidiary stock sales, a $25M gain on the BCI-related investment, and −$33M of other income net. 1988 is the cleanest year of the four.
- **Off-balance-sheet bottling.** Moving the capital-hungry bottling businesses into 49%-owned equity affiliates (CCE, TCC, Britain, and others) raises Coca-Cola's reported returns. It does not change what the system needs to operate. CCE earned $142.7M on $1,808M of net assets in 1988 (about 8%), and it carries $2.3B of noncurrent liabilities. The high returns depend partly on keeping that business at arm's length. This is legitimate under the equity method, but an owner should look through it.
- **Currency.** Seventy-six percent of operating income comes from outside the US. Key foreign currencies strengthened against the dollar by 27% in 1986, 16% in 1987 and 10% in 1988 (`ar-1988` MD&A). Part of the earnings growth since 1985 is translation, not business progress. Management does not give a currency-neutral figure.
- **Current-cost earnings.** Management says these "would be less than reported," but gives no number.

**EPS for reference only.** From 1981 to 1988, EPS from continuing operations grew from $1.17 to $2.85 (about 13.6% a year). Net income grew about 13.4% a year. The framework treats this as secondary.

**Conclusion on 3.1:** The business passes clearly. Returns on equity were above 20% with almost no debt for a decade, and the core operation earns roughly 40% after tax on the capital it uses. The recent rise to 33% is mostly financial engineering applied to a business that was already excellent, so it is not the evidence to rely on.

## 3.2 Durable competitive advantage (`brk-letter-1986#16`)

GEICO's moat in that letter is low cost. Coca-Cola's moat in the packet has four parts.

1. **The trademark and consumer preference.**
   - Coca-Cola has about 45% of world flavored carbonated soft-drink volume (excluding China and the USSR), "more than double" anyone else. Its US share is over 40% (`ar-1988`, `upi-1989-01-31`).
   - Its US share was 9 points ahead of the main competitor in 1986 (`ar-1986`), and it leads US fountain 2-to-1 (`ar-1987`).
   - Third-party surveys (Landor) put the brand "off the charts" (`ar-1988`).
   - The 1985 New Coke episode supports this. Consumers forced the original formula back, and the press treated it as a social story. That suggests the brand's value sits in customers' minds, beyond management's power to damage it easily.

2. **Pricing power and margins.**
   - Gross margin was 55.6% in 1988.
   - Gross margin on US concentrate was about 63% (`ar-1986`).
   - The new CCE contract allows "flexible pricing… at its discretion," and international bottler contracts "generally do not contain restrictions" (`ar-1986`).
   - Operating margin on the restated basis rose from 13.7% (1985) to 17.3% (1987) to 19.2% (1988).
   - Latin America raised operating income 17% on 1% volume (`upi-1989-01-31`).

   This fits the test in `brk-letter-1986#22` and `fortune-1977-inflation`: a strong franchise keeps cost and tax benefits, and can price against inflation without much added capital. That is the opposite of the Burlington case in `brk-letter-1985#9`.

3. **The distribution system.** The company describes itself as the low-cost producer "in most of the 155 countries." Its system has more than 1,000 bottlers, 700,000 vending machines in Japan, and over 100,000 added in the US in 1988 (`ar-1987`, `ar-1988`). A competitor would need decades and billions of dollars to copy it.

4. **Room to grow without much capital.**

   Annual servings per person:

   | Market | Servings per year |
   |---|---|
   | US | 274 (1987), target 315 |
   | International average | about 51 |
   | Indonesia | about 4 |
   | China | supply-constrained |

   Worldwide volume grew 7% in 1988, which follows earlier double-digit years. Capital spending on soft drinks was $240M against $1.69B of segment operating income. A business that can grow while paying out most of what it earns is the type `brk-letter-1987#1` describes.

**Where the moat is weaker:**
- **Foods** (orange juice) is a commodity-exposed business. Volume fell 22% in frozen and 6% in chilled in 1988, and it had restructuring in 1987. It is small, about 5% of segment operating income.
- **CPE** (entertainment, 49% owned) is outside any simple-business circle. It took a $114M write-down, and equity income from it was negative in early 1988. Its carrying value is $598M.

Neither threatens the core business, but neither earns franchise returns.

**Conclusion on 3.2:** This is a wide and, on the evidence of a century, durable moat. Its cost of maintenance is visible in the accounts: advertising was $842M in 1988 (up from $648M in 1986), plus cooperative payments to bottlers.

## 3.3 Owner earnings (`brk-letter-1986`)

The formula is (a) reported earnings, plus (b) depreciation, amortization and other non-cash charges, minus (c) the average capital spending needed to maintain competitive position and unit volume. The 1988 inputs are from the cash-flow statement in `ar-1988`:

| Item | $M |
|---|---|
| Net income | 1,044.7 |
| + Depreciation and amortization | 169.8 |
| + Deferred taxes | 43.9 |
| − Equity income not received in cash | (35.8) |
| − Preferred dividends | (6.4) |
| **Subtotal before capex** | **1,216** |
| − Capex: 1988 actual / 1986–88 average / depreciation proxy for maintenance | (387) / (346) / (170) |
| **Owner earnings** | **~830 / ~870 / ~1,046** |
| Per share (354.8M shares at year-end) | **$2.34 / $2.45 / $2.95** |

The range depends on what share of capital spending is maintenance and what share is growth. The packet does not say. Most of the soft-drink capex appears to be growth spending: coolers, vending machines, international bottling lines, the Shanghai concentrate plant, and the Dunkirk and Signes plants. That points toward the upper part of the range, with owner earnings roughly equal to reported net income. That is unusual, and it supports the moat assessment.

One addition belongs in (c). Coca-Cola keeps putting money into bottlers to hold the system together:
- Purchases of investments were $339M in 1987 and $129M in 1988.
- It has guarantees and a finance subsidiary that lends to bottlers (for example, $167M owed by Johnston).

Some of this is a real, recurring cost of defending the franchise. If I charge an average of about $150–200M a year, the realistic range is about **$2.40–2.65 per share**. My working figure is about **$2.50**, and I'll test that range in the valuation stage.

As a cross-check, net cash from operations in 1988 was $1,167M, less capex of $387M, giving about $780M of free cash flow before investment activity.

## Facts the framework wants that the packet lacks

1. **The split between maintenance and growth capex**, by segment and especially for international soft drinks. This is the largest open variable in owner earnings.
2. **How much of 1986–88 operating income growth came from volume, price, mix and currency.** Management gives the currency moves but no constant-currency figure.
3. **The system's economics at the bottler level.** I need bottler returns on capital outside CCE, and how much capital Coca-Cola must keep injecting through equity stakes, finance loans and guarantees to keep bottlers healthy. CCE's roughly 8% return suggests the capital-hungry half of the system sits off Coca-Cola's balance sheet.
4. **Competitor data.** The packet has no figures on the main US competitor's margins, share trend or advertising spending, so I cannot measure the gap directly.
5. **Country-level profit.** The packet names Japan as the largest profit contributor but gives no figures. Without them I cannot judge exposure to hyperinflationary markets such as Brazil and Argentina.
6. **The size of the current-cost (inflation-adjusted) earnings shortfall** that management mentions.
7. **1988 10-Q detail.** The packet has only the UPI wire stories.
8. **Pending accounting effects.** FASB 96 could cut equity by about $175M. Contingent guarantees are $133M, plus $101M of CPE receivables (`ar-1988` notes). These are small, but they should be noted.

## Stage 2 verdict

The core soft-drink concentrate business is an outstanding one. It has earned more than 20% on equity for a decade, starting from a time when it had almost no debt. Its core return on the capital it uses is about 40% after tax. It has a dominant, century-old trademark with clear pricing power, low capital needs, and long room for growth outside the US. Owner earnings are close to reported earnings, roughly $2.40–2.95 per share, with about $2.50 a reasonable working figure.

The cautions are:
- the recent ROE jump comes mostly from leverage and buybacks;
- the high returns depend partly on keeping capital-hungry bottling at arm's length;
- currency translation flatters recent growth;
- Foods and CPE are low-quality appendages.

None of these weakens the business itself. They bear on how carefully the price must be judged, which is for the next stages.
