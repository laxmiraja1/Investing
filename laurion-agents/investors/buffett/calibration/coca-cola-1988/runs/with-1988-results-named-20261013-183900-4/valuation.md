# Stage 4: Valuation

## 5. Valuation: the framework's stated rule (`bpl-letter-1965`)

The only valuation rule in the framework is the net-working-capital bargain test. I apply it first, using the 1988 balance sheet (ar-1988, $M):

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Current assets | 3,245.4 |
| − Current liabilities | 2,868.9 |
| **Net working capital** | **376.5**, or about **$1.06/share** on 354.8M shares |
| − Long-term debt, other liabilities, deferred taxes | 1,236.4 |
| Current assets minus *all* liabilities | **about −$860M** (negative) |
| Book value per common share ($3,045.3M ÷ 354.8M) | $8.58 |

The price is $46.56, about 44 times net working capital per share and 5.4 times book value. **On the 1965 test, Coca-Cola fails completely and has no asset margin of safety.**

That test is no longer the operative rule on the as-of date. Later passages in the framework replace it for a business of this kind:
- "the same energies and talent are much better employed in a good business purchased at a fair price than in a poor business purchased at a bargain price" (`brk-letter-1979`)
- "Our goal is to find an outstanding business at a sensible price, not a mediocre business at a bargain price … In the interim, I searched for 'bargains' – and had the misfortune to find some" (`brk-letter-1987#18`)
- buy at "prices that will make sense, even should the future of the acquired enterprise develop much along the lines of its past … We may very well pay a fairly fancy price for a Category 1 business if we are reasonably confident of what we are getting" (`brk-letter-1981#3`)

So the method actually in force is this: take **owner earnings** (`brk-letter-1986`), assume the future resembles the **past record** and not management's projections, and ask whether today's price makes sense on that basis.

---

## Step 1: Inputs from earlier stages

**Owner earnings, 1988.** The Stage 2 range was $2.25 to $2.97 per share. I use **$2.50**, which treats much of the capex above depreciation as maintenance. Reported EPS was $2.85.

**The past record**, used as the projection basis:

| Measure | Period | Growth per year |
|---|---|---|
| EPS | 1981–88 ($1.17 → $2.85) | about **13.5%** |
| EPS | 1983–88 ($1.35 → $2.85) | about **16%** |
| Net income, before the help from buybacks | 1983–88 ($559M → $1,045M) | about **13.3%** |

**Internal check on the growth rate.** The business earns about 24% on unlevered capital and retains about 58% of earnings (payout 42%). That supports roughly **14%** growth before buybacks, provided incremental returns hold. Per `brk-letter-1987#1`, a business that needs little capital can grow while releasing most of its earnings.

**Haircuts to growth:**
- Part of the 1986–88 growth came from a weak dollar (+27%, +16%, +10% in key currencies).
- Part came from added leverage.
- The Puerto Rico tax exemption ends in 1995.

I therefore use **10–15%** as the range and **12%** as the central case. Management's own guidance of "mid-teens" (upi-1989-01-31) is a projection, which the framework says is "of little interest," so I do not rely on it.

**Discount rate.** The packet does not give the long Treasury yield for February 1989. **I assume about 9%** and flag that as an assumption. As a stricter test I also use management's stated 12% after-tax cost of capital (ar-1986, ar-1988).

## Step 2: What the owner-earnings stream is worth

Model: $2.50 of owner earnings, grown at the stated rate for 10 years, then at 5% a year afterwards, discounted to today.

| Growth, years 1–10 | at 9% | at 12% |
|---|---|---|
| 10% | ~$98 | ~$54 |
| **12% (central)** | **~$115** | ~$62 |
| 15% | ~$146 | ~$78 |
| 0% (no growth at all) | ~$28 | ~$21 |

How the central case is built:
- Present value of years 1–10 is about $29.1.
- Owner earnings reach $7.77 in year 10.
- The terminal value at year 10 is $8.15 ÷ (0.09 − 0.05) ≈ $204, worth about $86 today.
- Total is about $115.

The terminal value is about three-quarters of the 9% figures. That is fragile, so I check the result a second way that does not depend on it.

## Step 3: Cross-check if the past simply repeats (`brk-letter-1981#3`)

Suppose earnings per share grow 12% a year for 10 years, from $2.85 to about $8.85 in 1998. The 1980 multiple of 10.5× is used as the harsh case.

| P/E in 1998 | Price | Price growth per year | Plus dividends (about 3% yield now, payout ~40%) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 15× | ~$133 | ~11% | **~14%/yr** |
| 10.5× (the 1980 multiple) | ~$93 | ~7% | **~10–11%/yr** |

At the 1981–88 growth rate of 13.5%, the 15× case reaches about $152, roughly 15% a year in total.

Even if the multiple contracts back to its 1980 level, an owner earns about 10–11% a year. That beats the assumed 9% bond with no further multiple expansion, and the yield on cost grows over time.

## Margin of safety

| Hurdle | Result |
|---|---|
| **The framework's stated valuation rule** (price below net working capital, `bpl-letter-1965`) | **Not met.** Price is about 44× net working capital per share. |
| **The rule in force in 1989** (outstanding business at a sensible price, valued on owner earnings) | **Met.** At the 9% central value of ~$115, the margin is (115 − 46.56) ÷ 115 ≈ **60%**. At 12% with 12% growth (~$62), it is ~25%. In the harshest case (10% growth at 12%, ~$50–54), it is ~5–15%. |
| **Floor with no growth** | ~$21–28, below the price. **The margin depends on the franchise continuing to grow, not on its assets.** |

The framework gives no numerical margin for the post-1979 rule. Its version of safety is "an extremely high probability that our facts and reasoning are correct with a very low probability that anything could drastically change the underlying value" (`bpl-letter-1965`, rule 7).

Stage 2 supports the first half of that. A moat that is widening, a 13–16% record, and a 1988 multiple of 16.3× reported earnings put the price below value in every scenario where growth continues even modestly.

The second half rests on one risk: growth stopping for good. Stage 2 found little evidence for that. International consumption per person is 16% of the US level, global share is rising, and China and Indonesia are short of supply.

This also confirms the provisional Stage 3 finding. The 1984–88 buybacks at an average of about $31.5 were **well below intrinsic value** under any reasonable growth assumption.

## 6. Catalyst and timing

- **No catalyst is needed** (`brk-letter-1987`). "The market may ignore business success for a while, but eventually will confirm it." This is a "general," held for business results, not for a market event.
- **Work-out** (`bpl-letter-1957`): **does not apply.** No sale, merger, or liquidation has been announced. The remaining buyback of 14.4M shares (about $670M at today's price) would add modestly to per-share value, but it is not a work-out.
- **Control** (`bpl-letter-1963`): **does not apply.** A $16.5 billion company cannot be controlled, and the framework rules out unfriendly takeovers (`brk-letter-1986#10`). This would be a "non-voting partnership" stake of the kind `brk-letter-1981#3` describes.
- **Fearful or greedy** (`brk-letter-1986`): there is no evidence of greed in this stock.
  - In August 1987 the stock traded near $50–53, about 20–22× 1987 EPS of $2.43.
  - Today it is $46.56, 16.3× a higher 1988 EPS of $2.85.
  - The multiple has come down since before the crash while the business improved.
  - The stock is near its 52-week high ($49.12) and above the mid-1988 level of about $38. That is a timing observation only, and the framework says not to act on it.
- **No market forecasting** (`bpl-letter-1966-mid`): valuation decides, not a view on where the market goes next. Per `brk-letter-1987`, "Mr. Market is there to serve you, not to guide you," and he is offering a rare business at a sensible price now.

## What the valuation lacks

1. **The long government bond yield on the as-of date.** I assumed 9%. A higher rate narrows the margin; see the 12% column.
2. **Currency-neutral earnings growth**, to tell how much of the 13–16% record is lasting.
3. **The split between maintenance and growth capex**, which is the main source of uncertainty in the $2.25–2.97 owner-earnings range.
4. **Market values of the CPE and CCE stakes.** The packet gives only "$291M above carrying value" for the publicly traded investees as a group. These are a small extra on top of the soft-drink value.
5. **Berkshire's capital on the as-of date**, needed for sizing in the next stage.

**Stage 4 conclusion.** Coca-Cola is not a bargain on any asset-based measure, and the framework's literal valuation rule rejects it. Under the "outstanding business at a sensible price" standard in force in 1989:
- Owner earnings of about $2.50, growing roughly as they have, are worth about **$100–145** at a 9% discount rate.
- Even at management's own 12% hurdle they are worth about **$54–78**.
- The price is **$46.56**, well below the central estimate.
- **No catalyst is needed.**
