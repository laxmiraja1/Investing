# Stage 2: Business analysis

I take the framework's three business-analysis tests in order: return on equity, the moat, then owner earnings. I recognize the company as Coca-Cola, but every figure below comes from the packet.

---

## 3.1 Return on equity, not EPS growth (`brk-letter-1979`)

The test asks for "a high earnings rate on equity capital employed (without undue leverage, accounting gimmickry, etc.)". The reported figures pass easily. The two qualifiers in parentheses are where the work is.

**Reported return on average equity** (taken from each year's selected data):

| Period | ROE | Debt / total capital |
|---|---|---|
| 1976–1983 | 19.4%–21.2% every year | 3.5% rising to 15.2% |
| 1984 | 21.8% | 32.4% |
| 1985 | 23.5% | 30.2% |
| 1986 | 28.8% | 33.1% |
| 1987 | 27.2% (27.5% restated) | 45.6% (48.4% restated) |
| 1988 | 33.3% (common) | 38.8%; 24.3% net of excess cash |

**Adjusting for one-time items:**
- **1986.** Reported income includes a $262M after-tax gain from the CCE share sale and $128M of after-tax restructuring and South Africa charges. Removing both leaves about $800M, or **about 24.6%** on average equity of $3,247M.
- **1987.** The $40M TCC gain and the $36M Foods provision roughly cancel, so the reported 27.2% stands.
- **1988.** Equity income includes Coca-Cola's share of a $104M pretax gain at CCE and a $25M gain on the BCI stake. It also absorbs the Columbia Pictures write-downs, which cut equity income from $113M to $48M. These are small and pull in both directions, so 33% is roughly clean.

**Adjusting for leverage.** Part of the rise from about 20% to 33% is financial, not operating:
- Treasury stock grew from $604M in 1985 to $2,069M in 1988.
- Book value per share fell from $9.13 (1986) to about $8.58 (1988, common equity of $3,045M over 354.8M shares).
- Debt was used to shrink the equity base.

I check this two ways:
- **Unlevered return.** Add the roughly $1.07B of net debt back to equity, giving net capital of about $4.4B. Divide that into net income plus after-tax net interest, about $1.065B. The result is **about 24%**. Roughly 9 points of the 33% therefore come from leverage and the buybacks.
- **Soft-drink segment pretax operating income ÷ identifiable operating assets.** This measure carries no leverage:
  - 1986: about 36%, excluding $180M of provisions ($1,181M ÷ $3,287M)
  - 1987: 34% ($1,432.5M ÷ $4,174M)
  - 1988: **44%** ($1,690.7M ÷ $3,809M)

For comparison, `brk-letter-1987#1` notes that only six of Fortune's 1,000 largest companies averaged above 30% on equity over the prior decade. Coca-Cola earned about 20% on almost no debt in 1976–83. It now earns about 24% unlevered and over 40% pretax on the soft-drink assets themselves. **The test is passed on the clean measure, not only the reported one.**

**Accounting distortions.** These are not "gimmickry" in the fraudulent sense, but they need watching:
- **Gains from bottler and IPO transactions recur:** $67M in 1985, $375M in 1986, $40M in 1987, and the BCI and CCE share in 1988.
- **Annual restatements** (CCE, CPE/TCC, the finance subsidiary) make the revenue and margin series discontinuous.
- **Bottling debt sits off the balance sheet.** Deconsolidating CCE moved the $2.25B of bottler-acquisition debt into a 49%-owned affiliate, which reported $2.86B of liabilities at end-1988. Coca-Cola's balance sheet looks lighter than the system it depends on.
- **Disclosure is honest about these items.** Management separates out "unusual items" in its own letter, and the auditors' notes disclose them. That supports the candor standard (`brk-letter-1979`).

**EPS versus ROE.** Earnings per share grew about 16% a year from 1981. In 1988, net income rose 14% and EPS 17.3%; the gap is the buybacks. The framework judges ROE, not the EPS figure. Management itself puts ROE and a 12% cost of capital at the front of its reporting, which is a good sign.

## 3.2 Durable competitive advantage, the moat (`brk-letter-1986#16`)

The framework's GEICO example asks two things: is there a structural advantage competitors cannot copy, and is it widening?

**Evidence of the moat:**
- **Share.**
  - World: about 45% of flavored carbonated soft drinks, "more than double the number anyone else sells" (ar-1988).
  - US: over 40%.
  - US fountain: 59% in 1986, outselling the nearest rival more than 2-to-1. Coca-Cola captured about three-quarters of the segment's growth over four years (ar-1987).
- **The moat is widening.** World share rose from under 38% (1980) to nearly 45% (1988). In the US, Coca-Cola's 7% volume gain in 1986 supplied over 60% of the market's growth.
- **Trademark strength.**
  - Independent Landor surveys put Coca-Cola "off the charts", with a gap to brand #2 larger than the gap from #2 to #10.
  - The 1985 formula change and the return of Coca-Cola classic shows consumer attachment that no other packaged good would produce.
- **Cost and distribution.**
  - The company describes itself as the "low-cost producer in most of the 155 countries".
  - It has 1,000+ bottlers and 559 million servings a day.
  - Concentrate is cheap to ship, and its US gross margin was about 63% (ar-1986).
- **Pricing power.**
  - New CCE contracts allow discretionary pricing.
  - Management states it "is able to adjust prices to compensate for increasing costs".
  - In Latin America in 1988, volume grew 1% but operating income grew 17%.
  - Limits do exist: German volume fell 4% after the 1987 price increases.
- **Room to grow.**
  - International per-capita consumption was 16% of the US rate in 1986, and about 51 servings a year internationally in 1988.
  - US consumption is still rising: 203 servings in 1980, 274 in 1987, with a target of 315 by 1990.
  - Markets such as China and Indonesia are supply-constrained.
- **Little change in the product.** Per `brk-letter-1987#3`, "severe change and exceptional returns usually don't mix", and this product has barely changed.
- **The opposite of Burlington** (`brk-letter-1985#9`). Soft-drink capex is modest relative to earnings, and the earnings rise rather than being competed away.

**Where the moat does not extend:**
- **Foods.** Orange juice behaves like a commodity, driven by the price of orange solids. In 1988 frozen OJ volume fell 22%. Operating income was $89M, against $120M in 1986, and the segment is about 5% of operating income.
- **Entertainment.** It is now a 49%-owned affiliate. It took a $114M write-down, and Coca-Cola recorded about $44M of equity losses in 1988.
- These two businesses were diversification outside the moat, a point for Stage 3. They are now small or separated off.

**The tax test** (`brk-letter-1986#22`) cannot be run cleanly. The effective tax rate barely moved (33.5% → 35.1% → 34.0%) because most taxes are foreign. Net margin did rise on the restated basis, from 11.5% in 1986 (excluding items) to 12.5% in 1988.

**Risks to how durable the moat is:**
- **Currency.** 76–79% of operating income comes from outside the US. Key currencies strengthened 27%, 16% and 10% against the dollar in 1986–88. Roughly half of the 21–25% international operating-income growth looks like currency, not business.
- **High-inflation markets.** Volume declined in Brazil and Argentina.
- **Puerto Rico tax exemption.** It expires in 1995.

**Conclusion.** The soft-drink business has a wide moat that is getting wider.

## 3.3 Owner earnings (`brk-letter-1986`)

The framework defines owner earnings as reported earnings, plus D&A and other non-cash charges, minus the average capex needed to maintain competitive position and volume.

**1988** (all $M; 364.6M average shares; $6.4M of preferred dividends deducted):

| | Low | Middle | High |
|---|---|---|---|
| Net income | 1,044.7 | 1,044.7 | 1,044.7 |
| + Depreciation and amortization | 169.8 | 169.8 | 169.8 |
| + Deferred taxes | – | – | 43.9 |
| − Capex | 386.8 (all of 1988's) | 345.7 (1986–88 average) | ~170 (maintenance ≈ depreciation) |
| − Preferred dividends | 6.4 | 6.4 | 6.4 |
| **Owner earnings** | **~821** | **~862** | **~1,082** |
| **Per share** | **~$2.25** | **~$2.37** | **~$2.97** |

- **Cross-check.** Operating cash flow of $1,166.8M less capex of $386.8M gives about $780M. That figure includes working-capital swings and excludes undistributed equity income.
- **Working estimate: about $2.40–2.60 a share.** Reported EPS of $2.85 is a little generous, because capex runs about 2.3 times depreciation. Much of that excess appears to be growth spending: Asian infrastructure, coolers, and 100,000+ new US vending machines. If so, true owner earnings sit toward the upper half of the range.

**What would lower owner earnings:**
- **Spending to keep bottlers strong.** Coca-Cola puts money into bottlers: $128.5M of investment purchases in 1988, $339M in 1987, and $68M of net marketing payments to CCE. Some of that is plausibly the cost of keeping the system competitive.
- **Inflation.** Management says current-cost income "would be less than reported" but gives no number.

## Facts the framework would want that the packet lacks

1. **The split between maintenance and growth capex**, for the company and for the bottling system it partly funds. This is the largest open variable in owner earnings.
2. **Currency-neutral operating income growth by region.** Without it, I cannot separate franchise growth from the weak dollar.
3. **Profit per unit case or gallon, and real price trends over time.** These would quantify pricing power directly instead of inferring it.
4. **The primary competitor's share, margins and returns**, especially outside the US. This would test the moat in the GEICO way: our costs versus theirs.
5. **The current-cost (inflation-adjusted) earnings figure** referred to in the MD&A.
6. **Look-through economics of the 49%-owned bottlers.** For example, CCE earned about 8% on equity in 1988 including a one-time gain, and carries heavy debt. This matters for judging the whole system's leverage.
7. **The 1988 10-Qs and the 10-K country segment detail.** Japan is the top profit contributor, but its figures are not broken out.

**Stage 2 conclusion.** Coca-Cola's core soft-drink business passes all three tests:
- **Returns:** about 24% unlevered and over 40% pretax on segment assets, with no turnaround needed.
- **Moat:** wide and widening.
- **Owner earnings:** roughly $2.40–2.60 a share against reported EPS of $2.85.

Leverage, the off-balance-sheet bottling system and currency all need to be carried into the valuation stage. Whether management uses the retained cash well (buybacks and debt, the past diversification into Foods and Entertainment) is a Stage 3 question.
