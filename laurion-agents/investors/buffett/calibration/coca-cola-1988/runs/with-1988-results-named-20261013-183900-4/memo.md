**The Coca-Cola Company: Recommendation to Buy**

We recommend buying shares of The Coca-Cola Company at about $46.56 and building the stake into a major commitment of roughly a quarter of the equity portfolio. We intend to hold it indefinitely. This memo sets out what the business is, why it meets our standards, what we think it is worth, and what would make us reconsider.

Coca-Cola is, at its core, a simple business. It makes soft-drink concentrate and syrup and sells them to more than a thousand bottlers in over 160 countries. The bottlers do the capital-heavy work of filling, distributing and stocking product. The company's own job is to protect and promote the most widely recognized trademark in the world. Soft drinks produced more than 90% of operating income in 1988. The other businesses no longer matter much to the result:
- The entertainment business has been moved into a 49%-owned affiliate.
- The juice business is small and has struggled.

The business passes the tests we apply. It is not a turnaround. Return on equity was close to 20% every year from 1976 to 1983, when the company carried almost no debt, and it reached 33% in 1988. Some of that recent rise comes from borrowing to repurchase shares. Even after stripping out leverage, the company earns about 24% on its capital, and the soft-drink operations earn about 44% pretax on the assets they use. Few large American companies come close to that.

The advantage behind these returns is widening, not eroding:
- Coca-Cola's share of world soft-drink volume rose from under 38% in 1980 to nearly 45% in 1988, more than twice that of any competitor.
- In the United States it sells over 40% of all soft drinks and close to 60% of fountain volume.
- Outside the U.S., consumption per person is a small fraction of the American level, and in markets such as China and Indonesia demand currently exceeds supply.
- The product changes little, and the business needs modest capital to grow, which leaves most of its earnings free for the owners.

Management has used that free cash well, though not flawlessly. Roberto Goizueta and Donald Keough have run the company since 1981. Over that period:
- They concentrated the company on its core business and reorganized a fragmented U.S. bottling system.
- They cut the dividend payout from about 65% to about 42% so that more earnings could be reinvested at high returns.
- They repurchased about 11% of the shares at an average of roughly $31.50.

The best summary of their record is that the company's market value rose by about $11.7 billion while it retained only about $2.5 billion of earnings. Even if we remove the market's higher valuation multiple, each retained dollar still produced well over one dollar of value.

There are blemishes, and we want partners to see them:
- The move into film and television earned below the company's own cost of capital.
- Stock was issued for one of those acquisitions at a price that looks cheap today.
- Debt has risen to fund buybacks.
- A large option grant in 1988 deserves scrutiny.

Parts of the annual report read more like promotion than reporting. On balance, though, the reports disclose problems plainly, and the direction of the company's capital spending is right.

This is not a bargain by asset measures. Working capital comes to about a dollar a share and book value to under nine dollars. What we are buying is the earning power of the franchise. We estimate owner earnings, meaning profit plus depreciation less the capital spending needed to sustain the business, at about $2.50 per share for 1988, against reported earnings of $2.85.

Our valuation projects the past forward rather than relying on management's forecasts. If owner earnings grow about 12% a year for a decade and more slowly thereafter, and we discount them at a rate near today's long-bond yield, the shares are worth around $115. That figure rests on our assumption of a 9% long-bond yield, because the information we have does not include it. At a more demanding 12% discount rate, value is still roughly $55 to $80. A simpler check gives a similar answer. Suppose earnings compound at 12% for ten years and the market then pays only the low multiple it paid in 1980. A buyer today would still earn about 10% to 11% a year including dividends, and considerably more at a normal multiple.

The price of $46.56 is 16 times last year's earnings. That multiple is lower than the stock carried before the 1987 crash, even though earnings have risen since. We see no need for any event to unlock value. If the business keeps performing, the share price will eventually follow.

Our margin of safety lies in the quality and durability of the business, not in its balance sheet. If growth stopped entirely, the shares would be worth only about $21 to $28. Our protection therefore depends on being right that the franchise keeps growing, and the evidence for that is unusually strong.

We would change our view in several circumstances:
- Currency-adjusted results show that the recent growth came mainly from a weak dollar. About three-quarters of operating profit is earned abroad, and foreign currencies strengthened sharply from 1986 to 1988.
- Most of the capital spending above depreciation turns out to be needed just to defend existing volume.
- A competitor begins taking share in fountain or overseas markets.
- Management drifts back into acquisitions outside soft drinks, issues stock on poor terms, or repurchases shares at prices above their value.

Before completing the position, we would want to meet management and review the proxy statement for compensation and insider ownership, neither of which our materials cover.

Absent those developments, we expect to own this business for a very long time.
