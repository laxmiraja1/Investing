# Memo: Purchase of The Coca-Cola Company, February 1989

We recommend buying shares of The Coca-Cola Company at about $46.56. We intend to build the position over time to about a quarter of the portfolio and to hold it with no planned sale date. The reasoning follows the order of our usual process: what the business is, whether it meets our standards, what it is worth, and what would change our minds.

The business is simpler than its size suggests. Coca-Cola makes soft-drink syrup and concentrate and sells it to more than a thousand largely independent bottlers in over 155 countries. The bottlers carry the heavy capital: plants, trucks, coolers and vending machines. Coca-Cola owns the trademarks and the marketing. In 1988 soft drinks produced more than 90% of operating income. The rest came from an orange-juice business that matters little, and from minority stakes in bottlers and in Columbia Pictures Entertainment. About 559 million servings of the company's drinks are sold each day, up from 359 million in 1980. Its share of world soft-drink sales rose over the same period from under 38% to nearly 45%, more than double any competitor.

Coca-Cola passes our filters. It is not a turnaround: earnings per share rose every year shown, from $0.84 in 1977 to $2.85 in 1988. It needs no technology we cannot follow, it earns about $1 billion after tax, and the people who built the current record are still running it. Our one concern at the outset was debt. Total debt is about 39% of capital, which is more than we usually like. On closer inspection the borrowing pays for share repurchases, not for running the business. Interest is covered about seven times, and cash offsets most of the debt.

The economics are exceptional. Reported return on equity was 33% in 1988. We discount that figure, because buybacks and borrowing have shrunk the equity base. Even counted with no debt at all, the company earned about 25% on all the capital it employs. Excluding the low-return stakes in bottlers and film, the concentrate business itself earned something closer to 45–50%. These are not new results. Between 1978 and 1981, when the company had almost no debt, return on equity ran 21–23%. Cash generation matches the earnings. Operating cash flow was $1.17 billion in 1988 against net income of $1.04 billion, and spending on plant beyond depreciation goes to expansion overseas. We estimate owner earnings, the cash the owners could take out while keeping the business's position intact, at $2.40–2.90 a share.

The competitive advantage is plain and getting stronger. The trademark leads all others by a wide margin. The distribution system cannot be copied at any reasonable cost. Coca-Cola is the low-cost producer in most of its markets. It has also shown it can price: when the corporate tax rate was cut, the company kept the benefit and its margins rose. The largest source of future growth lies abroad. Outside the United States, people drink about 51 of the company's drinks per person each year, against about 274 at home, and the bottlers pay for most of the equipment needed to close that gap.

Management has earned our confidence with one exception. Since 1981 shareholders have gained about $5 of market value for each dollar the company kept instead of paying out. Management repurchased about 70 million shares at an average near $31.50, cut the dividend payout so more could be reinvested, and states a 12% after-tax hurdle for new investment. The exception is the move into motion pictures in the 1980s, which sat outside what the company does well. Most of it has since been distributed to shareholders, but about $600 million is still tied up there, earning little. Their annual reports carry a good deal of promotion, while the financial sections are frank about one-time gains, charges and weak spots.

On value, the price is clearly below what the business is worth. With no growth at all, owner earnings plus the bottler and film stakes would be worth about $32 a share. The current price implies that owner earnings grow only about 3% a year forever. The record since 1980 is about 12%. We assumed a slower 10% for a decade, then 5% thereafter, discounted at 10%, and arrived at about $89 a share. Under harsher assumptions, either 8% growth or management's own 12% cost of capital, the value is still $64–69, about 27–32% above today's price. We do not need the market to recognise this soon. If the business keeps compounding, the price will follow over time.

We have deliberately stopped short of a larger position. The packet gives us no long-term interest rate, no split between maintenance and growth spending, and no direct contact with management. Three quarters of profits are earned abroad, so a stronger dollar would reduce reported earnings for some years. The bottlers carry substantial debt outside Coca-Cola's balance sheet.

Several things would change our view: sustained loss of share to the main competitor, evidence that recent growth came mostly from a weak dollar and from borrowing, a return to large acquisitions outside soft drinks, or debt rising beyond the company's own limit. Movements in the share price alone would not. We expect to own this business for a long time.
