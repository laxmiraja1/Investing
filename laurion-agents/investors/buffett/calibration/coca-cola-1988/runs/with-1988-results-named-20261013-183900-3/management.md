# Stage 3: Management and capital allocation (framework §4)

I apply the three rules of §4 in order: (1) honest, able management already in place, with candid reporting; (2) buybacks only well below intrinsic value; (3) each $1 retained must create at least $1 of market value. Acquisitions and compensation are covered where they bear on those rules. Figures are in $ millions unless shown per share, and per-share figures are post-1986 split.

---

## 4.1 Honest, able management in place; candid reporting

### In place and able

- **The team.** Goizueta (CEO) and Keough (COO) have run the company since 1981. M. Douglas Ivester is CFO. The 1981 "Strategy for the 1980s" is still the stated plan, so the people who built the record are the ones running it now.
- **The operating results under them** (from Stage 2):
  - ROE rose from about 20% to 33%.
  - World share rose from under 38% to about 45%.
  - Servings per day rose 56%.
  - US soft-drink operating income grew about 9–10% a year in 1987–88 without help from currency.
- **They measure themselves by return on equity.** This lines up with the framework's primary test (`brk-letter-1979`). Examples:
  - The 1988 letter calls the 33% ROE "perhaps the most significant" number.
  - The 1987 report states an after-tax cost-of-capital hurdle of about 12% for soft-drink-type investments.
  - The 1986 report says they invest only where long-term cash returns exceed the weighted average cost of capital (`ar-1986`, `ar-1987`, `ar-1988`).

  Most managements of this period lead with EPS growth, so this is uncommon.

### Candor

The framework says "We don't expect a public relations document" (`brk-letter-1979`) and asks for "the business facts that we would want to know if our positions were reversed" (`brk-letter-1983#2`). The reports have two layers:

**The narrative sections are promotional.** Examples:
- "The potential appears limitless" (`ar-1986`).
- Coke's $50B of system sales "added an estimated $250 billion to the world economy" through a "multiplier factor of five" (`ar-1987`).
- Pages of photographs and anecdotes.

These are public-relations material and carry no weight.

**The financial review is candid where it matters:**
- One-time items are separated every year: the $375M CCE gain, the $180M provisions, the $40M TCC gain and the $36M Foods charge. The 1987 headline growth of 17.4% is computed fairly; I checked that it excludes items on both sides.
- Restatements for deconsolidation are explained, and each one notes that net income is unaffected.
- Weak spots are admitted:
  - Columbia film results were "below expectations" (1986).
  - "Our foods business is obviously in transition" (1987).
  - Latin American volume and operating income declined (1988).
- Two disclosures are against management's own interest:
  - Current-cost earnings "would be less than reported."
  - Purchasing-power gains from debt "do not necessarily imply that incurring more debt would be beneficial."
- Contingent guarantees and the possible $175M FASB 96 hit to equity are disclosed.

**Verdict on 4.1: passes.** The managers are able, and the financial reporting is straightforward even though the front of each report is marketing.

## 4.2 Buybacks only well below intrinsic value

The rule (`brk-letter-1984#31`, quoted in the framework) is that major repurchases at prices well below per-share intrinsic value immediately increase that value. Buybacks done to support the stock price or to manage EPS do not qualify.

### Repurchase record (cost ÷ shares, from the statements of shareholders' equity)

| Year | Shares bought (M) | Cost | Avg price | Context |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1984 | 18.4 | 335.7 | $18.24 | |
| 1985 | 15.0 | 379.9 | $25.33 | |
| 1986 | 2.9 | 110.5 | $38.37 | |
| 1987 | 14.2 | 605.1 | $42.62 | 52-week range $29–53; heavy buying before the October crash |
| 1988 | 19.0 | 758.2 | $39.94 | |
| **Total** | **~69.5** | **~2,189** | **~$31.50** | Shares outstanding fell from ~396M (1984 avg) to 355M (end-1988) |

### Were these "well below intrinsic value"?

The intrinsic value estimate belongs to Stage 4, but the record can be tested roughly now.

- **1984–85, at about 7–9x earnings.** Owner earnings were close to reported earnings and the business earned 20%+ on equity. These purchases were clearly below what the business was worth.
- **1988, at about $40.** That is roughly 14–15x the Stage 2 owner-earnings range of $2.60–2.90, or an owner-earnings yield of about 7%. The company was compounding mid-teens per share, and the core earns about 45–50% on its unlevered capital. A price of 14–15x is very likely well below value for a business like that. Every buyback since 1984 is below today's price of $46.56, which is weak evidence because it depends on the market.
- **1987 is the weakest year.** Buying averaged about $42.60 into a market peak, and some purchases were made when the price was in the high $40s to low $50s. Even so, judged against the business's economics rather than the October price, it does not look like overpayment.

### How management frames the buybacks

- Repurchases were "initiated after management determined that... available cash and debt capacity would exceed the funds required" (`ar-1986`).
- They continue "as long as investing in our own stock remains one of the best uses of our excess cash" (`ar-1988`).

This is an opportunity-cost test, which is sensible. It is not an explicit "we buy only below intrinsic value" commitment, and the packet does not state management's own estimate of intrinsic value.

### The debt question from Stage 1

Buybacks were partly funded with borrowing:
- The net debt ceiling was raised to 35%.
- About $1.2B of short-term debt was added in 1987, "due principally to the funding of the share repurchase program."
- In 1988, $300M of money-market preferred stock was issued.

This is leverage applied to a business with very stable cash flow, with interest covered about 7x. It does not violate a hard rule. It does mean part of the 33% ROE is financial engineering on top of a great business, as noted in Stage 2.

### Share issuance

The framework treats value-diluting issuance as harmful in its own right (`brk-letter-1982#17`). About 7.1M shares (count as given in the 1986 annual report) went to buy Embassy in 1985, with the price of $267M partly consisting of retiring Embassy debt. Coke later recovered "virtually all of the investment" by selling Embassy Pictures and Home Entertainment and converting receivables to cash (`ar-1986`). Since 1985 there has been no issuance for acquisitions, and repurchases are about ten times the shares issued to employees.

**Verdict on 4.2: passes, with a qualification.** The buybacks were large and probably well below value. The qualifications are that they were debt-funded in 1987 and that the stated discipline is "best use of cash" rather than an explicit value test.

## 4.3 Each $1 retained must create at least $1 of market value (`brk-letter-1983`)

### Per-share test, 1981–1988 (this management's tenure)

| Item | Per share |
|---|---|
| Sum of EPS 1981–88 (1.30 + 1.32 + 1.37 + 1.59 + 1.84 + 2.42 + 2.43 + 2.85) | $15.12 |
| Less cash dividends (0.77 + 0.83 + 0.89 + 0.92 + 0.99 + 1.04 + 1.12 + 1.20) | −$7.76 |
| Less the 1987 CPE dividend in kind | −$0.90 |
| **Retained per share** | **$6.46** |
| Market price end-1980 → end-1988 ($11.13 → $44.63) | **+$33.50** |
| **Market value per $1 retained** | **≈ $5.20** |

### Removing the market re-rating

- The price/earnings ratio rose from about 9.8x (11.13 / 1.14 in 1980) to about 15.7x.
- Valuing 1988 EPS at the 1980 multiple gives about $27.90. That implies a gain of about $16.80 against $6.46 retained, or about **$2.60 per $1 retained**.

Part of the per-share gain comes from buying back shares cheaply. That is itself a capital allocation decision, so it counts toward management's record.

### Company-level cross-check

- Retained earnings 1981–88: net income of about $5.8B, less about $3.0B of cash dividends and $0.34B in kind, leaves about $2.5B.
- Market value rose from about $4.1B to $15.8B even after about $2.2B was spent buying stock back.

**Verdict on 4.3: passes by a wide margin.**

### Dividend policy follows the same logic

- The payout ratio was cut from 65% (1983) to 42% (1988).
- The target is 40%, with dividends growing more slowly than earnings.
- The stated reason is to "reinvest more substantially in opportunities offering returns in excess of the cost of capital" (`ar-1987`).

Given the return on retained capital measured above, retaining more is justified.

## Acquisitions and diversification: the blemish

The record includes one large move away from the franchise.

**Columbia Pictures (1982), Embassy and Tandem (1985), Merv Griffin (1986, about $200M).**
- Entertainment grew its operating income 30%+ a year through 1986.
- It was still a business outside the core. Management itself was "not satisfied" with film performance.
- The packet does not give Columbia's purchase price, so the full return on this diversification cannot be computed.

**Partial reversal in 1987.**
- Entertainment was merged into Tri-Star to form CPE.
- 51% of CPE went to shareholders as a dividend in kind worth $0.90 a share at carrying value.
- Coke collected $545M of CPE notes in cash.

**The capital still tied up there earns very little.**
- The CPE carrying value fell from $989M to $598M.
- CPE took a $114M write-down in early 1988.
- Coke's share of CPE was a $51M loss for the stub period.
- About $600M of capital, roughly 18% of common equity, sits in a low-return business.

On the framework's test of mistakes in "the future economics of the business" (`brk-letter-1981#3`), this looks like one. What matters is that management saw it and reversed most of it rather than keeping capital in entertainment.

**Bottler investments.**
- CCE was formed in 1986. Coke bought Lupton and BCI's bottlers for $2.25B, which CCE funded with its own debt. CCE then raised $1.12B in its IPO, and Coke booked a $375M gain.
- Miami, Rainwater and Memphis were bought and resold at roughly carrying value.
- Coke took 20–30% stakes in Consolidated and Johnston, plus about 30 other bottlers.

The approach keeps capital-heavy bottling off Coke's books while protecting the concentrate business. The problem is that CCE earns about 8% on its equity, below management's own 12% hurdle. These stakes are justified only by what they protect: concentrate volume and pricing. That argument is reasonable but cannot be tested from the packet.

**Foods.** Nutri-Foods ($30M) and H.P. Hood citrus foodservice ($45M) were small. The coffee business was sold. None of this is material.

## Compensation (what the packet shows)

- **Stock options:** 4.18M options were granted in 1988, against 1.11M in 1987 and 1.80M in 1986. Outstanding options are 8.66M, about 2.4% of shares. Options are priced at market and carry no cost-of-capital adjustment.
- **Restricted stock:** 511,500 shares in 1988.
- **Unit agreements:** Performance Units on 555,000 shares at a $20.63 base (1985), and Incentive Units on 300,000 shares (1988).
- **Assessment:** Annual dilution from employees is small next to the buybacks: about 0.9M shares issued against 19M repurchased in 1988. The jump in the 1988 option grant is worth asking about. The packet has no proxy data on individual pay or on how much stock management owns.

## What this framework would have relied on that the packet lacks

1. **Direct judgment of the people.** The framework puts heavy weight on character, judged firsthand (`brk-letter-1987#18`, `brk-letter-1984#7`). Meeting Goizueta and Keough, or knowing them through mutual associates or directors, would have been the main input. The packet contains none of this.
2. **Management's view of intrinsic value and its buyback rule.** I need to know whether they would stop buying above some price.
3. **Insider ownership and the proxy statement.** This means individual pay, option terms, and whether managers "eat their own cooking" (`brk-letter-1983#0`).
4. **The Columbia purchase price and the full economics of entertainment.** This is needed to score the diversification precisely.
5. **Management's intentions for the remaining 49% of CPE and the bottler stakes.** In particular, whether low-return capital will be released or will keep growing.
6. **Succession plans** below Goizueta and Keough.
7. **Board independence and how the board views capital allocation.**

## Stage 3 summary

| Rule | Finding | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Able, honest management; candid reporting | Proven team since 1981, focused on ROE with a stated 12% hurdle. The financial review is candid about one-offs and weak spots; the narrative sections are public-relations material. | Pass |

| $1 retained → ≥ $1 market value | About $5.20 per $1 retained, or about $2.60 with the re-rating removed. | Pass, strongly |
| Acquisitions | The entertainment diversification was a mistake, now largely reversed. About $600M remains in CPE earning little. Bottler stakes earn below the hurdle but protect the franchise. | Blemish, contained |
| Compensation | Options and restricted stock are modest next to buybacks. The 1988 grant jump is noted. Individual pay and ownership are unknown. | Neutral, data missing |

Management clears the §4 tests. The open question for the next stage is price: whether $46.56 leaves enough room below intrinsic value, given that these same managers judged about $40 to be a good use of cash.
