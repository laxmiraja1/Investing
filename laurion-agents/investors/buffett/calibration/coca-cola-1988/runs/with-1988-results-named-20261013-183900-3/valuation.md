# Stage 4: Valuation (framework §5) and Catalyst / timing (§6)

**Conclusion:** at $46.56, the price is about 27% below my most conservative estimate of intrinsic value (about $64) and about 48% below the central estimate (about $89). That clears the framework's requirement for a good business: a sensible price that still works if the future merely repeats a slower version of the past. The framework's one explicit valuation rule, buying below net working capital, fails badly, but the investor's own writing shows it no longer applies to a business of this kind. No catalyst is needed.

---

## 5.1 The framework's stated valuation rule: buy below net working capital

The only rule listed under §5 comes from `bpl-letter-1965`: buy when net working capital alone, with nothing assigned to plant, exceeds the price.

| Item (end-1988) | $M |
|---|---|
| Current assets | 3,245.4 |
| Less current liabilities | (2,868.9) |
| Net working capital | 376.5 |
| Less long-term debt | (761.1) |
| Less preferred stock | (300.0) |
| Net-net working capital | ≈ −685 |
| Per share (354.8M shares) | ≈ −$1.93 (NWC alone: $1.06) |
| **Price** | **$46.56** |

**Result: fails completely.** Coke is not a net-net, and it never would be at any realistic price.

**Why this does not decide the case.** By 1988 the investor's own writing had explicitly moved away from this test for businesses like this one:

- "Our goal is to find an outstanding business at a sensible price, not a mediocre business at a bargain price… I searched for 'bargains' – and had the misfortune to find some" (`brk-letter-1987#18`).
- "We may very well pay a fairly fancy price for a Category 1 business if we are reasonably confident of what we are getting" (`brk-letter-1981#3`).
- The 1981 letter adds that the purchase should make sense "even should the future of the acquired enterprise develop much along the lines of its past."

The net working capital rule belongs to the partnership-era "generals" category. For a franchise whose value lies in a trademark and a distribution system rather than in its balance sheet, the operative method in this framework at this date is capitalized **owner earnings** (§3.3, `brk-letter-1986`). The test applied to that value is whether the price makes sense if the future resembles the past, with no reliance on projections. That is the method I use below.

## 5.2 Owner-earnings valuation

### Inputs (from Stage 2 and the packet)

| Input | Value | Source / basis |
|---|---|---|
| 1988 owner earnings | $0.88–1.05B; midpoint **$0.95B ≈ $2.70/share** (354.8M shares) | Stage 2 §3.3. Already after interest, so debt is accounted for |
| Equity affiliates, valued separately | $1,912M carrying ≈ **$5.40/share** | `ar-1988` note 3. Adds nothing for the $291M by which public investees' market value exceeds book; CPE may be worth less than book |
| Past growth rate | EPS 1980→1988: $1.14 → $2.85, **≈12%/yr**. Servings ≈5.7%/yr. US operating income ≈9–10%/yr in 1987–88 without currency help | `ar-1988` selected data; Stage 2 |
| Discount rate | **Not in the packet.** I test 10%, and 12% as management's own after-tax cost of capital | `ar-1987`, `ar-1988` |
| Terminal growth after year 10 | 4–5% | Assumption: roughly inflation plus a little real volume |

**On the 10% discount rate.** The framework compares business returns with long government bond yields, and the packet gives no Treasury yield. My assumption is that long Treasuries yield somewhat below 10%, so 10% is a conservative rate. I also show 12%.

**On the growth assumptions.** Following "even should the future develop along the lines of its past," I do not use management's "mid-teens" guidance (`upi-1989-01-31`). I use about 10%, below the 12% record. That haircut allows for the currency tailwind of 1986–88, which could reverse, and for the share of recent ROE that came from leverage.

### Floor: no growth at all

$2.70 ÷ 10% = **$27.00**, plus $5.40 of affiliates = **$32.40**.

The market is paying for some growth. The question is how much.

### Growth implied by today's price

Using a perpetual-growth formula on the operating owner earnings, after removing the affiliates from the price:

$46.56 − $5.40 = $41.16 = $2.70 × (1 + g) / (0.10 − g) → **g ≈ 3.3%**

At $46.56 the market is pricing Coke's owner earnings as if they will grow at roughly the rate of inflation forever. The actual record is 10–12% a year, with international per-capita consumption at about one-fifth of the US level. That gap is the core of the case.

### Two-stage value (owner earnings per share, years 1–10, then terminal)

| Case | Growth yrs 1–10 | Discount | Terminal g | PV yrs 1–10 | PV terminal | Operating value | + Affiliates | **Intrinsic value** |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **D: management's 12% hurdle** | 10% | 12% | 5% | $24.48 | $33.81 | $58.3 | $5.4 | **$63.7** |
| **C: record roughly cut by a third** | 8% | 10% | 4% | $24.44 | $38.95 | $63.4 | $5.4 | **$68.8** |
| **A: central, record trimmed** | 10% | 10% | 5% | $27.00 | $56.70 | $83.7 | $5.4 | **$89.1** |
| **B: record continues** | 12% | 10% | 5% | $29.85 | $67.90 | $97.7 | $5.4 | **$103.1** |

**Worked example, Case A.** Because the growth rate equals the discount rate, each of years 1–10 has a present value of $2.70, so the first stage is worth 10 × $2.70 = $27.00.
- Year-10 owner earnings: $2.70 × 1.10¹⁰ = $7.00. Year 11: $7.35.
- Terminal value at year 10: $7.35 / (0.10 − 0.05) = $147.1.
- Discounted back: $147.1 / 2.594 = $56.7.

**Is the terminal weight reasonable?** In Cases A and B the terminal value is about 60–70% of the total. That weight is justified only if the franchise lasts. Stage 2 found it durable and widening, and Case D shows that even with a heavier discount rate and lower terminal value, the result stays above the price.

### Sanity checks

- **Owner-earnings yield:** $2.70 / $46.56 = 5.8% today, on a stream that has grown about 10–12% a year.
- **Price/earnings:** 16.3x 1988 EPS. Stage 2 found 45–50% unlevered returns on core capital.
- **Retention:** management plans to retain about 60% of earnings. Even if incremental capital earns half the current core return, that alone supports roughly 10%+ growth, so the 10% assumption is not stretched.
- **Management's own buying:** the company bought 19M shares at about $40 in 1988 (Stage 3). The price is now about 16% higher, while 1988 EPS rose 17%. The relationship between price and value has hardly changed since management judged its own stock a good use of cash.

## 5.3 Margin of safety against the hurdle

**What the hurdle is.** The framework gives no numerical margin. For the partnership's generals it required a "substantial excess of value" that "creates a comfortable margin of safety" (`bpl-letter-1961#3`). For an outstanding business it accepts a "sensible," even "fairly fancy," price, provided the purchase works if the future resembles the past (`brk-letter-1981#3`, `brk-letter-1987#18`).

**How I apply it.** I require that:
1. the price sits below value in the conservative cases, not only in the optimistic ones; and
2. the purchase still works if growth runs below the historical record.

| Case | Intrinsic value | Price / value | Discount (margin of safety) |
|---|---|---|---|
| D (12% hurdle) | $63.7 | 73% | **27%** |
| C (8% growth) | $68.8 | 68% | **32%** |
| A (central) | $89.1 | 52% | **48%** |
| B (record continues) | $103.1 | 45% | **55%** |

**Result: passes.**
- If growth falls by a third against its record, or if the investor demands management's own 12% return, the stock is still about 27–32% below value.
- On the central case it is about half of value.
- The price is above value only if growth falls to about 3–4%, roughly inflation, and stays there. Given the record and the moat, that is a low-probability outcome.

**Risks to the value estimate:**
- **Currency.** A sustained rise in the dollar would lower reported earnings for several years, since 76% of operating income comes from abroad. It would not damage the franchise itself.
- **CPE.** Coke's share of CPE could be worth well below its $598M book value, a risk of about $1–1.70 a share.
- **Leverage.** Debt beyond the 35% ceiling would add financial risk.
- **Latin America.** Inflation there caps price increases.

None of these comes close to closing a gap of 27% or more.

## 6. Catalyst / timing

| Rule | Application | Result |
|---|---|---|
| **No catalyst needed** ("The market may ignore business success for a while, but eventually will confirm it", `brk-letter-1987`) | The value case rests entirely on the business compounding owner earnings at about 10%+. It needs no event to unlock it. | Applies. No catalyst required |
| **Mr. Market serves, does not guide** (`brk-letter-1987`) | The stock is near its 52-week high: $46.56 against a $35.04–49.12 range, after rising from about $37 a year ago. That says nothing about value. The rise roughly matched earnings growth, so the multiple barely changed. | Neutral. The recent rise is not a reason to wait |
| **Work-outs** (`bpl-letter-1957`) | No announced merger, tender or liquidation. The ongoing buyback (14.4M shares left of the 40M authorization) is not a work-out. | Not applicable |
| **Controls** (`bpl-letter-1963`) | A $16.5B company with active management. There is no question of influencing policy, and none is needed. | Not applicable |
| **Fearful when others are greedy** (`brk-letter-1986`) | The packet gives no read on market-wide sentiment. Coke itself does not appear euphorically priced: about 16x earnings for a 45%-return franchise, implying roughly 3% perpetual growth. | No sign of greed in this stock's price |
| **No market forecasting** (`bpl-letter-1966-mid`, `fortune-1977-inflation`) | The decision rests on value against price, not on the direction of the market. | Applies |

**Timing note.** The company's own buyback competes for the same shares. A large outside buyer would also be buying alongside the company, which could lift the price during accumulation. That affects the cost of building a position, not the investment case.

## Facts the valuation needs that the packet lacks

1. **The long Treasury yield** on the as-of date, the framework's natural benchmark for discounting. I assumed 10% and tested 12%.
2. **Maintenance versus growth capital spending**, which accounts for most of the $2.40–2.90 owner-earnings range.
3. **Currency-neutral earnings**, to separate the real growth rate from the 1986–88 dollar decline.
4. **Market values of each affiliate**, especially CCE and CPE. The packet gives only the aggregate $291M by which public investees exceed book.
5. **Berkshire's alternative uses of capital and its size at end-1988**, needed to judge opportunity cost and for sizing in Stage 5.
6. **Management's own estimate of intrinsic value**, which would bear on whether the buybacks will continue at these prices.
