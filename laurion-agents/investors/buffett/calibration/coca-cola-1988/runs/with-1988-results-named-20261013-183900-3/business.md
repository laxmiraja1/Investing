# Stage 2: Business analysis (framework §3)

I apply the three rules of §3 in the order the framework lists them: return on equity first, then the moat, then owner earnings. All figures are in $ millions unless shown per share. Unless noted, they come from `ar-1988` (selected data, which restates 1984–87), `fin-summary`, and `ar-1987`.

## 3.1 Return on equity, not EPS growth

The rule (`brk-letter-1979`) asks for a high return on equity capital "without undue leverage, accounting gimmickry, etc." I take the headline figure first and then check it against each of those two conditions.

### Headline record

| Year | ROE (net to common / avg common equity) | Total debt / total capital |
|---|---|---|
| 1978 | 22.6% | 3.8% |
| 1979 | 23.0% | 6.3% |
| 1980 | 21.1% | 9.3% |
| 1981 | 22.2% | 9.1% |
| 1982 | 20.3% | 15.1% |
| 1983 | 19.6% | 15.2% |
| 1984 | 22.2% | 32.3% |
| 1985 | 25.3% | 30.3% |
| 1986 | 29.1% | 34.7% |
| 1987 | 27.5% | 48.4% |
| 1988 | 33.3% | 38.8% |

### Leverage check

**Baseline without much debt.** From 1978 to 1981, debt was below 10% of capital and ROE still ran 21–23%. That level was achieved with almost no borrowing. It also included lower-return businesses that were later sold, such as the plastics companies and Ronco.

**Part of the recent rise comes from leverage and buybacks.** Treasury stock at cost rose from $604M in 1985 to $2,069M in 1988. Buybacks from 1984 to 1988 totalled about $2.19B (335.7 + 379.9 + 110.5 + 605.1 + 758.2). That spending shrank the equity denominator. Debt rose at the same time.

**Unlevered return on the whole company, 1988.**
- Net interest expense was $31M (230.5 − 199.3), or about $21M after a 34% tax. Adding it back gives earnings before net financing of about $1,065M.
- Net capital (equity + debt − cash) was $4,238M at end-1988 and about $4,148M at end-1987. The 1987 figure nets out the $545M of CPE notes, which were collected in cash in January 1988. The average is about $4,190M.
- Return on net capital is therefore about **25%**. That is roughly what the business earns with no help from debt.

**That 25% understates the core business.** About $1.9–2.2B of the net capital is equity investments in affiliates: CPE, CCE, TCC and other bottlers. These earned only $48M in 1988 (about 2.5%) and $113M in 1987 (about 5%).
- Excluding the affiliates from both earnings and capital: about $1,017M on about $2,130M, or **roughly 45–50% unlevered**.
- The segment data points the same way. Soft-drink operating income was $1,691M (USA 351.9 + International 1,338.8) on soft-drink identifiable assets of $3,809M. That is about 44% pretax on *gross* assets, before netting off payables. The comparable 1987 figure was 34%.

**Conclusion on leverage.** Measured with no leverage at all, the concentrate business earns a very high return. Debt and buybacks have lifted the reported 33% above the underlying figure, but the underlying business is a high-return business on its own. This answers the question carried forward from Stage 1.

### Accounting gimmickry check

**One-time items have been large and need stripping out.**
- In 1986, EPS of $2.42 included a +$0.68 gain on the sale of CCE stock and −$0.33 of provisions. Excluding both, ROE was about 25%, not 29%.
- In 1987, a $40M TCC gain roughly offset the $36M Foods provision.
- In 1988, earnings include Coke's share of CCE's $104M pretax gain on selling a bottler, a $25M BCI gain, CPE write-down losses, and $13M of exchange losses. These roughly net out. The 1988 result looks substantially clean.

**The structures are legal, but they flatter the reported returns.** Coke moved capital-heavy bottling (CCE, TCC, the UK joint venture) and entertainment (CPE) into 49%-owned affiliates. Their debt does not appear on Coke's balance sheet. CCE alone carried about $2.3B of non-current liabilities at end-1988.
- Coke says it does not guarantee CCE's debt (`ar-1986` note 3).
- It remains contingently liable for about $133M of bottler guarantees and $101M of CPE receivables sold with recourse.
- Reported ROE is high partly because the low-return capital of the Coca-Cola *system* sits off the balance sheet.
- What matters for Coke's owners is whether the concentrate business keeps its margin while the bottlers carry that capital. So far it has: US concentrate gross margin was about 63% in 1986 (`ar-1986`), and Coke has flexible pricing rights in the new CCE contract.

**Pending accounting change.** Under FASB 96, adoption could cut shareholders' equity by about $175M. That is about 5% of equity, which is modest.

**Currency.** Key hard currencies strengthened against the dollar by about 27% in 1986, 16% in 1987 and 10% in 1988, and 76% of operating income is earned abroad. So part of the 1985–88 earnings growth is a falling dollar, not business progress. The cleaner gauge is US soft-drink operating income, which grew about 10% in 1987 and 9% in 1988 on volume growth of about 6%. Volume was 6% (US retail) and 7% (worldwide).

### Why the framework looks past EPS growth

EPS from continuing operations went from $1.72 in 1985 to $2.85 in 1988, about 18% a year. Average shares fell from 393M to 365M over the same period, so about 2.5 points of that annual growth is buybacks. Currency adds more. The return-on-capital figures above are the better measure, and they are excellent.

## 3.2 Durable competitive advantage: the moat

The GEICO passage (`brk-letter-1986#16`) defines the test: a cost or franchise gap that competitors cannot close, and that management keeps widening. The packet offers six pieces of evidence for that gap.

**1. Share, and its direction.**
- World share is close to 45%, up from under 38% in 1980 (`ar-1988`). That is more than double the nearest competitor.
- US share is over 40%, about 9 points ahead of the primary US competitor in 1986.
- In US fountain, Coke has a 59% share and outsells its nearest rival by more than 2-to-1. It captured about three-quarters of segment growth over four years (`ar-1986`, `ar-1987`).
- The share leader is gaining share. That is the opposite of a franchise being eroded.

**2. A trademark gap competitors cannot buy.**
- Landor's 1988 surveys put the gap between Coca-Cola and the No. 2 brand wider than the gap between No. 2 and No. 10 (`ar-1988`).
- The 1985 reformulation is the most telling evidence. Consumers rejected changes to the product itself, and "classic" became the best-selling US soft drink by 1986. A brand that customers will not let its own owner change has unusually strong customer attachment.

**3. Pricing power and capture of tax cuts.**
- Management states it "is able to adjust prices to compensate for increasing costs" (`ar-1986`, `ar-1987`, `ar-1988`).
- The new bottler contracts give Coke discretionary pricing, and it used them in October 1986.
- The federal statutory tax rate fell from 46% to 34%. Coke's effective rate fell from 38% (1986) to 34% (1988) while operating margins rose: total-company operating margin went from about 15% in 1986 (ex-provision, restated) to 19.2% in 1988. That pattern fits the 1986 letter's analysis (`brk-letter-1986#22`) that strong unregulated franchises keep a tax cut rather than passing it to customers.

**4. A cost and scale moat, like GEICO's.**
- Coke describes itself as the low-cost producer in most of its 155–160 countries.
- More than 1,000 bottlers and about 559M servings a day (up from 359M in 1980, roughly 5.7% a year) give it distribution no competitor matches outside a few markets.
- The shift from syrup to concentrate lowers transport costs. About 89% of US product was made from concentrate in 1988.

**5. Room to grow without new capital.**
- International per-capita consumption was about 16% of the US rate in 1986 and about 51 servings a year in 1988, against 274 in the US (1987).
- Each additional serving abroad flows largely through bottlers' capital, not Coke's. International soft-drink operating margin was about 30% in 1988 (1,338.8 / 4,503.8).

**6. Management widening the moat.**
- Advertising rose from $648M (1986) to $842M (1988).
- Coke is consolidating bottlers in Germany, France and the Netherlands.
- It is placing low-cost equipment such as coolers in emerging markets: about 55,000 coolers in Indonesia, and 26,000 vending machines added in Japan in 1988.
- This parallels the GEICO executive driving costs down "thereby defending and strengthening the economic franchise."

**Weak spots in the moat.**
- **Foods.** This segment is a commodity-exposed orange juice business: operating margin of about 6%, volume down 22% in frozen and 6% in chilled in 1988. It is about 6% of operating income. It is outside the moat, not a threat to it.
- **Latin America.** Triple-digit inflation (volume and operating income down in Argentina and Brazil) shows the franchise cannot fully offset destruction of local purchasing power. Price increases there are capped at inflation (`ar-1987`).
- **Currency.** Translation risk is significant, but it is a reporting-volatility risk, not a franchise risk.
- **Competitor and health trends.** Coke leads diet and one-calorie in the US, with diet Coke at the No. 3 brand and growing 13% in 1988. The packet contains nothing on the primary competitor's economics, so the size of the share gap can be confirmed but the competitor's cost position cannot.

**Verdict.** The moat is wide and widening, and its sources are understandable: brand, distribution scale and low cost. Ten years out, the most likely state is the same business with more of the world drinking its product. That combination of "business castle" and "moat" is what the framework is looking for.

## 3.3 Owner earnings (`brk-letter-1986`)

The definition is: reported earnings, plus depreciation, amortization and other non-cash charges, minus the average annual capital spending the business needs. The packet's passage is truncated after "plant and equipment." In the full 1986 letter, the deduction is the capital spending needed to maintain competitive position and unit volume, together with any extra working capital needed. The packet does not split capital spending into maintenance and growth, so I give a range.

### Inputs, 1988 (with 1986–88 cash-flow data)

| Item | 1988 | Comment |
|---|---|---|
| Net income | 1,044.7 | 1986–88 average ex-one-offs ≈ 930 |
| Depreciation & amortization | 169.8 | 1987: 154.5; 1986: 155.4 |
| Deferred income taxes | +43.9 | 1987 was −27.0, so it averages out |
| Undistributed equity income (non-cash) | −35.8 | |
| Capex | 386.8 | 1987: 303.5; 1986: 345.8; 3-year avg ≈ 346 |
| Working capital | small inflow | Business has run on negative working capital: payables exceed receivables plus inventory; 1987 released $272M |

### Two bounds

**Low bound: treat all capex as maintenance**, using the 3-year average.
1,044.7 + 169.8 + 43.9 − 35.8 − 346 ≈ **$877M**, about **$2.40 per average share** (365M).

**High bound: maintenance capex ≈ depreciation.** This is plausible because soft-drink capex in 1988 was $239M against soft-drink D&A of $110M, and the excess is explicitly growth: international infrastructure, coolers and fountain equipment.
That gives ≈ **$1,050M**, about **$2.88 per share**. On 355M year-end shares it is about $2.96.

**Point estimate.** Owner earnings are probably close to reported earnings, about **$2.6–2.9 per share** for 1988. The concentrate business converts nearly all its earnings to cash. Net cash from operations was $1,167M in 1988 and $1,212M in 1987.

### Two deductions the formula does not capture

- **Bottler equity investment.** Coke puts money into bottlers every year: $339M of investment purchases in 1987 and $129M in 1988, against about $1.9B carried in affiliates. Some of this is the price of keeping the system strong. On a strict reading it is "required to maintain competitive position" and partly a deduction. I cannot separate it from discretionary spending with the packet.
- **Inflation.** Management states that earnings on a current-cost basis "would be less than reported." The LIFO reserve is only about $30M and fixed-asset intensity is low, so the gap is probably small. The 1977 inflation article (`fortune-1977-inflation`) makes the point that low-capital, high-return businesses suffer least from it.

## Facts the framework wants that the packet lacks

1. **Capital spending split between maintenance and growth.** This drives the owner-earnings range of about $2.40 to $2.90 per share.
2. **Currency-neutral operating income by region.** This would show how much of 1986–88 growth was the dollar.
3. **Concentrate price, volume and mix bridges, especially internationally.** This would show how pricing power is being exercised outside the US. Only the US 63% concentrate gross margin (1986) is given.
4. **The primary competitor's share, margins and costs.** This is needed to measure the width of the moat, not only its existence.
5. **Bottler economics.** CCE earned about $143M on $1.8B of net assets in 1988, roughly 8%. Data for the other bottlers would show whether the system can keep absorbing the capital that keeps Coke's own returns high.
6. **Market values of each equity investment.** The packet gives only that public investees exceeded their carrying value by $291M at end-1988. This matters for valuing the non-core assets in Stage 4.
7. **1988 10-Qs and full 1988 cash taxes.** Interim detail is limited to the July 1988 wire story.
8. **Berkshire's capital at end-1988.** This is still not in the packet. It is needed for sizing in later stages.

## Stage 2 summary

| Test | Finding | Result |
|---|---|---|
| ROE | Reported 33%. About 25% on all net capital with no leverage, about 45–50% on the concentrate business ex-affiliates. Above 20% even in the near-debt-free years of 1978–81. Leverage and buybacks inflate the headline but do not create it. | Passes clearly |
| Accounting | Gains and provisions in 1986–87 must be stripped out; 1988 is substantially clean. The deconsolidation structures flatter reported returns but appear to be real and non-recourse. | Passes, with adjustments noted |
| Moat | Widening share, an unmatched brand, pricing power (it kept the tax cut), scale and low cost, and a long volume runway abroad that bottlers largely fund. Foods and Latin America are weak spots outside the core. | Strong, durable, widening |
| Owner earnings | About $0.88–1.05B, or roughly $2.40–2.90 per share for 1988, close to reported earnings. Cash conversion is high and the working-capital need is negative. | High quality |

This is the kind of business the framework is built to find: a high-return franchise that needs little capital, with competent management widening its advantage. Price, management's capital allocation and position size are for the next stages.
