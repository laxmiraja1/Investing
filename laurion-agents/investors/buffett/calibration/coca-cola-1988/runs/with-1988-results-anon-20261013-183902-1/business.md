# Stage 2: Business analysis of Company A, as of 1989-02-27

The framework's business-analysis section has three tests, applied here in order: return on equity (adjusted for leverage and accounting effects), durable competitive advantage, and owner earnings. Figures are $ millions unless stated.

## 1. Return on equity, not EPS growth

The rule (`brk-letter-1979`) is that the primary test is "a high earnings rate on equity capital employed (without undue leverage, accounting gimmickry, etc.)." So the reported return needs checking against both of those conditions.

**Reported ROE** (`ar-1988`, selected financial data; 1985–88 restated):

| | 1979 | 1981 | 1983 | 1985 | 1986 | 1987 | 1988 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| ROE on average equity | 23.0% | 22.2% | 19.6% | 25.3% | 29.1% | 27.5% | 33.3% |
| Total debt / total capital | 6.3% | 9.1% | 15.2% | 30.3% | 34.7% | 48.4% | 38.8% |

The rise in ROE from about 20% to 33% happened while leverage rose from almost nothing to 39%. Two other things also shrank the equity base:
- Buybacks: treasury stock at cost is $2,069M, against $604M at end-1985.
- The $335M in-kind dividend of entertainment-affiliate shares.

Book value per common share fell from $9.13 (1986) to $8.58 (1988; $3,045M over 354.8M shares) even though earnings were retained. Part of the higher ROE is therefore a smaller denominator, not better business performance.

**Adjustment 1: remove leverage.**
- Net capital at end-1988 = common equity $3,045 + preferred $300 + net debt ($2,124 debt − $1,231 cash) $893 = about $4,238.
- Unlevered income = net income $1,045 + after-tax net interest (about $31 pretax, $21 after tax) = about $1,065.
- Return on net capital is about **25%**. That compares with roughly 20–22% in 1979–81, when the company held net cash.

So the business itself has improved, but by less than the headline 33% implies.

**Adjustment 2: separate the core from the affiliates.** About $1,912M of the $4,238M is in equity-method affiliates: bottlers CCE, TCC and others, plus the entertainment affiliate CPE. These produced only $48M of equity income in 1988, a 2.5% return, and 1987's $113M on $2,218M was 5% (`ar-1988` Note 15). Removing them:
- Core capital is about $2,326M.
- Core after-tax income is about $1,017M.
- Return on core capital is about **44%**.

**Cross-check against segment assets**, which involve no leverage or financing (`ar-1988` Note 15; `ar-1987` Note 16):

| Pretax operating income / identifiable assets | 1986* | 1987 | 1988 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Soft drinks, USA | 20.6% | 15.8% | 20.6% |
| Soft drinks, International | 47.7% | 52.1% | 63.8% |
| Soft drinks, combined | 35.9% | 34.3% | **44.4%** |
| Foods | 20.3% | 10.6% | 12.9% |

*1986 excludes the $180M of provisions.

The combined soft-drink figure of 44% pretax is about 29% after tax, earned on gross assets with no leverage. That is the core economic fact about this business. It sits in the same range as the Berkshire businesses described in `brk-letter-1987#1` and well above the Fortune 1,000 benchmark cited there, where only six companies averaged more than 30% over a decade.

**Accounting points to note:**
- **Repeated gains and charges.** There was a $375M IPO gain in 1986 and a $40M gain in 1987, alongside $180M (1986) and $36M (1987) of provisions. 1988 still contains one-offs: the company's share of CCE's $104M pretax gain, CPE's $114M write-down, a $25M gain on the BCI spin-off, and $(13)M of currency losses. These roughly cancel out, so 1988 is close to a clean year.
- **Bottler debt is off the balance sheet.** Holding 49% of the bottlers keeps their leverage off the company's books. CCE alone has $2.86B of liabilities. A 49% share of that would add about $1.4B of look-through debt.
- **Restatements.** Restatements in each of 1986, 1987 and 1988 break the comparability of revenue. Net income is unaffected.
- **Inflation.** Management says current-cost income "would be less than reported" but gives no figure (`ar-1988` MD&A).

**EPS versus ROE.** EPS grew 15.7% a year from 1985 to 1988 ($1.84 to $2.85). Net income grew 13.1% a year. The gap is the buyback. Under the framework, the EPS growth is not the evidence that matters. The evidence is the 44% pretax return on soft-drink assets and its rise since 1986.

## 2. Durable competitive advantage (moat)

The GEICO test (`brk-letter-1986#16`) asks whether an advantage protects the castle and whether management is widening it.

**Evidence of a moat:**
- **Share leadership, still growing.** Worldwide share rose from below 38% in 1980 to nearly 45% in 1988, "more than double the number anyone else sells." U.S. share is over 40%. In fountain, it outsells its nearest rival by more than 2 to 1, and it took about three-quarters of the segment's growth over four years (`ar-1987`, `ar-1988`).
- **Trademark strength.** In the 1988 Landor surveys, the gap between Brand A and the second-ranked brand was larger than the gap between the second and the tenth (`ar-1988`).
- **Survived a self-inflicted error.** After the 1985 reformulation, the original formula was reintroduced as "Brand A classic." By 1986 it was the best-selling soft drink in the U.S. (`ar-1986`). A franchise that comes through its own management's mistake intact is strong.
- **Pricing power.** Gross margin was 50.5% in 1986, 52.6% in 1987 and 55.6% in 1988 (restated basis). Operating margin went from 15.4% (1986, before provisions) to 17.8% to 19.2%. U.S. concentrate gross margin was about 63% in 1986. New contracts let the company set prices to CCE "at its discretion," and Latin America moved to prices that raise margins in 1988.
- **Distribution as a second barrier.** The system has more than 1,000 bottlers in more than 160 countries and serves 559M servings a day. Japan alone has 700,000+ vending machines. The company says it is the "low-cost producer in most of the 155 countries" (`ar-1987`).
- **Long growth runway with little capital.** International consumption of company products is about 51 servings per person per year, against 274 in the U.S. International volume grows 7–12% a year, and international assets earn 64% pretax.
- **The moat is widening.** Daily servings rose from 359M (1981) to 559M (1988), and share and margins rose over the same period. This matches the GEICO pattern of management deepening the moat.

**Limits and risks:**
- **The U.S. business is much less profitable** than the international one: 15.4% operating margin and 21% pretax return on assets, against 30% and 64% internationally. It faces a strong primary competitor and pays about $68M a year in cooperative marketing to CCE alone.
- **Foods is closer to a commodity business.** Its returns depend on orange-solid prices, juice volumes fell in 1988, and it earns about 13% pretax on assets. It is small (6% of operating income), but it is the kind of business the textile lesson (`brk-letter-1985#9`) warns about.
- **Currency.** About 76% of operating income comes from outside the U.S. The dollar weakened 10%, 16% and 27% against key currencies in 1988, 1987 and 1986, so part of the reported international growth is translation rather than volume or price.
- **Capital tied up in low-return affiliates.** Roughly $1.9B sits in bottlers and entertainment earning 2.5–5%. This is a capital-allocation question for Stage 4.

## 3. Owner earnings (1988)

The rule (`brk-letter-1986`) is reported earnings, plus depreciation, amortization and certain non-cash charges, less average capitalized expenditures.

| Item | $M |
|---|---|
| Net income available to common | 1,038.3 |
| + Depreciation and amortization | 169.8 |
| − Average capex, 1986–88 ($346 / $304 / $387) | (345.7) |
| **Owner earnings (conservative)** | **≈ 862** |
| Per year-end share (354.8M) | **≈ $2.43** |

**Upper bound.** If maintenance capex is close to depreciation, owner earnings approach reported earnings of about $1,038M, or $2.93 per share. Capex is running at 2.3 times depreciation, and the company describes it as international infrastructure, capacity expansion and a corporate building. That suggests much of it is for growth, but the packet does not split maintenance from growth.

**Cash check.** Operating cash flow of $1,167M less capex of $387M gives $780M. This includes working-capital and other swings.

**Look-through earnings.** Equity income of $48M includes $36M not received in cash. A share of the affiliates' retained earnings is economically the company's, but 1988 is distorted by CPE's write-down.

**Working range:** owner earnings of about **$860–1,040M**, or **$2.40–2.90 per share**. At $46.56, that is about 16–19 times owner earnings. Valuation is Stage 5.

The business needs little capital. Soft-drink capex was $240M in 1988 against $1,691M of soft-drink operating income, so about 14 cents of each operating dollar is reinvested. This is the See's-like trait the 1977 inflation article (`fortune-1977-inflation#6`) favors: high profitability with low capital needs, and management states it can "adjust prices to compensate for increasing costs."

## Facts the framework wants that the packet lacks

1. **Maintenance versus growth capex**, needed for item (c) of owner earnings. Only total capex is given.
2. **International results in local currency**, to separate real volume and price gains from dollar translation.
3. **Price and cost per unit of concentrate over 10+ years**, to show pricing power directly rather than through margin ratios.
4. **Bottler economics.** The system's strength depends on bottlers earning adequate returns, but there is no data on independent bottlers' returns or leverage, and only aggregate figures for CCE.
5. **Competitor data**, such as the primary competitor's margins, costs and share by country. This would be the equivalent of GEICO's expense-ratio comparison and would measure the moat's width.
6. **Performance through a severe recession or soft-drink downturn.** The record runs from 1976 to 1988 and does not include such a test at the current scale.
7. **Current-cost (inflation-adjusted) earnings.** Management says they are lower but gives no figure.
8. **1988 quarterly reports (10-Qs)**, which are not in the packet, and the full CCE balance sheet for look-through leverage.
9. **Bottler contract terms**: duration, exclusivity, and formula versus flexible pricing beyond the U.S. Brand A syrup case.

## Summary

The underlying business earns about 44% pretax (about 29% after tax) on unlevered soft-drink assets. That return is rising, and it is protected by the world's strongest trademark and a distribution system no competitor matches. Reported ROE of 33% overstates the improvement because of debt, buybacks and off-balance-sheet bottlers. Net of those, the core is still exceptional, and the moat is measurably widening. The weaker parts (U.S. margins, Foods, and capital parked in affiliates) are small next to the international concentrate franchise. Owner earnings are about $2.40–2.90 per share.
