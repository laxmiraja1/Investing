# Stage 4: Valuation of Company A, as of 1989-02-27

Price at the last close (1989-02-24): **$46.56**. Shares outstanding: 354.8M. Market value: **$16.52B** (`prices`).

## 1. The framework's valuation rule: buy below net working capital

The only explicit rule in the Valuation section is the partnership-era test: what is net working capital "alone (before placing any value on the plants and equipment)" per share (`bpl-letter-1965`)?

From the end-1988 balance sheet (`ar-1988`):

| | $M | Per share |
|---|---|---|
| Current assets | 3,245.4 | |
| − Current liabilities | (2,868.9) | |
| **Net working capital** | **376.5** | **$1.06** |
| − Long-term debt, other liabilities, deferred taxes, preferred stock | (1,536.5) | |
| **Net-net working capital** | **(1,160.0)** | **−$3.27** |
| Book value per common share | 3,045.3 | $8.58 |

The price is about **44 times net working capital** and 5.4 times book value. Net-net working capital is negative. Adding the equity affiliates at market (about $2.2B, or $6.20 a share) and all cash does not change this.

**Result:** the stock fails the framework's explicit valuation hurdle completely. On this test there is no margin of safety; the shortfall is about $45.50 a share.

## 2. Why the analysis does not stop there

The framework's own later writings set the net-working-capital test aside for businesses like this one:
- `brk-letter-1987#18`: "Our goal is to find an outstanding business at a sensible price, not a mediocre business at a bargain price… In the interim, I searched for 'bargains'… My punishment was an education in the economics of short-line farm implement manufacturers…"
- `brk-letter-1981#3`: buy at "prices that will make sense, even should the future of the acquired enterprise develop much along the lines of its past," and "fractional interests in easily-identifiable princes at toad-like prices," while weighing "alternative uses for the funds."
- `bpl-letter-1963#5`: value is "what careful analysis indicates its value to a private owner to be."
- `brk-letter-1986`: owner earnings are the earnings that matter to an owner.

These passages sit outside the Valuation section. I treat them as the operative test as of 1989, while noting that the explicit rule fails.

**The test applied:** what does a private owner earn on $46.56 if owner earnings follow roughly their past course, compared with the alternative use of the money?

## 3. Inputs

- **Owner earnings per share:** $2.43 (subtracting average capex) to $2.93 (treating maintenance capex as equal to depreciation), from Stage 2. Midpoint **$2.70**. The 1988 one-offs (the CCE gain against the CPE write-down) roughly cancel.
- **Record of the past:**
  - Net income from continuing operations: $432M (1981) to $1,045M (1988), **13.5% a year**.
  - EPS: $1.17 to $2.85 (13.6% a year since 1981; 16.1% a year since 1983). Buybacks help the per-share figure.
- **Growth rates I will test:** 8%, 12% (below the record) and 15%. Management's guidance of mid-teens EPS growth in 1989 (`upi-1989-01-31`) is not used; projections "are of little interest."
- **Alternative use of funds:** the packet gives no long-term Treasury yield. **I assume about 9%.** Packet evidence is consistent with this: the finance subsidiary's 9.32% bond due 1990, about 11.8% on other debt, the money-market preferred at about 6.5–6.8%, and management's 12% after-tax cost of capital.

**Initial owner-earnings yield:** $2.43–2.93 / $46.56 = **5.2–6.3%**, against about 9% on bonds. At this price the purchase only works if the business keeps growing. Stage 2 supports that: about 44% pretax return on soft-drink assets, international consumption at 51 servings per person against 274 in the U.S., and reinvestment needs of about 14 cents per dollar of operating income.

## 4. Test: what if the future resembles the past?

Owner earnings per share are compounded from $2.70 for ten years and valued at the end at 10× or 15× (the current multiple is about 17×). Dividends start at the 1989 rate of $1.36 and grow with earnings.

**Value at 9%** (present value of the year-10 value plus the dividends):

| Owner-earnings growth | Year-10 owner earnings/share | Exit 10× | Exit 15× |
|---|---|---|---|
| 8% | $5.83 | ~$37 | **~$49** |
| 12% | $8.39 | ~$50 | **~$67** |
| 15% | $10.92 | ~$62 | **~$85** |

**Approximate annual return from $46.56**, with about 3 points of dividends included:

| Owner-earnings growth | Exit 10× | Exit 15× |
|---|---|---|
| 8% | ~5–6% | ~10% |
| 12% | ~9–10% | ~14% |
| 15% | ~12% | ~17% |

**Reading the tables:**
- **Base case** (12% growth, below the 13.5% record; exit at 15×, below today's multiple): value about **$67**, return about 14%. That is about 5 points better than bonds, from a business whose earnings stand up to inflation (`fortune-1977-inflation`).
- **Pessimistic but plausible** (8% growth and 15×, or 12% growth and a fall to 10×): value about **$49–50**. The owner earns roughly the bond rate.
- **Bad case** (8% growth and 10×): about 5–6% a year. Returns are positive and capital is kept, so there is no "permanent loss of capital" (`bpl-letter-1967-oct`). To lose money over ten years, growth would have to fall to about 5% and the multiple would have to compress. Nothing in the 1977–1988 record suggests that.

## 5. Margin of safety

| Hurdle | Value | Margin at $46.56 |
|---|---|---|
| Framework's explicit rule: price below net working capital | $1.06 | **None (fails)** |
| Value to a private owner, base case | ~$67 | **~30%** |
| Value to a private owner, pessimistic case | ~$49 | ~5% |
| Value to a private owner, favorable case | ~$85 | ~45% |

The partnership-era rule asked for a "substantial excess of value… a comfortable margin of safety" (`bpl-letter-1962#4`). About 30% in the base case is a real margin, though not the half-price bargain of the earlier years. Here the margin comes from the durability of the business, and by 1987 the framework says that is where it should come from: "an outstanding business at a sensible price."

**Cross-check with Stage 3.** The 1988 buybacks at an average of $39.94 were about 40% below the base value, so "well below intrinsic value" is met. The 1987 purchases at $42.70 were made when owner earnings were about $2.2, so they were close to value and not well below it.

## 6. Catalyst / timing (framework section 6)

- **No catalyst is needed.** This is not a work-out, because there is no pending sale, merger or liquidation (`bpl-letter-1957`). It is not a control situation either: at $16.5B a controlling stake is impossible (`bpl-letter-1963`). The return comes from the business compounding, and "the market may ignore business success for a while, but eventually will confirm it" (`brk-letter-1987`).
- **Timing.** There is no forecast of the market (`bpl-letter-1966-mid`). The price is set against value. Two observations:
  - The market is not offering a panic price. The stock is near its 52-week high ($49.12), up about 24% in a year, at 16.3× earnings against about 10.5× in 1980. The October 1987 low of $28.96 was the "fearful" moment, and it has passed.
  - "Mr. Market is there to serve you, not to guide you." A base-case discount of about 30% on a wide-moat business is enough on its own. Waiting for a lower price risks the "rare, fast-moving elephant" not coming back (`brk-letter-1987`).

## 7. Missing figures and assumptions

1. **Long-term government bond yield.** Assumed about 9% from the packet clues above. Every 1 point added to the hurdle lowers the base value by roughly 8–10%.
2. **Maintenance versus growth capex.** This sets the owner-earnings range ($2.43–2.93). I used the midpoint.
3. **International earnings in local currency.** A weaker dollar helped 1986–88 growth. If part of that reverses, the realistic growth rate is nearer 10–12% than the record's 13.5%. That is one reason the base case uses 12%.
4. **Look-through earnings of the affiliates** beyond reported equity income, and their full market value (only the combined $291M excess over carrying value is given). This is ignored here and is small, about $0.80 a share.
5. **Berkshire's competing opportunities and available capital on the as-of date**, which are not in the packet. These matter for the "alternative uses for the funds" comparison and for sizing in the next stage.

## Summary

The stock fails the framework's only explicit valuation rule, buying below net working capital, by an enormous margin. On the framework's later standard (a sensible price for an outstanding business, and a sensible price if the future follows the past), $46.56 is about 30% below a base-case private-owner value of about $67, using growth below the record and an exit multiple below today's. The pessimistic case is roughly fair value, and none of the realistic cases shows a permanent loss. No catalyst is required.
