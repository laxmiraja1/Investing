**Memo: Purchase of Company A (AAA) common stock — as of February 27, 1989**

We recommend buying Company A common stock at the current price of $46.56, building toward about 25% of the portfolio, and holding it indefinitely. This memo explains what the business is, why it meets our standards, what we think it is worth, and what would make us reconsider.

Company A sells soft-drink concentrate and syrup to more than a thousand bottlers in over 160 countries. The bottlers make, package, and distribute the drinks. In 1988 more than 90% of operating income came from this one activity, under what independent surveys rank as the world's best-known trademark. The company also owns minority stakes in several large bottlers and in a film and television company, and runs a juice business. These are secondary. The juice business produced about 6% of operating income last year and is close to a commodity business.

The business meets each of our entry tests. It is not a turnaround: income from continuing operations has risen every year since 1977, reaching $1.04 billion in 1988. It is simple, with no technology risk. It is large enough to matter. The managers who built the current record have been in place since 1981. The company carries meaningful debt, about 39% of total capital. Cash covers most of it, however, and net debt is less than one year's earnings. The core operation needs no borrowing at all.

The economics are what stand out. Reported return on equity was 33% in 1988, but that figure is flattered by debt, by buybacks that shrank the equity base, and by keeping bottler debt off the balance sheet. A cleaner measure is pretax operating income against the assets of the soft-drink operations, which involves no borrowing. That return was about 44% in 1988, up from 36% two years earlier. International operations earned about 64%. Reinvestment needs are small: soft-drink capital spending was $240 million against $1.69 billion of soft-drink operating income.

The advantage protecting these returns is growing. Worldwide share rose from under 38% in 1980 to nearly 45%, more than double the nearest competitor. Gross margin rose from about 50% to nearly 56% over three years. Outside the United States, consumption of the company's drinks is about 51 servings per person a year, against 274 at home, so there is plenty of room to grow without much new capital. The company survived its own 1985 reformulation mistake with the original brand back at number one within a year, which says a good deal about consumer loyalty.

On capital allocation, management's record is mostly good but not spotless. Since 1984 it has repurchased about 69 million shares, roughly a sixth of the company, at an average of $31.53. The 1988 purchases averaged under $40, well below our estimate of value. Earnings retained since 1981 total roughly $2.5 billion, and the company's market value has risen by about $12 billion over the same period. Against this, the move into film and television was a costly detour, financed in part by issuing stock at about 14 times earnings. About $1.9 billion now sits in bottling and entertainment affiliates earning low returns. Management has since refocused on the core. The annual reports are more promotional than we would like, but the financial statements underneath them are straightforward.

On valuation, the stock fails the test we once relied on most: buying below net working capital. Working capital is about $1 per share, against a price of $46.56. We do not think that test fits a business whose value lies in earning power rather than in assets. For a business like this, the right question is what a private owner earns if the future resembles the past.

We estimate 1988 owner earnings at $2.43 to $2.93 per share. That is reported earnings plus depreciation, less normal capital spending. The past record is about 13.5% annual growth in earnings. We assumed 12% growth for ten years, an exit multiple of 15 times owner earnings (below today's), and a 9% alternative return from long-term bonds. On those assumptions the shares are worth about $67, roughly 30% above the current price. If growth slows to 8%, value is about $49, close to the price. Even if both growth and the multiple disappoint, we expect returns of about 5–6% a year rather than a loss of capital.

No special event is needed for the investment to work. The return comes from the business compounding.

We would hold a full 40% position only with a deeper discount. Three things keep us at about 25%:

- The margin of safety is real but moderate.
- About three-quarters of operating income is earned abroad, so a stronger dollar would slow reported growth.
- Management uses debt by choice, including bottler debt held off the balance sheet.

We would reconsider under any of the following:

- Normal capital spending turns out to consume much more of earnings than we assume.
- Growth measured in local currencies proves much weaker than the dollar figures suggest.
- Long-term interest rates move well above 9%.
- Market share begins a sustained decline.
- Management returns to large acquisitions outside soft drinks, or issues shares on unfavorable terms.

A rising stock price alone would not be a reason to sell. Before committing, we would still want to meet management, review the proxy statement for compensation and insider ownership, and confirm that the capital is not needed for a better use elsewhere.
