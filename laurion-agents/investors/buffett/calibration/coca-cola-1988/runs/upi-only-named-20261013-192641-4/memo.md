**Memo: A Position in The Coca-Cola Company (as of February 27, 1989)**

We recommend buying The Coca-Cola Company at about $46.56 a share and building toward roughly a quarter of the portfolio. The case rests entirely on facts in the company's 1986 and 1987 annual reports, its 1988 earnings releases, and the price record through February 24, 1989.

The business is simpler than the consolidated statements make it look. At its core, Coca-Cola makes soft-drink syrup and concentrate and sells it to independent bottlers and fountain customers in more than 155 countries. The bottlers buy the trucks, plants and coolers. Coca-Cola supplies the brand, the marketing and a product with a gross margin of about 63 percent. In 1987 this core business produced more than 95 percent of operating income. Around it sit pieces that dilute the results. There is a juice business under pressure, 49 percent stakes in large bottlers such as Coca-Cola Enterprises, and a 49 percent stake in Columbia Pictures Entertainment. The company has been spinning these pieces off, selling them down or shrinking them.

The company passes each screen we apply. It is not a turnaround. Earnings from continuing operations rose every year from 1976 to 1988, reaching $1.04 billion, or $2.85 a share. It doesn't need debt to earn its returns. From 1977 to 1981, with debt between 3.5 and 9 percent of capital, it earned about 20 percent on equity while still carrying its owned bottlers and the juice business. More recent equity returns of 27 to 33 percent are partly the result of share repurchases and some borrowing. The better measure is the soft-drink segments themselves, which earned about 34 to 36 percent pretax on their assets in 1986 and 1987. International operations earned about 52 percent. Few large companies earn returns like these, and these are earned without much capital. Soft-drink capital spending in 1987 was about $170 million, against roughly $1.4 billion of soft-drink operating income.

The protection around those returns is the brand and its distribution system, and the evidence points to a franchise that is getting stronger. Gross margins rose from about 50 percent in 1985 to about 55 percent in 1988, during a period when corporate tax rates fell. Companies with weak franchises tend to pass tax savings on to customers, and this one kept them. U.S. share exceeds 40 percent, the fountain business outsells its nearest rival by more than two to one, and the company accounts for about 44 percent of soft drinks sold worldwide outside the Soviet Union and China. The 1985 formula change is the most telling evidence. Within a year of a widely publicized mistake, the company reached record U.S. share and Coca-Cola classic became the best-selling soft drink in the country. The room for growth is plain: consumption per person outside the U.S. is about one-sixth of the U.S. level.

Management deserves credit with some reservations. Since the current team took over in 1981, each dollar of earnings kept in the business has been matched by about five and a half dollars of increase in the share price. Even assuming the price-earnings multiple had not risen, the figure would be about two and a half dollars. The prior team retained earnings and saw the share price fall. About 69 million shares were repurchased from 1984 through 1988, mostly at 11 to 15 times earnings. The Columbia Pictures purchase earned less than the company's own stated 12 percent cost of capital, and the bottling affiliates earn mid-single-digit returns. The more important point is that management has been unwinding these rather than adding to them. Its annual reports are promotional in tone, but they disclose one-time gains and charges, currency effects and segment returns clearly. We haven't been able to assess the executives in person, and the proxy statement on their pay is not in our materials.

On value, the stock fails the old test of buying below net working capital. Its current assets barely exceed its current liabilities. That test is meant for mediocre businesses at bargain prices, and this is an outstanding business that has to be valued on its earning power. We estimate 1988 owner earnings at about $2.45 to $2.75 a share. That is reported earnings plus depreciation, less capital spending to maintain the business, excluding affiliate earnings that were not paid out in cash. Assume growth of 10 percent a year for a decade, below the company's 11 to 14 percent record. Assume 5 percent after that, discounted at 10 percent. On those assumptions the business is worth about $80 a share. If the past simply repeats, the figure is about $100. Under pessimistic growth, or at management's 12 percent hurdle rate, value falls into the mid-$50s. Only when both are assumed together does it drop to about $42, below today's price. The central margin of safety is about 40 percent. The stock trades near its 52-week high, but its earnings multiple is about where it was at the end of 1987, so the rise reflects earnings growth rather than speculative excitement. We need no catalyst, because buybacks and earnings growth add to per-share value each year.

We size the position at about 25 percent of the portfolio, short of the 40 percent we would reserve for a case with no open questions. Three things hold us back. Our 1988 owner-earnings figure is an estimate, because the 1988 cash-flow statement is not yet in hand. Roughly three-quarters of operating income is earned abroad, and a weaker dollar has flattered recent growth. Management is willing to borrow up to a 35 percent net debt ratio. We would add toward 40 percent on a price decline if the business holds up. We would reconsider in four cases: if 1988 maintenance spending proves well above depreciation, if growth with currency effects removed turns out to be weak, if long-term interest rates are well above 10 percent, or if management returns to large diversifying acquisitions. We intend to hold the shares indefinitely. A rising price alone would not prompt a sale. A lasting loss of share or margin, or evidence that management is destroying shareholder value, would.
