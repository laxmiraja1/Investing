# Stage 3: Management and capital allocation

The framework's section has three rules:
1. Honest, able management already in place, with candid reporting (`brk-letter-1979`, `brk-letter-1983#2`).
2. Buybacks only at prices well below intrinsic value (`brk-letter-1984`).
3. Each $1 retained should create at least $1 of market value (`brk-letter-1983`).

I also apply the share-issuance test in `brk-letter-1982#17`: issue shares only when you receive as much business value as you give.

---

## 4c. The $1-retained test (taken first, because the other judgments rest on it)

All figures are per share, adjusted for splits, from the `ar-1986` and `ar-1987` selected data, `upi-1989-01-31`, and `prices`.

**Current management's period (end-1980 to today):**

| | Per share |
|---|---|
| Net income 1981–1988 (1.30, 1.32, 1.37, 1.59, 1.84, 2.42, 2.43, 2.85) | $15.12 |
| Cash dividends 1981–1988 | ($7.76) |
| Columbia Pictures Entertainment (CPE) stock paid as an in-kind dividend, at carrying value (1987) | ($0.90) |
| **Earnings retained** | **≈ $6.46** |
| Price, end-1980 → 1989-02-24: $11.13 → $46.56 | **+$35.43** |

That is roughly **$5.50 of market value per $1 retained**.

Part of the gain is the market paying a higher multiple. The price/earnings ratio went from about 9.8x (on 1980 EPS of $1.14) to 16.3x. To remove that effect, hold the multiple at 9.8x: 1988 EPS of $2.85 would then support a price of about $27.90. That is still +$16.80 against $6.46 retained, or about **2.6 : 1**. The test passes even without the re-rating. The per-share gains include the effect of buybacks, which is a legitimate source of per-share value.

**Prior management (end-1976 to end-1980), for contrast:**
- About $1.70 per share was retained. Earnings were $4.17, and dividends were about $2.46. The 1978 dividend is garbled in the scanned table, so these figures are approximate.
- The price fell from $13.17 to $11.13.
- That regime failed the test. Coca-Cola's franchise was in place under both teams, so the difference points to capital allocation.

## 4b. Buybacks: only well below intrinsic value

Shares bought, implied average price, and the multiple of that year's EPS paid (`ar-1986` and `ar-1987` equity statements):

| Year | Shares (M) | Cost ($M) | Avg price | Multiple of EPS |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1984 | 18.4 | 336 | ~$18.24 | 11.5x |
| 1985 | 15.0 | 380 | ~$25.33 | 13.8x |
| 1986 | 2.9 | 110 | ~$38.37 | 15.9x reported; ~18.5x excluding the CCE gain |
| 1987 | 14.2 | 605 | ~$42.61 | 17.5x |
| 1988 | 18.9 | not in packet | est. $38–43, from the 1988 price range | est. 13–15x |

Over 1984–88 the company bought back about 69M shares, roughly 17% of the shares outstanding in 1984.

**What the packet supports:**
- Every repurchase was made below today's $46.56.
- Most of it was done at 11–15x earnings, in a business earning returns of 30% and up on its core assets. That is very likely below intrinsic value. Valuation is the next stage, so I hold the final judgment until then.
- Buying back stock while issuing very little is the kind of behavior `brk-letter-1982#17` says earns a company a better price relative to its value.

**Reservations:**
- Management justifies buybacks as a use of excess cash: they began "after management determined that... available cash and debt capacity exceeded the funds required" (`ar-1986`, `ar-1987`). They do not justify them by comparing price to value.
- The closest they come is "Often the best use of excess cash... has been to invest in our own stock" (`ar-1987`).
- The 1987 purchases averaged about $42–43. The 1987 quarterly highs were $49–53, the year closed at $38.13, and the post-crash low was $29. The packet doesn't say when in 1987 the shares were bought, so I can't tell whether management used the crash.
- Some of the buyback was funded with borrowing. Short-term debt went from $698M to $1,685M in 1987. The framework prefers businesses that use little debt.

## Reinvestment, acquisitions and share issuance

| Capital use | Packet evidence | Assessment |
|---|---|---|
| **Core concentrate business** | Low capital needs. Soft-drink capital spending was about $170M in 1987, against about $1.43B of soft-drink operating income. Pretax returns on identifiable assets were about 34–36%. | Excellent. Retained earnings beyond this business's needs are the real allocation question. |
| **Columbia Pictures / entertainment (bought 1982)** | Earned about $101–109M of net income on about $1.33–1.44B of net assets, roughly **7.5%** (`ar-1987` note 3). Management's own after-tax cost of capital is **12%** (`ar-1986`). Restructured into CPE and 51% spun off to shareholders in 1987. CPE write-downs cut Coca-Cola's equity income by 57% in 1988. | **A mistake by management's own measure.** Films are complex and capital-hungry, the reverse of a simple business. Management reversed it rather than adding more capital, which counts in their favor. |
| **Embassy Communications (1985)** | Paid with about 7.1M treasury shares, worth about $169M at roughly $23.75 a share, plus payment of Embassy's debt. "Recovered virtually all of the investment" through asset sales (`ar-1986`). | Shares were issued at about 13x earnings for an entertainment business. Did shareholders receive as much value as they gave? The packet can't settle it. At best the deal broke even. |
| **Bottlers (CCE, T.C.C. Beverages, Consolidated, Johnston, joint ventures)** | CCE earns about 5.8% on net assets. About $1.8–2.2B of capital sits in affiliates earning mid-single digits. Bottlers bought temporarily (Miami, Rainwater, Memphis) were resold "approximately at carrying value". | The strategic case for strengthening the bottling system that carries the brand is coherent. The direct returns are poor. Moving bottling debt off Coca-Cola's balance sheet also makes the parent's returns look better than the whole system's. |
| **Divestitures** | Presto/Winkler plastics (1985), Ronco pasta (1984), Embassy Home Entertainment (1986), South Africa. | Rational pruning toward the core. Soft drinks now produce more than 95% of operating income (`ar-1987`). |
| **Dividend policy** | Payout ratio cut from 65% (1983) to 58% (1984), 46% (1987), and about 42% (1988, $1.20 / $2.85). Target is 40%. | Retaining more is justified only if the retained money earns well. Given the low-return affiliates, the record is mixed, but the overall $1 test passes clearly. |
| **Leverage policy** | Net debt-to-capital ceiling raised to 35%, "aimed at reducing the weighted average cost of capital". The actual figure is 15.3%. | **A mild concern.** The framework favors businesses that use little or no debt. The appetite for debt is a policy choice, not something the business needs. |

**Share issuance and compensation:**
- Stock options outstanding are about 5.6–6.1M shares, around 1.5% of shares outstanding. Exercises run about 1.1–1.3M shares a year, against buybacks of 15–19M a year.
- There is a restricted stock plan of up to 3M shares and a new 8M-share option plan (1987).
- Performance units on 555,000 shares are tied to the stock price, measured from a $20.63 base.
- Dilution is small, and the buybacks swamp it.
- **Missing:** salaries, bonus formulas, and whether pay is tied to return on capital or only to the stock price. That would be in the proxy statement, which isn't in the packet.

## 4a. Management in place and candid reporting

**Able and in place:**
- Goizueta and Keough have run the business since 1981. M. Douglas Ivester is CFO.
- Their results: core returns and margins rising, US share at a record, and a series of structural moves (CCE, T.C.C. Beverages, CPE, the joint venture with Cadbury Schweppes) that left the parent focused on concentrate.
- The framework cares about management because Berkshire "can't supply it" (`brk-letter-1982`). This team clearly qualifies on ability.

**Candor, judged against `brk-letter-1979` ("We don't expect a public relations document") and `brk-letter-1983#2` (report "the business facts that we would want to know if our positions were reversed"):**

*In their favor:*
- One-time items are named and quantified each year: the $375M CCE gain worth 68¢, the $180M provisions worth 33¢, the $40M T.C.C. gain, and the $36M Foods charge.
- The 1987 letter states its growth figures excluding the unusual items.
- The reports disclose the 63% gross margin on concentrate, the 12% cost of capital, exchange-rate movements by currency, and current-cost caveats.
- They admit problems: "not satisfied with the performance of Columbia Pictures' feature films" (`ar-1986`), and "our foods business is obviously in transition" (`ar-1987`).

*Against:*
- The reports are glossy and promotional. Examples: "The potential appears limitless"; a "$250 billion" contribution to the world economy from a "multiplier factor of five"; slogans about "shareholder value".
- The 1986 letter leads with 41% EPS growth and 28.8% ROE, both inflated by a non-cash gain that is only explained further down.
- The heavy contribution of currency to 1986–88 growth is disclosed, but it isn't put up front as clearly as an owner would want.
- Management issues forecasts ("EPS growth once again well into the mid-teens", `upi-1989-01-31`). The framework gives projections little weight.

**Verdict on candor:** The disclosure is adequate and the numbers are honestly separated, but the tone is that of a public-relations document. The facts an owner needs are present, and the framing around them is promotional.

## What the investor would have relied on that the packet lacks

1. **A personal judgment of Goizueta and Keough:** their character, owner-mindedness, and whether they would welcome a large long-term holder. `brk-letter-1987#18` says the investor buys only into businesses with "high-grade, talented and likeable managers". Only conversation or acquaintance settles that, and the packet has neither.
2. **How management decides to buy back stock:** whether they explicitly compare price to intrinsic value or simply spend surplus cash.
3. **The proxy statement:** executive pay, bonus measures, and insider and board ownership. "Eat our own cooking" (`brk-letter-1983#0`) can't be checked.
4. **Management's intentions for the remaining CCE and CPE stakes and for future diversification**, after the Columbia experience.
5. **1988 buyback detail** (prices and timing) and the 1988 cash-flow statement.

---

## Stage 3 conclusion

**Management passes.**
- The $1-retained test passes decisively since 1981. It failed under the prior team, which shows this management's allocation choices made the difference.
- Buybacks were heavy, mostly at 11–15x earnings, with little offsetting issuance.
- The portfolio is being cut back to the core franchise.

**Reservations to carry forward:**
- The entertainment detour earned below management's own 12% hurdle.
- The bottler affiliates earn low returns.
- Management prefers more debt than the framework likes.
- The reports are promotional in tone.

None of these is disqualifying, because the mistakes are being reversed rather than compounded. Whether today's $46.56 is well below intrinsic value is the next stage, "Valuation".
