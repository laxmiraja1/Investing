# Stage 2: Business analysis

I apply the three rules in "Business analysis" in the framework's order: return on equity, then the moat, then owner earnings.

---

## 3a. Return on equity, not EPS growth, is the primary test

The rule asks for "a high earnings rate on equity capital employed (without undue leverage, accounting gimmickry, etc.)" (`brk-letter-1979`). So I take the reported ROE first, then clean it on both of those points.

**Reported ROE (income from continuing operations / average equity)**, from the `ar-1986` and `ar-1987` selected data and `upi-1989-01-31`:

| 1976–79 | 1980–83 | 1984 | 1985 | 1986 | 1987 | 1988 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 20.3–21.2% | 19.4–19.9% | 21.8% | 23.5% | 28.8% | 27.2% | 33% |

**Adjustment 1: one-time items (accounting).**
- 1986 includes a $375M pretax gain from Coca-Cola Enterprises (CCE) selling its own stock. That added $262M after tax, or 68¢ a share. The year also carried $180M of provisions, which cost $128M, or 33¢.
- Stripping both leaves about $800M of 1986 earnings on average equity of about $3,247M. That is roughly **24.6%**, not 28.8%.
- 1987 has a $40M gain from T.C.C. Beverages selling its stock, against a $36M Foods provision. These roughly cancel, so **27.2%** stands.
- There is a recurring pattern to note. The company buys bottlers, sells 51% of them to the public, and books a gain each time: $67M in 1985, $375M in 1986, $40M in 1987. I treat these gains as non-operating.

**Adjustment 2: leverage and a shrinking equity base.**
- The steady ~20% ROE of 1977–81 was earned with total debt at only 3.5–9% of capital. The business earned about 20% essentially unlevered, while still carrying owned bottlers and the Foods business.
- The rise from 20% toward 33% is partly arithmetic. Treasury stock at cost went from $604M at end-1985 to $1,309M at end-1987. The company bought back another 18.9M shares in 1988. A $335M in-kind dividend of Columbia Pictures Entertainment (CPE) stock also reduced equity. A smaller denominator lifts ROE without the business itself improving.
- Gross debt to capital was 45.6% at end-1987. Net of $1.27B excess cash, $545M of CPE notes (paid in January 1988) and $308M of temporary investments (sold in January 1988), it was 15.3%.
- Interest expense was $279M against $207M of interest income. Operating income of $1,324M covers the $72M of net interest about 18 times. **This is not undue leverage.**
- Off-balance-sheet items: $394M of guarantees, and recourse on $325M of CPE receivables (`ar-1987` note 7).

**Adjustment 3: separate the core business from the drag.**

Pretax segment returns on identifiable assets (`ar-1987` note 16):

| | 1986 (before provisions) | 1987 |
|---|---|---|
| Soft drinks, USA | 293/1,425 = 21% | 324/2,047 = 16%* |
| Soft drinks, International | 888/1,862 = **48%** | 1,109/2,127 = **52%** |
| Soft drinks, combined | **36%** | **34%** |
| Foods (before provisions) | 120/593 = 20% | 103/627 = 16% |

*US assets jumped in 1987. This is probably because bottlers held for resale were parked there. The packet doesn't confirm it.

The equity affiliates earn far less. CCE earned $88M on $1,526M of net assets in 1987, about **5.8%**. Coca-Cola's share was $43M on a $749M carrying value. CPE is carried at $989M, and Coca-Cola's equity income from it fell sharply in 1988 because of CPE's write-downs (`upi-1989-01-31`). Roughly $1.8–2.2B of capital sits in businesses earning mid-single digits.

**So the consolidated ROE understates the core.** The syrup and concentrate business earns about 34–36% pretax on its identifiable assets, which is about 22–24% after tax on assets, not on equity. For comparison, `brk-letter-1987#1` reports that only six of the Fortune 1000 averaged over 30% ROE across the previous decade.

**EPS** is secondary under this rule, but for the record: continuing EPS went from $1.17 in 1981 to $2.85 in 1988, about 13.6% a year, helped by buybacks.

**Verdict on 3a:** This passes clearly. The business earned about 20% unlevered for a decade before the buyback era. The core franchise earns well above that.

## 3b. Durable competitive advantage: the moat around the castle

The rule describes a moat as an advantage competitors can't close, which the owner keeps widening (`brk-letter-1986#16`). GEICO's moat came from low costs. The evidence for Coca-Cola's moat is brand and distribution.

**Pricing power and margins (the strongest test):**
- Gross margin on concentrate sold to US bottlers was about **63%** (`ar-1986`).
- Consolidated gross margin, on the restated basis, rose from 50.5% (1985) to 50.5% (1986) to 52.6% (1987). In 1988 it was about 55% ($4.6B on $8.3B, with the UPI story's "$4.6 million" read as billion).
- Net margin, excluding one-time items, rose from 11.5% to 11.5% to 12.0% in 1985–87.
- The statutory tax rate fell from 46% to 40% to 34%, and margins widened over the same period. Under the reasoning in `brk-letter-1986#22`, a strong unregulated franchise keeps the tax benefit rather than passing it to customers. The margin data is consistent with that.
- Management states it can "adjust prices to compensate for increasing costs" (`ar-1986`, `ar-1987`). It used new contract rights to raise prices to CCE in October 1986.
- **Caveat:** the standard contracts with most other US bottlers set Coca-Cola syrup prices by formula (`ar-1986`). That limits US pricing freedom outside CCE.

**Share and scale:**
- US share was over 40% in 1988, nine points ahead of the main competitor in 1986.
- Fountain share was 59%, more than a 2-to-1 lead, with "nearly three-fourths" of the segment's growth over four years.
- Five of the top ten US soft drinks belong to the company.
- Worldwide share is about 44% (excluding the USSR and China), and volume is double that of the nearest competitor (`ar-1987`).

**Resilience:** The 1985 change to the Coke formula was the company's own mistake. Within a year, US share reached a record of more than 39% and Coca-Cola classic became the best-selling soft drink (`ar-1986`). A franchise that survives its owner's blunder has a deep moat.

**Runway, with growth that needs little capital:**
- International per-capita consumption is **16% of the US rate** (`ar-1986`).
- International markets are 65% of volume and about 77% of soft-drink operating income (`ar-1987`).
- International operating income rose 21% in 1988.
- US per-capita consumption rose from 203 servings in 1980 to 274 in 1987.
- Soft-drink capital spending in 1987 was about $170M, against about $1.43B of soft-drink operating income. That is the reverse of the Burlington case in `brk-letter-1985#9`, where heavy capital spending was needed just to keep up.

**Cost of keeping the moat:** Media advertising was $720M in 1987, about 9% of revenues. Cooperative advertising for bottlers ran at 19–21% of the related revenues. This is a recurring cost, and I treat it as an operating expense, not a sign of weakness.

**Threats in the packet:**
- **Currency:** About 74% of operating income is earned outside the US. Key hard currencies strengthened 27%, 16% and 10% against the dollar in 1986–88. Part of the reported growth is translation, not more drinks sold.
- **Latin America:** hyperinflationary economies, low margins.
- **Bottler health:** The moat depends partly on independent bottlers, and the bottlers the company owns stakes in earn about 6%.
- **Foods:** Orange juice volume fell 22% (frozen) and 6% (chilled) in 1988.

**Verdict on 3b:** The moat is wide, durable and widening. Expanding margins, record share and the recovery from the 1985 formula change are the best evidence.

## 3c. Owner earnings

The definition (`brk-letter-1986`) is reported earnings, plus depreciation, amortization and other non-cash charges, minus *average maintenance* capital spending.

**1987, the last full cash-flow statement in the packet (`ar-1987`):**

| | $M |
|---|---|
| Net income | 916 |
| + Depreciation and goodwill amortization | 153 |
| + Restructuring provision (non-cash) | 36 |
| − Gain on T.C.C. Beverages stock sale (non-cash) | (40) |
| − Equity income not received in cash | (80) |
| − Deferred tax reversal | (29) |
| **Subtotal before capital spending** | **~956** |
| − Capital spending: depreciation as a proxy for maintenance ($152M) or total capex ($300M) | |
| **Owner earnings** | **~$660–805M** |

I exclude the undistributed affiliate earnings. `brk-letter-1981#3` credits retained earnings only when they sit in good businesses, and CCE and CPE are earning about 6%. The $273M release of working capital in 1987 is outside the definition, and I leave it out.

**1988 estimate:** The packet has no 1988 cash-flow statement. Net income was $1,040M. Equity income fell sharply, so less non-cash income needs removing. I assume depreciation of about $160M and maintenance capital spending close to depreciation. On those assumptions, owner earnings come to about **$900M–$1.0B**, or about **$2.45–2.75 per share** on roughly 365M average shares (implied by $1,040M ÷ $2.85).

Owner earnings are probably close to reported earnings. Much of the capital spending is growth (international coolers, vending machines and plant), which the definition doesn't deduct.

## Facts the framework would want that the packet lacks

1. **The 1988 annual report and 10-K:** the cash-flow statement, capital spending, depreciation, segment returns, year-end balance sheet and debt, and the size and price of the 1988 buybacks. My 1988 owner-earnings figure is an estimate built on these gaps.
2. **The split of capital spending into maintenance and growth.** This is the central input to owner earnings, and it isn't disclosed.
3. **Volume and profit growth with currency effects removed.** Without it, I can't separate real growth from translation.
4. **The competitor's economics:** its margins and returns on capital, to measure how wide the moat is.
5. **Bottler contract terms by market:** how much US pricing is fixed by formula, and for how long.
6. **The current market value of the CCE and CPE stakes.** The packet gives only the end-1987 note that public investees were worth $44M more than carrying value, and the end-1986 CCE price of $14.25.

**Stage 2 conclusion:** The core concentrate business clears all three tests with room to spare. It earns high returns without needing leverage, has a moat that is widening, and its owner earnings are close to its reported earnings. The weaknesses are in the low-return affiliates and in the currency effects on reported growth. I'll take up management's use of capital (buybacks, affiliate investments, and the dollar-retained test) under "Management & capital allocation," and price under "Valuation."
