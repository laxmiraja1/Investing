**Company A (AAA): Recommendation to buy, about 25% of equity holdings, as of February 27, 1989**

Company A sells soft drink syrup and concentrate to bottlers and fountain operators in more than 155 countries. Since its entertainment business was moved into a 49%-owned affiliate in 1987, the concentrate operation has produced more than 95% of operating income. The company's brands account for about 44% of soft drinks sold worldwide outside the Soviet Union and China, roughly double the volume of the nearest competitor. Its US share has risen from 39% in 1986 to over 40% in 1988, and it outsells its main rival two to one in fountain accounts. A juice business and a set of minority stakes in bottlers and entertainment make up the rest. The juice business is weak, with frozen orange juice volume down 22% last year, and too small to affect the conclusion. The product itself has changed little in a century, and that is why the economics can be judged ten years out.

The company passes each gate the framework applies. It is not a turnaround. Return on average equity was between 19% and 22% every year from 1976 to 1984. In that period debt was mostly under 10% of capital, so those returns were not produced by borrowing. Returns then rose to 27% in 1987, and the company reports 33% for 1988. Part of the recent rise is arithmetic, because buybacks and a stock dividend have shrunk the equity base. The underlying level is still very high: about 25% on equity plus net debt in 1987. The soft drink segments earned $1.43 billion of operating income on $4.2 billion of gross assets.

The source of those returns is a franchise that gives the company pricing power. Gross margin on concentrate sold to US bottlers is about 63%. The company raised prices to its largest bottler at its own discretion in 1986. In Latin America it grew 1988 operating income 17% on 1% volume growth by widening margins. The franchise also survived the company's own 1985 formula change: the original formula was restored and became the best-selling soft drink in the country the following year. The room to grow is large. Consumption per person outside the United States is about one-sixth of the US level.

Reported earnings are close to what owners could actually take out. In 1987 operations produced about $959 million of working capital against $152 million of depreciation and $300 million of capital spending. A good part of that spending was expansion abroad. After allowing for maintenance spending and stripping out non-cash items, owner earnings were roughly 90% of core profits.

Management is acceptable but not spotless. Reporting is candid in substance. Management separates out one-time gains and charges, discloses how much a weaker dollar helped results, and states plainly that its profits would be lower measured at current costs. Since 1984 it has retired about 69 million shares, about 17% of the count, and it bought most heavily after the 1987 crash, when the price was lowest. About $2.6 billion of retained earnings since 1981 is matched by market value that has risen from about $4.3 billion to about $16.5 billion. That is roughly $1.90 of value per dollar retained even after removing the stock's re-rating.

The blemishes are real. In 1982 and 1985 the company issued stock to buy television and film businesses that earned mediocre returns. It has since partly reversed those purchases. It also raised its debt ceiling to 35% of capital, using cost-of-capital reasoning rather than price-versus-value reasoning. And it is putting more capital into bottler stakes that show low accounting returns. We have not met management, and we would want to before the position is complete.

On value, we estimate normalized 1988 owner earnings from the core business at about $870 million, or $2.45 per share. That figure removes the equity income from affiliates and trims about 7% for the currency tailwind. We deduct the reinvestment needed to support growth and add the equity stakes net of debt. On that basis and at a 9% discount rate, the shares are worth about $73 if earnings grow 10% a year for a decade, a rate below the company's eleven-year record of about 12%. They are worth about $50 even if growth falls to the rate of inflation.

At $46.56, the price is about 64% of the base-case value and is covered even in the no-growth case. The market is pricing the company roughly as though it will stop growing in real terms. We see no need for a catalyst; over time the share price should follow the business results.

The case depends most on one input we do not have, the long-term government bond rate. If 12% is the right rate to discount at, which is the company's own stated cost of capital, the base-case value falls to about $44 and the margin of safety disappears. That uncertainty, along with not having met management, is why we recommend about 25% of equity holdings rather than the larger concentration the framework allows when the facts are entirely clear.

We would reconsider on any of the following: interest rates well above 9%; 1988 statements showing heavier capital needs, debt or guarantees than we assumed; constant-currency results revealing only single-digit growth; or a meeting that shows management buys back stock without regard to price, or wants to diversify again. We would sell if the franchise loses share or pricing power over a sustained period, if management issues stock or borrows heavily for businesses outside the core, or if its reporting stops being candid. A higher share price alone would not be a reason to sell. A business like this is one to hold for as long as the economics and the people stay as they are.
