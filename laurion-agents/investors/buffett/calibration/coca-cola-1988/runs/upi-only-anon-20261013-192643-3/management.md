# Stage 3: Management and capital allocation (Framework §4)

The packet covers 1981 to 1988, which is the whole tenure of "our present management team," assembled in 1981 (`ar-1987` letter). The three §4 rules are taken in order, followed by acquisitions, leverage and compensation, which the rules bear on.

## 4.1 Honest, able management and candid reporting

The test is whether the reports tell owners the facts they would want, not "a public relations document" (`brk-letter-1979`, `brk-letter-1983#2`).

**Evidence of candor:**
- **Unusual items are separated out by management itself.** The 1987 letter states that "excluding 35 cents per share of unusual items in 1986, operating income climbed 23 percent, while net income gained 14.5 percent." The $375M and $40M bottler-IPO gains and the $180M and $36M provisions are quantified, with their effect on EPS (`ar-1986`, `ar-1987` MD&A).
- **The currency tailwind is disclosed.** Key currencies rose 27% in 1986 and 16% in 1987 against the dollar, with a table by country. The 1988 release adds a 10% figure (`upi-1989-01-31`).
- **Inflation is disclosed against interest.** Management says current-cost income "would be less than reported." It also says the purchasing-power gains from net monetary liabilities "do not represent an increase in funds available for distribution to shareholders and do not necessarily imply that incurring more debt would be beneficial." That is the kind of statement a self-promoting management would leave out.
- **Failures are admitted.** In 1986: "we were not satisfied with the performance of the film studio's feature films," and leadership was changed. In 1987: "Our foods business is obviously in transition," with a new head and a charge.

**Evidence against:**
- **The 1987 report reads partly as a promotional document.** Examples are the "multiplier factor of five" producing "$250 billion to the world economy," a "value-creation process" diagram, and repeated "shareholder value" language, which the framework distrusts when it is said rather than shown (`brk-letter-1984#4`: "this season's favorite").
- **Restatements are frequent.** Each year's figures are restated as businesses are deconsolidated. The 1986 revenue figure fell from $8,669M to $6,977M. Net income is unaffected and the restatements are disclosed, but a reader has to work to follow the business.
- **Obligations sit off the balance sheet.** Bottler debt has been moved into 49%-owned affiliates (CCE: $2.7B of liabilities). The parent guarantees $394M of others' debt and $325M of the entertainment affiliate's receivables sold with recourse. All of this is disclosed, so this is a matter of complexity rather than concealment.

**Verdict:** the substance is candid even where the tone is not. The facts I needed in Stage 2 to strip out one-time items, currency and inflation came from management's own disclosures. This passes, with a note on tone.

## 4.2 Buybacks only well below intrinsic value (`brk-letter-1984#4`)

**The record (`ar-1986`, `ar-1987` cash-flow and equity statements; 1988 from `upi-1989-01-31`):**

| Year | Shares bought | Cost | Avg price | EPS that year | Price / EPS |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1984 | 18.4M | $336M | ~$18.25 | $1.57 | ~11.6x |
| 1985 | 15.0M | $380M | ~$25.30 | $1.72 | ~14.7x |
| 1986 | 2.9M | $110M | ~$38.40 | ~$2.07 ex-items | ~18.5x |
| 1987 | 14.2M | $605M | ~$42.60 | $2.43 | ~17.5x |
| 1988 | 18.9M | not given | est. $37–43 | $2.85 | est. ~13–15x |

Since 1984, the company has retired about 69M shares, roughly 17% of the 1983 average share count.

**Assessment:**
- **1984–85 clearly pass.** Stage 2 put owner earnings at roughly 90% of reported earnings and growing in the mid-teens, with returns on capital around 25%+. Paying 11–15x reported earnings for such a business was buying "$2 of present value for $1."
- **1986–87 are less clear.** At about 17–18x, the purchases are below value only if the franchise keeps compounding as Stage 2 suggests. Whether they were *well* below value depends on the Stage 4 valuation.
- **1988 is encouraging.** The buyback sped up to 18.9M shares in the year *after* the October 1987 break, when the stock traded at $35–45 and the multiple had compressed. More buying at lower prices is the behavior the rule describes.

**The stated rationale is a flag.** Repurchases "were initiated after management determined that the Company's available cash and debt capacity would exceed the funds required" (`ar-1986` MD&A). Management frames buybacks as the use of *surplus* cash, not as purchases made *because* price is below value. It also measures everything against a 12% "cost of capital." This is the right action, but the reasoning given is not the framework's. Price discipline has only been shown in one direction: they bought more when the stock was cheaper.

**Verdict:** the buyback programme is large and sustained, with the heaviest buying at the lowest multiples. That is a "demonstrated" pro-owner record in the sense of `brk-letter-1984#4`. It passes, with the final judgment on 1986–87 deferred to Stage 4.

## 4.3 Each $1 retained must create at least $1 of market value (`brk-letter-1983`)

**Retained earnings, 1982–1988:**
- Net income: 512 + 559 + 629 + 722 + 934 + 916 + 1,044 ≈ **$5.3B**.
- Cash dividends: about **$2.7B**. Figures for 1984–87 are taken from the statements; 1982, 1983 and 1988 are estimated from dividends per share times average shares.
- Retained: about **$2.6B**. Of this, about $2.2B was then spent on buybacks and $0.34B paid out as the in-kind entertainment dividend.

**Market value:**
- End-1981: $11.58 × 372M shares ≈ **$4.3B**.
- Now: about 354.8M shares (372.4M at end-1987, less 18.9M bought, plus about 1.3M option exercises; my estimate) × $46.56 ≈ **$16.5B**. The packet's $17.34B uses the end-1987 share count and overstates this. Adding the in-kind dividend at carrying value ($0.34B) gives about **$16.9B**.

**The test:**
- **Crude:** about $12.6B gained in market value on about $2.6B retained, roughly **$4.80 per $1**. This holds even though about $2.2B of the "retained" money has already left the company through buybacks, which lowers today's market value.
- **Excluding re-rating:** the stock has moved from about 8.9x to about 16.3x earnings. At the 1981 multiple, today's EPS of $2.85 implies about $25.40 per share, a value of about $9.0B plus the $0.3B in-kind dividend. The gain is still about $5.0B on $2.6B retained, roughly **$1.90 per $1**.

**Verdict:** this passes, and passes even with no help from the market's re-rating.

**Forward-looking caution:** the payout target is falling from about 65% (1983) to 40%, so much more will be retained in future. That is good if retained cash goes back into the concentrate business, which earns 25–30%. Recent non-buyback reinvestment has gone mainly into bottler equity: CCE, the Canadian bottler, Consolidated and Johnston ($117M), the British joint venture, and the "temporary" Miami, Rainwater and Memphis purchases that were resold to CCE at carrying value. On book figures these earn about 5–6%: equity income was $118M on about $2.26B of affiliate investments, and CCE earned $88M on $1.5B of net assets. Management argues these stakes support concentrate volume, which would not show up in equity income. That argument is plausible, but the packet cannot verify it. This is where I would watch future retained dollars most closely.

## Acquisitions and share issuance (`brk-letter-1982#17`)

This is the weakest part of the record.
- **1982 film studio purchase.** It was made partly with stock. Average shares rose from 372M (1981) to 390M (1982) and 408M (1983). Issuing roughly 10% of a business earning about 20% on equity at about 9–13x earnings, in order to buy a film studio, gave away franchise value for a business outside the core. That is the "double whammy" pattern the framework warns about.
- **1985 Embassy acquisition.** Also paid partly in stock (7.1M pre-split shares, about $267M).
- **Entertainment results afterwards.** The segment's operating income grew more than 30% a year. It still earned only about 11% pre-tax on $2.17B of identifiable assets in 1986. A $100M restructuring followed in 1987, and 1988 write-downs cut equity income 57%.

**The correction counts for something.** Management sold the plastics businesses (1985, $112M) and pasta (1984). It combined entertainment into a separate company, distributed part of it to shareholders, and kept 49%. It also repurchased about 69M shares, more than undoing the roughly 36M shares issued in 1982–83 and the shares issued for Embassy. In its own words, the focus is now "first and foremost a marketer of soft drinks." This is a management that made a diversification mistake and is unwinding it, which is better than one that compounds it. It is not a clean record.

## Leverage policy

In 1986 management raised its net debt-to-capital target to 35%, explicitly to lower "the weighted average cost of capital." In 1987 it borrowed about $1.2B short-term, largely to fund buybacks and bottler stakes. Net debt is about 15% of capital, and the business could carry far more. The concern is the direction and the language: borrowing to raise reported returns is what the "without undue leverage" rule in `brk-letter-1979` guards against. Two facts limit the concern:
- the business does not need the debt;
- net interest expense fell 52% in 1988 (`upi-1989-01-31`).

This is a watch item, not a disqualifier.

## Compensation

- **Option grants:** 1.1–1.8M per year on about 372M shares, roughly 0.3–0.5% a year. Exercises are more than offset by buybacks.
- **Restricted stock:** a 3M-share plan.
- **Performance units:** 555,000 performance units that pay cash based on the share price above $20.63.

Pay is tied to *share price*, not per-share intrinsic value. That rewards re-rating as well as business progress. The dilution is modest. The packet does not show executive pay levels or insider ownership.

## What the investor would have relied on that the packet lacks

1. **Meeting and judging the people.** The framework puts heavy weight on joining "high-grade, talented and likeable managers" (`brk-letter-1987#18`) and on evaluating "the quality of the people we will be joining" rather than inspecting plants (`brk-letter-1986#10`). Nothing here substitutes for knowing how the chairman and president think about price versus value in buybacks, about the debt target, and about avoiding a repeat of the entertainment detour.
2. **Insider and director ownership** (the "we eat our own cooking" test, `brk-letter-1983#0`) and the proxy statement's compensation details.
3. **1988 buyback prices and dollars**, plus any statement of management's own view of intrinsic value.
4. **How the 1982 stock issuance was decided** and whether management now regards it as a mistake.
5. **Director relationships.** The board includes bankers and an investment banker, and the packet does not show whether any have business ties to the company.
6. **Economics of the bottler investments** apart from equity income: whether the concentrate volume they generate justifies the capital put into them.

## Stage 3 conclusion

On balance, management passes §4:
- reporting is candid in substance;
- a large, sustained buyback programme has been bought hardest when the stock was cheapest;
- retained earnings have passed the $1-for-$1 test by about 2–5×.

The early record is weaker. In 1982–85 management issued stock and spent capital outside the core. It has since been reversing that, and the stated reasoning, cost of capital and "excess cash," is corporate-finance vocabulary rather than the framework's price-versus-value discipline.

Three points carry into Stage 4:
1. whether today's price is well enough below value that continued buybacks at about 16x add value;
2. how much of the planned higher retention goes into low-yielding bottler stakes;
3. the leverage policy.

A direct judgment of the people, which the framework treats as essential, is the most important missing input.
