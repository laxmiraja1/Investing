**Memo: Purchase of Company A common stock, February 27, 1989**

We recommend buying shares of Company A at about $46.56, building toward roughly a quarter of the portfolio over time. This memo explains what the business is, why it passes our tests, what we think it is worth, and what would make us change course.

Company A's core business is simple. It makes soft-drink concentrate and syrup and sells them to about a thousand bottlers, which produce and distribute the finished drinks in more than 160 countries. Soft drinks account for over 90% of operating income. A juice business makes up most of the remainder. Over the past three years the company moved its large US and Canadian bottling operations and its entertainment business into separate public companies, keeping 49% of each. What is left is mainly a brand and a supply arrangement. No technology needs to be understood, and the economics depend on a few things we can follow: case volume, per-capita consumption abroad, concentrate margins, and the health of the bottlers.

The business passes every screen we use. It is not a turnaround: earnings per share from continuing operations rose every year from $0.84 in 1977 to $2.85 in 1988. Return on average equity was above 20% in every year since 1978. It was already above 20% in 1978 to 1981, when debt was under a tenth of capital, so the high returns are not a product of borrowing. The reported 33% for 1988 does overstate things. Management has borrowed to buy back stock, and if we remove that effect the company earns about 25% on all the capital it employs. That figure includes $1.9 billion sitting in bottling and entertainment stakes that earn very little. The concentrate business by itself earns about 44% pretax on the assets it uses.

The competitive position explains those returns and is getting stronger. Worldwide share of soft-drink sales rose from under 38% in 1980 to nearly 45% in 1988, more than twice the nearest competitor. Gross margins rose from about 50% to nearly 56% over the last three years. Soft-drink capital spending is only about a seventh of soft-drink operating income, so most of what the business earns is available to owners. There is also a long way to grow: outside the US, people drink about 51 servings a year of the company's products, against about 274 in the US. The advantage costs money to keep. Media advertising reached $842 million last year. The company also relies on bottlers that earn modest returns and that it must sometimes support with capital.

Management has been in place since 1981 and has been sensible with shareholders' money where it counts. It has bought back about 69 million shares since 1984, cutting the average share count by about 8%. Each dollar of earnings kept in the business since 1981 has been matched by about five dollars of market value. Even if the stock's P/E had not risen, it would be about $2.60 per dollar retained. The weaker part of the record is the 1982 to 1987 move into entertainment. Management has since largely unwound it, recovering $545 million in cash and leaving a minority stake. The annual reports are more promotional than we would like, but the facts that matter, including one-time items and disappointments, are disclosed. We have not seen the proxy statement or met management, and we would want to do both before reaching full size.

Valuation is where the case is least comfortable. By our old standard of buying below net working capital, the stock fails badly: the price is more than forty times working capital per share. That rule was built for cheap, mediocre businesses. For an outstanding business, the question is whether the price makes sense if the future looks roughly like the past.

We estimate the cash the business actually generates for owners at $2.39 to $2.88 per share in 1988. This is net income plus depreciation and other non-cash charges, less the capital spending needed to sustain the business. At today's price that is a yield of 5 to 6%. That is below the roughly 9% we assume long-term government bonds pay (the packet does not give a bond yield), so the case depends on growth.

The record supports growth of 11 to 13% a year per share. That is 10.9% over 1978 to 1988, and about 13% in local currency once buybacks are included. On those rates, valuing the business at 12 to 15 times earnings ten years out, and adding the affiliate stakes at book value, we arrive at roughly $56 to $76 a share. That is a margin of safety of about 17 to 39% and an expected return of about 9.5 to 13.5% a year. If growth slows to 8% and the foreign currencies that helped recent results weaken again, value is about the current price. The cushion therefore comes from the durability of the franchise, not from the balance sheet.

That is why we propose a large position but not the largest. The franchise is about as unlikely to be badly damaged as any we know. But three things keep us from the maximum. About three-quarters of operating income is earned abroad. The margin of safety is moderate. Several facts are still missing, including how capital spending splits between maintenance and growth, and how much stock the directors own. We would buy steadily and add more heavily on weakness; in 1988 the stock traded between $35 and $40. No particular event is needed for the investment to work. If per-share value keeps compounding, the market price should eventually follow.

Several things would change our view. Evidence that most capital spending is maintenance rather than growth would lower the earnings owners can count on. So would local-currency growth well below 10%, or bond yields well above 9%. On the business side, we would reconsider if worldwide share or concentrate margins started to fall, if management again moved large amounts of capital outside soft drinks, or if it issued stock or borrowed beyond its stated limits to support earnings per share. A high stock price alone would not be a reason to sell. We intend to own this business for a very long time.
