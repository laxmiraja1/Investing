# Stage 3: Management and capital allocation

The framework has three rules here (section 4). I take them in order:
1. Honest, able management already in place, with candid reporting.
2. Buybacks only well below intrinsic value.
3. Each $1 retained must create at least $1 of market value.

I then cover two areas the question asks about that the rules touch only indirectly: acquisitions and compensation.

---

## 4a. Honest, able management in place; candid reporting

**In place and proven.** The chairman/CEO and president put together the current team in 1981 and wrote a "Strategy for the 1980s" (`ar-1987`, `ar-1988`). Over 1981–88:
- EPS rose from $1.30 to $2.85.
- ROE rose from about 22% to 33%, or about 25% without leverage (Stage 2).
- Worldwide share rose from under 38% to about 45%.

These are the same people who would run the business after a purchase. The framework's requirement that management already be in place (`brk-letter-1982`) is met.

**Ability: the core business.** They have been consistently sound with the concentrate business. They focused on it, simplified the US system by moving from syrup to concentrate (over 89% concentrate in 1988), pushed international availability, and raised margins in Latin America. The framework judges managers by the returns they earn on the capital they run (`brk-letter-1987#1`). On the core business, that record is strong.

**Ability: outside the core.** Here the record is mixed:
- They bought the film studio in 1982 and Embassy/Tandem in 1985. They then folded entertainment into a 49%-owned affiliate in 1987.
- Foods needed a restructuring charge in 1987. Coffee was sold in January 1989.
- The 1985 change to the formula was reversed within the year by bringing back the original as "classic." That reads as a costly mistake that was corrected quickly, though the packet says little about it.

The pattern since 1986 is a retreat to the core. That matches the framework's preference for "a good business… at a fair price" over effort spent on weaker ones (`brk-letter-1979`).

**Candor.** The framework does not expect "a public relations document" (`brk-letter-1979`). It asks for "the business facts that we would want to know if our positions were reversed" (`brk-letter-1983#2`).

*Against:* the reports are heavily promotional.
- "The potential appears limitless" (`ar-1986`).
- A "multiplier factor of five" turning $50B of system sales into "$250B to the world economy" (`ar-1987`).
- Anecdotes about hospital prescriptions (`ar-1988`).
- The phrase "increasing shareholder value" appears throughout. The framework warns that this kind of talk counts only when it is "demonstrated" (`brk-letter-1984#4`).
- The 1986 letter opens with 41% EPS growth before explaining that the CCE stock-sale gain is part of it.

*In favor:* the essential facts are disclosed, including unfavorable ones.
- EPS shown excluding unusual items in both directions; the 1987 letter states that reported net income fell (`ar-1987`).
- "We were not satisfied with the performance of the film studio's feature films" (`ar-1986`); "our foods business is obviously in transition" (`ar-1987`).
- Stated cost of capital (12% after tax), a net-debt ceiling (35%), and exact repurchase counts and costs.
- CCE pricing terms and cooperative-advertising payments to CCE.
- A possible $175M equity reduction from the new tax-accounting rule (FASB 96), and an admission that current-cost earnings would be lower than reported.

**Verdict.** The tone is promotional, but nothing essential is hidden. On candor the reports pass, though not by a wide margin.

## 4b. Buybacks only well below intrinsic value

**The record** (treasury stock purchased, from the equity statements in `ar-1986`, `ar-1987`, `ar-1988`):

| Year | Shares (M) | Cost ($M) | Average price | P/E on that year's EPS |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1984 | 18.4 | 335.7 | ~$18.2 | ~11.5x |
| 1985 | 15.0 | 379.9 | ~$25.3 | ~13.8x |
| 1986 | 2.9 | 110.5 | ~$38.4 | ~18.5x on EPS excluding one-time items ($2.07) |
| 1987 | 14.2 | 605.1 | ~$42.6 | ~17.5x |
| 1988 | 19.0 | 758.2 | ~$39.9 | ~14.0x |
| **Total** | **~69.5** | **~2,189** | | |

Average shares fell from 396M (1984) to 364.6M (1988). From 1985 to 1988, net income grew about 15.3% a year and EPS about 18.3% a year. Roughly 3 points a year of per-share growth came from shrinking the share count.

**Measured against the rule:**
- **Large and sustained.** These are "major repurchases" by any measure, about 17% of the 1984 share count. The framework names this as the clearest evidence a manager favors shareholders over expanding his own domain (`brk-letter-1984#4`).
- **The stated reason is not price versus value.** Management repurchases after determining "available cash and debt capacity exceeded the funds required" and "as long as investing in our own stock remains one of the best uses of our excess cash" (`ar-1988`). The framework's test is the gap between price and intrinsic value. Management's test is surplus cash measured against a 12% cost of capital. These may lead to the same decisions, but the packet never shows management comparing price with intrinsic value.
- **Partly paid for with debt.** Short-term debt rose about $1.2B in 1987, "due principally to the funding of the share repurchase program" and bottler stakes (`ar-1987`). Coverage is ample: 1988 operating income of $1,598M against $230M of interest expense. But it is the elective leverage flagged in Stage 1.
- **An inconsistency with issuing stock.** In 1985 the company issued about 7.1M treasury shares toward the roughly $267M Embassy purchase, at about $23.7 per share on the treasury-stock entries. In the same year it bought back 15M shares at about $25.3. Issuing stock below the price at which you are buying it back is the opposite of applying "the same yardstick in the measurement of both businesses" (`brk-letter-1982#17`). The amount is small, but it suggests the price-versus-value discipline was not applied consistently in 1985.

**Verdict (provisional).** Whether the 1987–88 purchases at about $40–43 were "well below" intrinsic value depends on the Stage 5 valuation. Against owner earnings of about $2.3–$2.9 per share (Stage 2) growing in the mid-teens, they look sensible. They do not look obviously deep-discount. The earlier purchases at $18–25 (1984–85) are the clearer cases.

## 4c. Each $1 retained must create at least $1 of market value

The test runs "over time" (`brk-letter-1983#0`, cited as `brk-letter-1983`). I measure it on a per-share basis from 1 January 1981, when the current team started, to 31 December 1988, using the restated data in `ar-1988`.

| Item | Per share |
|---|---|
| Net income, 1981–88 (1.30 + 1.32 + 1.37 + 1.59 + 1.84 + 2.42 + 2.43 + 2.85) | $15.12 |
| Dividends, 1981–88: cash $7.76 plus the $0.90 in-kind entertainment-affiliate distribution | $8.66 |
| **Retained** (including what was spent on buybacks) | **$6.46** |
| Market price: $11.13 (end-1980) to $44.63 (end-1988) | **+$33.50** |
| **Market value per $1 retained** | **~$5.2** |

**Stripping out the rerating.** Part of the gain comes from the market paying more per dollar of earnings: about 9.8x 1980 EPS ($11.13 / $1.14) versus 15.7x in 1988. At an unchanged 9.8x, the 1988 price would be about $27.9. The gain would then be about $16.8, or **about $2.6 per $1 retained**. The test still passes.

**Caveat.** Most of the value created comes from the moat in the concentrate business, which needed little of the retained capital. The retained dollars went mainly to three places:
- buybacks, about $2.2B since 1984
- the move into entertainment
- bottler stakes earning about 8% or less

The overall test passes comfortably. The best returns came from the core and from buybacks, not from acquisitions.

## Compensation (from the notes only)

- **Options.** Options outstanding rose from 5.6M to **8.66M** in 1988, with 4.18M granted that year compared with 1.1–1.8M in earlier years. That is about 2.4% of shares outstanding. The new 1987 plan covers 8M shares.
- **Restricted stock.** 511,500 shares were granted in 1988, against 178,500 in 1987. They vest on death, disability or retirement, and now also on a "change of control." They are forfeited only on departure, so the condition is tenure, not performance. Unearned restricted stock stands at $51.5M.
- **Cash units.** There are cash awards tied to the stock price: 555,000 performance units with a $20.63 base (1985) and 300,000 incentive units (1988).
- **Assessment.** Pay is linked to the share price, not to returns on capital. The 1988 jump in grants and the new change-of-control vesting clause are worth looking into. Dilution so far is modest compared with the buybacks.

## What the investor would have relied on that the packet lacks

1. **Meeting management directly**, mainly the chairman/CEO and president. The aim would be to judge how they think about price versus value in buybacks, and about future acquisitions outside the core.
2. **The proxy statement**: salaries, bonus formulas, the terms of the 1988 option and restricted-stock grants, and **insider and director ownership**. The framework's owner-orientation (`brk-letter-1983#0`) would ask whether directors "eat their own cooking." The packet says nothing about this.
3. **The price paid for the film studio in 1982**, and the full return on the entertainment move.
4. **The views of people who know the managers**: bottlers, past counterparties, other directors. The framework uses this kind of reputational check for acquisitions (`brk-letter-1986#10`).
5. **Management's own estimate of intrinsic value**, if they have one, to test the buyback rule directly.
6. **The large holders' positions** (for example, the late former chairman's family or foundation), which may affect the board's independence.

## Stage 3 conclusion

| Rule | Result |
|---|---|
| Honest, able management in place; candid reporting | **Pass.** Strong in the core business. A history of moves outside the core, now being reversed. Promotional tone, but the facts are disclosed. |
| Buybacks only well below intrinsic value | **Provisional pass.** Large and sustained. The justification is surplus cash, not explicitly price versus value. Partly debt-funded. The 1985 stock-issue inconsistency is minor. Final judgment depends on the Stage 5 valuation. |
| $1 retained creates at least $1 of market value | **Pass.** About $5.2 per $1, and about $2.6 per $1 at an unchanged P/E. |

Taken together, management is in place, focused on the core business, and returns capital in volume. Two things are left open: the price discipline of its buybacks and the pay design. Neither rules the company out. Both would be checked in person and in the proxy before buying.
