# Stage 4: Valuation

All figures are in $ millions unless shown per share. Price on the as-of date is **$46.56** (close on 1989-02-24, `prices`), against 354.8M shares outstanding.

---

## 5. Valuation: the framework's stated rule

The framework's Valuation section has one rule: buy below net working capital (`bpl-letter-1965`).

| 1988 year-end (`ar-1988`) | $M | Per share |
|---|---|---|
| Current assets | 3,245.4 | |
| Current liabilities | (2,868.9) | |
| **Net working capital** | **376.5** | **$1.06** |
| Current assets less *all* liabilities ($4,105.3) | (859.9) | negative |
| Common book value ($3,045.3) | | $8.58 |
| **Price** | | **$46.56** |

**Result: fails, by a wide margin.** The price is about 44 times net working capital per share and 5.4 times book value. Read literally, the framework's valuation rule excludes this stock.

**Why that is not the end of the analysis.** The 1965 rule is a test for the "generals" category of statistically cheap stocks. By the as-of date the investor's own writing had replaced it for high-quality businesses:
- "Our goal is to find an outstanding business at a sensible price, not a mediocre business at a bargain price." The passage goes on to describe searching for "bargains" as a twenty-year mistake (`brk-letter-1987#18`).
- "We may very well pay a fairly fancy price for a Category 1 business if we are reasonably confident of what we are getting." Purchases should be "at prices that will make sense, even should the future of the acquired enterprise develop much along the lines of its past" (`brk-letter-1981#3`).
- Projections are "of little interest" (`brk-letter-1982`).

So the test this process would actually apply in February 1989 is this: **using owner earnings (section 3), and assuming only that the past continues, is $46.56 a sensible price?** I apply that test below. It is not a generic DCF built on management forecasts. Management's guidance of "mid-teens" EPS growth for 1989 (`upi-1989-01-31`) appears only as an upper reference point.

## 5a. Owner-earnings yield at today's price

From Stage 2, owner earnings are about $2.39–$2.88 per share, with a midpoint of about **$2.60**.

| Measure | Per share | Multiple of price | Yield |
|---|---|---|---|
| Owner earnings, conservative | $2.39 | 19.5x | 5.1% |
| Owner earnings, midpoint | $2.60 | 17.9x | 5.6% |
| Owner earnings, if maintenance capital spending ≈ depreciation | $2.88 | 16.2x | 6.2% |
| Reported 1988 EPS | $2.85 | 16.3x | 6.1% |
| 1989 dividend ($1.36) | | | 2.9% |

**What the investor would compare this with.** The packet has no Treasury yield, and the framework does not state a discount rate as of this date. **I assume a long-Treasury yield of about 9%.** The packet supports that figure: the company's own notes carry coupons of 9.3–11.75%, and its other long-term debt averages about 11.8% (`ar-1988`, note 7). Management's own after-tax cost of capital is 12%.

A 5–6% starting yield is below that bond rate. The purchase therefore makes sense only if owner earnings keep growing at about the rate the record shows.

## 5b. "Past continues" test

**Growth rates taken from the record (per share):**
- EPS, 1978–88: $1.01 to $2.85, **10.9% a year**.
- EPS from continuing operations, 1981–88: $1.17 to $2.85, **13.6% a year**.
- Underlying growth (Stage 2): about 10% a year in operating income at constant currency, plus about 3 points from buybacks, gives **about 13%**.
- 1985–88 EPS growth of 18.3% a year was flattered by currency, so I do not use it.

**Method.** Grow owner earnings from $2.60 for 10 years. Pay dividends starting at $1.36 and growing at the same rate. Value year 10 at 12–15 times owner earnings, which is inside the company's own 1980–88 range of 9.8x to 21.9x and below today's 17.9x. Discount everything at 9%.

| Scenario | Growth | Year-10 multiple | PV of year-10 value | PV of dividends | **Value / share** | Approx. annual return at $46.56 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| C: currency reverses, slower growth | 8% | 12x | 28.5 | 12.0 | **$40.4** | ~6.5% |
| A: 10-year EPS record continues | 11% | 12x | 37.4 | 13.6 | **$51.0** | ~9.5% |
| B: constant-currency record plus buybacks | 13% | 15x | 55.9 | 14.8 | **$70.7** | ~13.5% |
| D: management's "mid-teens" (reference only) | 15% | 15x | 66.7 | 16.1 | **$82.7** | ~15% |

Returns are approximate: change in price plus a starting dividend yield of about 3%. Using conservative owner earnings of $2.39 instead of $2.60 lowers scenario A to about $48.

## 5c. Adjustment: affiliate stakes not captured in owner earnings

Owner earnings count only the roughly $12.5M of dividends actually received from affiliates, about $0.03 per share. The stakes themselves are worth more than that.
- They are carried at **$1,912M, or $5.39 per share**.
- The publicly traded stakes are worth $291M more than their carrying value, which puts the total at about $2,203M, or **$6.21 per share** (`ar-1988`, note 3).
- They earn little (Stages 2 and 3), so I add only the **carrying value of $5.39**, not market value.

| Scenario | Core value | Plus stakes | **Total value** | **Margin of safety at $46.56** |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| C (downside) | 40.4 | 5.4 | 45.8 | **−2%** (price slightly above value) |
| A (record continues) | 51.0 | 5.4 | 56.4 | **17%** |
| B (constant-currency record) | 70.7 | 5.4 | 76.1 | **39%** |
| D (management guidance) | 82.7 | 5.4 | 88.1 | 47% |

If the stakes are left out entirely, scenario A's margin falls to about 9%.

## 5d. Margin of safety against the framework's hurdle

**The framework sets no number.** It asks for:
- a "substantial excess of value" creating "a comfortable margin of safety" (`bpl-letter-1961#3`, `bpl-letter-1962#4`)
- a price that makes sense "even should the future… develop much along the lines of its past" (`brk-letter-1981#3`)

**Judged against those standards:**
- **If the past simply continues** (scenarios A–B), the stock is worth about **$56–76**. That is a margin of safety of **about 17–39%**, and an expected return of about **9.5–13.5% a year**, against an assumed 9% bond yield. That meets the "sensible price" standard for an outstanding business.
- **If growth slows and currency reverses** (scenario C), price roughly equals value. Nothing in the packet suggests this is likely. Volume growth, share gains and room to grow international consumption all point the other way. But it shows the cushion is small unless the moat keeps delivering.
- **The price is not a bargain.** It fails the framework's written valuation rule by a wide margin. Every bit of the margin of safety depends on how durable the moat is, which Stages 2 and 3 judged to be high, not on assets.

**Temperament check** ("fearful when others are greedy", `brk-letter-1986`). The better entry was during 1988, when the stock traded between $35 and $40, about 12–14 times 1988 EPS, after the October 1987 drop to $29 (`prices`). Since then it has risen 22% from the March 1988 close of $38.24, while earnings grew 17%. Today's 16.3x P/E is below the 1987 peak of about 22x ($53.13 / $2.43). The market is not euphoric about this stock. It is simply no longer fearful.

## 6. Catalyst / timing

- **No catalyst needed** (`brk-letter-1987`). The case is that per-share business value compounds at about 11–13% a year and that "the market… eventually will confirm it." The ongoing buyback, with 14.4M shares still authorized, raises a holder's percentage ownership without any action by the holder.
- **Work-out** (`bpl-letter-1957`). No sale, merger, liquidation or tender is pending. The entertainment-affiliate dividend and the CCE share sales are already complete. **Not applicable.**
- **Control** (`bpl-letter-1963`). A $16.5B company is not a candidate for control. The relevant model is a large "non-voting partnership" stake (`brk-letter-1981#3`), which goes to sizing.
- **No market forecasting** (`bpl-letter-1966-mid`, `fortune-1977-inflation`). I make no call on the 52-week high or on the dollar. Currency risk is covered in scenario C, not by trying to time it.

---

## Missing figures and assumptions

1. **Long-Treasury yield.** Assumed about 9%, based on the company's own recent borrowing costs. A higher yield would lower every value above.
2. **How capital spending splits between maintenance and growth.** This drives the $2.39–$2.88 range in owner earnings.
3. **Market value of each affiliate stake**, especially the entertainment affiliate, given its losses.
4. **Inflation and currency outlook.** Not forecast, by design. Scenario C covers the downside.
5. **Berkshire's capital at the as-of date.** Needed for sizing.

## Stage 4 conclusion

- **The framework's literal valuation rule (net working capital): fail.**
- **The investor's operative standard by 1989 (outstanding business at a sensible price): pass, moderately.**
  - Value is about **$56–76 per share** if the record continues, against a price of $46.56.
  - That is a margin of safety of about **17–39%**, or about 9–34% without the affiliate stakes.
  - Expected return is about **9.5–13.5% a year**, against a bond yield of about 9%.
  - In the downside case the cushion disappears, so the margin rests on the durability of the moat, not on assets.
- **No catalyst is required.** The business result is the catalyst.
