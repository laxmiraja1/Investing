**Memo: Purchase of Company A common stock, as of February 27, 1989**

We recommend buying Company A common stock and building it over time to about a quarter of the portfolio, as a permanent holding. This memo explains what the business is, why it meets our standards, what we think it is worth, and what would cause us to reconsider.

Company A sells soft-drink concentrate and syrup to more than a thousand bottlers in over 160 countries. In 1988 that business produced more than 90 percent of the company's operating income. The bottlers do the capital-intensive work of bottling and distribution. Company A owns the trademark, supplies the concentrate, and directs the marketing. Its products account for nearly 45 percent of the world's flavored carbonated soft drinks outside China and the Soviet Union, more than double the nearest competitor, and for over 40 percent of the U.S. market. The company also has a juice business, about six percent of operating income, and 49 percent stakes in its largest bottlers and in an entertainment company. These are secondary to the concentrate business, and we treat them that way.

The business passes our first screens without difficulty. Earnings per share from continuing operations rose every year from 1979 to 1988, from $1.04 to $2.85. The business is simple, and the management team that has run it since 1981 is still in place. The one screen that needed a closer look was debt. Total debt is about 39 percent of capital, or 24 percent net of excess cash. The core business does not need this borrowing: in 1988 it generated about $1.17 billion of operating cash against $387 million of capital spending. The debt was taken on by choice, mainly to fund share repurchases and investments in bottlers. We prefer companies that do not do this, but in this case the borrowing does not put the business at risk.

Our main test of a business is how much it earns on the capital it uses. Reported return on common equity was 33 percent in 1988. Part of that figure reflects debt and buybacks that have shrunk the equity base, so we also looked at returns without them. In 1978 to 1981, when debt was only 4 to 9 percent of capital, return on equity was already 21 to 23 percent. In 1988 the soft-drink business earned about 44 percent before tax, roughly 29 percent after tax, on its identifiable assets, with no debt involved. Growth has needed little new capital: from 1985 to 1988, operating income in soft drinks rose by more than the increase in the assets behind it.

Two facts make us confident these returns will last. First, the company's share of world and U.S. soft-drink sales has risen steadily, and outside surveys show the trademark well ahead of any other brand. Second, the company keeps gains that weaker businesses would have to pass on. Between 1985 and 1988 the federal tax rate fell from 46 to 34 percent and advertising spending rose by about $200 million, yet gross margin widened from 50.5 to 55.6 percent. The company has room to keep growing: consumption of its products outside the United States is about 51 servings per person a year, against 274 in the U.S.

Management has used retained earnings well. From 1984 through 1988 the company kept about $1.9 billion of earnings. Over the same period its market value rose by about $8.5 billion, after it had also spent about $2.2 billion buying back 69 million shares. Those repurchases were made at roughly 12 to 18 times earnings, at an average price of about $31.55, and the company bought heavily when the price was low and little in 1986 when it was high. Management also recognized that its 1982 move into film and television, partly paid for with new shares, had been a mistake, and undid most of it by 1987. Its financial reporting is candid even where the rest of the annual report reads like advertising. We would still want to meet management and read the proxy statement before we are fully satisfied about its compensation and its own ownership of the stock. The 1988 option grants were large.

On value, the balance-sheet test we once relied on is irrelevant here: net working capital comes to about 22 cents a share. The relevant question is whether the price makes sense if the future simply resembles the past. We estimate the cash earnings available to owners at about $2.60 a share. At $46.56, the market price is consistent with those earnings growing about 3 to 4 percent a year indefinitely. Over 1983 to 1988 they grew 12 to 16 percent a year, and management expects mid-teens growth in 1989. If growth slows to 10 percent for a decade and 5 percent after that, discounted at 10 percent, the stock is worth about $81, roughly 42 percent above the current price. If the record continues, the value is near $150. Only under deliberately pessimistic assumptions does value fall to about the current price. The market value of the bottling and entertainment stakes adds a few dollars a share beyond these estimates.

The main risks are four. About three-quarters of operating income comes from outside the United States, so a stronger dollar would reduce reported results. Management uses debt deliberately. Some of the capital tied up in the affiliates earns low returns. And our earnings estimate depends on an assumption about maintenance capital spending that the packet does not let us confirm. Several things would change our view: constant-currency figures showing much weaker underlying growth, evidence that maintenance spending is far higher than we assume, much higher long-term interest rates, or signs that management intends to diversify away from beverages again. We would sell only if the franchise itself weakened or management turned against shareholders. A higher stock price on its own would not be a reason to sell.
