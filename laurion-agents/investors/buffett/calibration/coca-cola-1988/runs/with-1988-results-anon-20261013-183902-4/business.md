# Stage 2: Business analysis (Framework §3)

The section names three measures in order: (1) return on equity rather than EPS growth, (2) the moat, and (3) owner earnings.

---

## 1. Return on equity is the primary test (`brk-letter-1979`)

The rule asks for a high earnings rate on equity capital "without undue leverage, accounting gimmickry, etc." So I first take the reported figure, then strip out leverage and accounting effects.

**Reported return on average common equity (1988 AR, selected financial data):**

| 1978 | 1980 | 1982 | 1983 | 1984 | 1985 | 1986 | 1987 | 1988 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 22.6% | 21.1% | 20.3% | 19.6% | 22.2% | 25.3% | 29.1% | 27.5% | **33.3%** |

The Fortune benchmark in `brk-letter-1987#1` is that only 6 of 1,000 large companies averaged over 30% for a decade. On the headline number, Company A is in that class.

**Adjustment for leverage.** Part of the rise from about 20% to 33% is financial engineering, not better business economics:

- Total debt to capital went from 3.8% (1978) and 9.1% (1981) to 32.3% (1984) and 38.8% (1988).
- Treasury stock went from $604M (1985) to $2,069M (1988). Buybacks have held equity nearly flat at $2,979M → $3,345M while net income rose from $722M to $1,045M.

The useful finding is in the earlier years. In 1978–1981, with almost no debt, the company already earned 21–23% on equity. That is high before any leverage was added.

**Unlevered tests for 1988:**

- **Return on total capital.** Net income plus after-tax interest is $1,045M + ($230.5M × 0.66) ≈ $1,197M. Average total capital (debt plus equity) is $5,826M. That gives about 20.5%. This understates the core business, because the capital base includes roughly $1.05B of excess cash and $1.91B of affiliate investments that together produced only about $48M of equity income.
- **Soft-drink segment.** Pretax operating income divided by identifiable assets. These are gross assets before netting payables, and no debt is involved:

| | 1985 | 1986 | 1987 | 1988 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Soft-drink op. income ($M) | 830 | 1,001 (1,181 before $180M provisions) | 1,433 | 1,691 |
| Soft-drink identifiable assets ($M) | 3,055 | 3,287 | 4,174 | 3,809 |
| Pretax return | 27% | 30% (36% before provisions) | 34% | **44%** |

At a 34% tax rate, 44% pretax is about 29% after tax on total soft-drink assets with no leverage. Within that, international earned 64% pretax (1,338.8 / 2,097.1) and the U.S. earned 21% (351.9 / 1,711.9).

- **Incremental returns.** From 1985 to 1988, soft-drink assets rose by about $754M and soft-drink operating income rose by about $861M. Growth has required little additional capital.

**Adjustment for accounting gimmickry:**

- **Non-recurring items in 1986–87 are large.** 1986 includes a $375M gain from the bottling-affiliate IPO and $180M of provisions. 1987 includes a $40M gain from the Canadian bottler IPO and a $36M provision. Taking reported EPS of $2.42 and $2.43 at face value would mislead. Management's own figure excluding 1986's unusual items shows 1987 EPS up 17%.
- **1988 is cleaner but not clean.** Items include a $25M pretax gain on the BCI stake, a share of the bottling affiliate's $104M pretax gain, and a share of the entertainment affiliate's $114M write-down. These roughly offset.
- **Deconsolidation flatters the ratios.** Capital-heavy, low-return bottling (1986–87) and entertainment (1987) have been moved into 49%-owned affiliates. This raises reported margins and ROE without changing much of the underlying economics. The bottling affiliate carries $2.86B of liabilities against $1.81B of net assets and earned about 8% on equity in 1988 ($142.7M / $1.81B). Company A still guarantees about $133M of bottler debt and $101M of the entertainment affiliate's receivables.
- **Earnings convert to cash.** Operating cash flow was $1,167M in 1988 against $1,045M of net income, and $1,212M in 1987 against $916M. The LIFO reserve is small (about $30M), and the U.S. pension plans are overfunded ($496M of assets against a $367M projected obligation). FASB 96 could reduce equity by about $175M, which would raise ROE, not lower it.
- **Currency inflates recent growth.** About 76% of operating income comes from outside the U.S. The key currencies strengthened against the dollar by 27% (1986), 16% (1987) and 10% (1988). Part of the rise in international returns reflects the weaker dollar, not operations.

**Verdict on the primary test:** passes clearly. The core concentrate business earns very high returns on the capital it actually uses, without needing leverage. The headline 33% is somewhat inflated by debt, buybacks and deconsolidation. Even so, the unlevered core return is higher than the headline, because the consolidated figure is pulled down by idle cash and low-return affiliates.

## 2. Durable competitive advantage: the moat (`brk-letter-1986#16`)

The GEICO passage asks two questions: what keeps competitors out, and is management widening the moat?

**What the moat is made of, according to the packet:**

- **The trademark.** Landor's 1988 surveys found the gap between Brand A and the #2 brand larger than the gap between #2 and #10 ("practically off the charts"). The 1985 reformulation is evidence of who holds the power: the company had to bring back the original formula as "Brand A classic," which became the best-selling U.S. soft drink in 1986. The brand's value lies in consumer attachment, which no competitor can easily copy.
- **Share and scale.**
  - World share of flavored carbonated soft drinks (excluding China and the USSR) rose from under 38% in 1980 to nearly 45% in 1988, more than double the nearest competitor.
  - U.S. share is over 40%, 9 share points ahead of the primary competitor (1986).
  - U.S. fountain share was 59% (1986). The company outsells its nearest fountain rival 2-to-1 and captured about three-quarters of the segment's growth over four years.
- **Distribution system and cost position.** More than 1,000 bottlers serve over 160 countries, and the company describes itself as "low-cost producer in most of the 155 countries where we do business." This is the GEICO-type cost moat, but the packet asserts it without showing competitors' costs.
- **Pricing power.** This is the test from `brk-letter-1986#22`: a strong, unregulated franchise keeps the benefit of a tax cut instead of passing it to customers.

| | 1985 | 1986 | 1987 | 1988 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Statutory federal rate | 46% | 46% | 40% | 34% |
| Gross margin (restated basis) | 50.5% | 50.5% | 52.6% | 55.6% |
| Operating margin (restated, before provisions) | 13.7% | 15.4% | 17.8% | 19.2% |
| Net margin | 9.4%* | 10.8%* | 12.0% | 12.5% |

\*1985 and 1986 net margins are on the original consolidated basis, so they are not strictly comparable with 1987–88.

Margins widened while the tax rate fell and while advertising rose from $648M (1986) to $842M (1988), about 10% of revenue. The company kept the gains, which is the behavior of a strong franchise. Other packet evidence:
- New contracts with the bottling affiliate allow flexible pricing, and prices were raised in October 1986.
- U.S. concentrate gross margin was about 63% (1986).
- Management says it is "able to adjust prices to compensate for increasing costs."

**Is the moat widening?** Yes, on the packet's evidence:
- World and U.S. share are both rising.
- U.S. consumption per person of Company products went from 203 to 274 servings (1980–87).
- International consumption is still only about 51 servings per person (1988), roughly 16% of the U.S. rate in 1986. Large markets are early: Indonesia about 4 servings, China supply-constrained, Mexico 219.
- The shift from syrup to concentrate and the consolidation of bottlers are reducing system costs.
- The 1986 passage describes widening the moat by "driving down costs still more." Here the comparable action is spending more on the brand and on availability (coolers, vending machines, fountain) while still widening margins.

**Weak points in the moat, from the packet:**
- **Formula pricing in the U.S.** Standard contracts with most U.S. bottlers other than the affiliate set Brand A syrup prices by formula. That limits domestic pricing freedom; international contracts are generally unrestricted.
- **Latin America.** Inflation forces price increases to stay in line with inflation, margins are low, and volume fell in Brazil and Argentina.
- **Spending to defend the brand.** The 1985 marketing spend was "extraordinarily high," and the primary competitor invests heavily, notably in citrus flavors. Advertising is a large and growing cost of keeping the moat.
- **Foods has no moat.** Orange-solids prices are a commodity input, the brand ranks #2 in chilled juice, and frozen and chilled volumes fell 22% and 6% in 1988. Operating income of $89M on $694M of assets is about 13% pretax, roughly the company's own 12% after-tax cost of capital. This is the low-return part, but it is only about 6% of operating income.
- **The entertainment affiliate** ($598M carrying value) is a hit-driven business with recurring write-downs. It is outside the franchise, but the reduced stake is a sign of reducing exposure.

**Verdict on the moat:** a wide, durable and widening moat in the core concentrate business. It rests on the trademark and the distribution system, and pricing power has been demonstrated in the numbers.

## 3. Owner earnings (`brk-letter-1986`)

The formula is reported earnings, plus depreciation, depletion, amortization and other non-cash charges, minus the average annual capital spending needed to keep the business's competitive position.

**Inputs for 1988 ($M):** net income available to common shareholders 1,038.3; depreciation and amortization 169.8; capital spending 386.8. For 1986–88, average capital spending was about $346M against average depreciation and amortization of about $160M.

The packet does not split capital spending into maintenance and growth, and the framework needs that split. Much of the spending is plainly for growth: international coolers, vending machines, new bottling infrastructure, and Asian joint ventures. Management says current-cost income would be lower than reported, which means the true maintenance level is above historical depreciation. So I give a range:

| Case | Calculation | Owner earnings | Per share (364.6M avg shares) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lower bound: all capex is maintenance | 1,038 + 170 − 387 | $821M | **$2.25** |
| Working assumption: maintenance ≈ $250M (above depreciation, to allow for inflation) | 1,038 + 170 − 250 | $958M | **$2.63** |
| Upper bound: maintenance ≈ depreciation | 1,038 | $1,038M | $2.85 |

**Cross-checks:**
- Operating cash flow minus all capital spending was $780M in 1988 and $908M in 1987.
- Working capital has been roughly neutral over 1987–88: a release in 1987, about $84M absorbed in 1988.
- Equity income was about $48M, of which only about $12.5M arrived as dividends. The rest is retained by bottlers and the entertainment affiliate, which earn low returns. Arguably it should be counted at a discount, which would trim about $0.05–0.10 per share from the figures above.
- The publicly traded equity investments were worth about $291M more than their carrying value at year-end 1988.

**Working figure for Stage 3: owner earnings of about $2.60 per share** (range $2.25–2.85). This compares with reported EPS of $2.85. The growth portion of capital spending has been earning very high incremental returns.

## Facts the framework wants that the packet lacks

1. **Maintenance versus growth capital spending.** This is the key input for owner earnings, and I had to assume it.
2. **Constant-currency operating income.** Needed to separate the dollar's decline from operating gains in the 64% international return.
3. **Competitors' cost ratios and margins.** The GEICO test is a cost comparison; here the low-cost claim is management's assertion.
4. **U.S. price per case against inflation over time.** This would test domestic pricing power directly, given that most U.S. bottler contracts use formula pricing.
5. **Why U.S. returns (21% pretax) are so far below international (64%).** The asset mix is not explained (syrup with sweetener versus concentrate, office building, fountain equipment).
6. **Economics of independent bottlers outside the affiliates.** A healthy system depends on their health.
7. **1988 10-K and 10-Q detail, and the current-cost earnings figure.** Management says current-cost earnings are lower but gives no number.
8. **Berkshire's capital on the as-of date.** Not needed here, but needed for position size later.

## Stage 2 conclusion

Company A passes all three §3 tests.

- **Return on capital:** the concentrate business earns roughly 29% after tax on unlevered soft-drink assets, and incremental capital earns more. The headline 33% ROE is partly due to leverage and buybacks, but the core return without them is higher still.
- **Moat:** the trademark and distribution system give a wide and widening advantage, with pricing power visible in rising margins through tax cuts and higher advertising.
- **Owner earnings:** about $2.60 per share as the working figure.

The weak parts are Foods, the entertainment stake, and leverage held in the bottling affiliates. They are small relative to the core business. They and currency risk should be priced in at the valuation stage; they don't call the business quality into question.
