# Stage 4: Valuation (Framework §5) and Catalyst/timing (§6)

## 5. Valuation

### Step 1: The framework's listed method, buying below net working capital (`bpl-letter-1965`)

**Net working capital** at 31 December 1988 (1988 AR balance sheet):

| | $M |
|---|---|
| Total current assets | 3,245.4 |
| Less total current liabilities | (2,868.9) |
| Net working capital | **376.5** |
| Less preferred stock at liquidation value | (300.0) |
| Available to common | 76.5 |
| Per common share (354.8M shares) | **~$0.22** (about $1.06 before deducting preferred) |

The price is **$46.56**. Common book value is $8.58 a share ($3,045M / 354.8M), so the stock trades at 5.4 times book.

**Result: the stock fails this test by a factor of more than 40.** Plant, the trademark and the affiliates add nothing under this method.

**Does the test decide the matter?** The framework says to use rules in the version in force on the as-of date, and the investor's own writings by 1989 explicitly reject this method for businesses of this kind:

- "Our goal is to find an outstanding business at a sensible price, not a mediocre business at a bargain price… In the interim, I searched for 'bargains', and had the misfortune to find some." (`brk-letter-1987#18`)
- "the same energies and talent are much better employed in a good business purchased at a fair price than in a poor business purchased at a bargain price." (`brk-letter-1979`)
- "We may very well pay a fairly fancy price for a Category 1 business if we are reasonably confident of what we are getting… at prices that will make sense, even should the future of the acquired enterprise develop much along the lines of its past." (`brk-letter-1981#3`)

The working-capital rule belongs to the Partnership-era "generals." For a franchise business whose value lies in earning power, the test in force in 1989 is the one in `brk-letter-1981#3`: **does the price make sense if the future looks much like the past?** The yardstick is owner earnings (§3; `brk-letter-1986`). The 1984 buyback passage frames value as "per-share intrinsic business value," and the 1986 letter calls that "a soft number" on which "two equally well-informed observers might make judgments more than 10% apart" (`brk-letter-1986#0`). So I give ranges, not a single point.

### Step 2: Price against owner earnings, assuming the future resembles the past

**What the market is paying for the core business** ($M, at the 24 February 1989 close):

| Item | $M |
|---|---|
| Market value of common (354.8M × $46.56) | 16,520 |
| Plus preferred | 300 |
| Plus debt, net of cash and securities (2,124 − 1,231) | 893 |
| Less affiliates at estimated market value (1,912 carrying + 291 excess, Note 3) | (2,203) |
| **Implied price of the core concentrate and foods business** | **~15,510** (≈ $43.70 per share) |

**Owner earnings, from Stage 2:**
- Working figure: **$2.60 per share**, based on maintenance capex of about $250M.
- Range: $2.25 (all capex treated as maintenance) to $2.85 (reported net income).

**Yields at $46.56:**
- Owner earnings yield: $2.60 / $46.56 = **5.6%**.
- Reported earnings yield: 6.1%.
- Price to 1988 EPS: 16.3x.

**The historical record "along the lines of its past" (packet data):**

| Measure | Period | Compound annual rate |
|---|---|---|
| EPS, continuing operations | 1978 $0.95 → 1988 $2.85 | 11.6% |
| EPS, continuing operations | 1983 $1.35 → 1988 $2.85 | 16.1% |
| Net income | 1983 $559M → 1988 $1,045M | 13.3% |
| Dividends per share | 1978 $0.58 → 1988 $1.20 | 7.5% |

Three things support growth continuing:
- **Management's 1989 guidance** is for EPS growth "well into the mid-teens" (`upi-1989-01-31`).
- **Physical volume** is growing about 7% a year worldwide. International consumption is about 51 servings per person against 274 in the U.S.
- **Buybacks** are retiring about 3–4% of shares a year.

Three things that lifted the 1983–88 record will not repeat:
- the weaker dollar;
- the fall in the tax rate from 46% to 34%;
- margin gains from deconsolidating bottling and entertainment.

**What growth does the price imply?** Using a perpetual-growth formula on $2.60 of owner earnings:
- at a 9% discount rate, 46.56 = 2.60 × (1+g) / (0.09 − g), which gives **g ≈ 3.2%**;
- at 10%, **g ≈ 4.2%**.

So the market is pricing owner earnings to grow at roughly the long-run rate of inflation, forever. Against the record, the 12–16% a year from 1983 to 1988, that assumption is very conservative.

**Discount rate.** The packet does not give the long-term U.S. Treasury yield on the as-of date, which is the yardstick I would want. As a proxy I use the company's own borrowing costs: notes at 9⅞–12¼%, other debt at about 11.8%, the finance subsidiary's bond at 9.32%. Management's stated cost of capital is 12% after tax at a 35% debt ratio. I use 9–10% for an unlevered equity cash stream of this quality, and test both.

**Owner-earnings value per share, two-stage:**

| Case | Starting OE | Growth yrs 1–10 | Growth after | Discount | Value of yrs 1–10 | Terminal value (PV) | **Value per share** |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A, record continues | $2.60 | 15% | 5% | 9% | $35.3 | $116.6 | **~$152** |
| B, growth slows | $2.60 | 10% | 5% | 10% | $26.0 | $54.6 | **~$81** |
| C, pessimistic | $2.25 | 7% | 4% | 10% | $19.4 | $29.6 | **~$49** |

How Case B is built: OE in year 10 is $6.74. Year 11 is $7.08, which capitalized at 5% (10% − 5%) is worth $141.6 in year 10, or $54.6 today. Years 1–10 are worth $26.0, because growth and discount rates are equal.

None of these cases gives the affiliates their full market value. Equity income of about $48M understates a stake worth about $2.2B, which is roughly **$3–4 a share** of extra cushion.

### Margin of safety

The framework gives **no numerical margin-of-safety hurdle**. The Partnership letters speak only of a "comfortable margin of safety" in each purchase (`bpl-letter-1961#3`, `bpl-letter-1962#4`), and the literal working-capital hurdle is inapplicable as explained above. The operative test is `brk-letter-1981#3`: the price must make sense if the future resembles the past. I judge against the conservative cases, not the record case.

| Against | Intrinsic value | Margin of safety at $46.56 |
|---|---|---|
| Case A (future resembles the past) | ~$152 | ~69% |
| **Case B (growth slows sharply)** | **~$81** | **~42%** |
| Case C (pessimistic, lower-bound earnings) | ~$49 | ~5% |

**Reading the table:**
- The price is roughly supported even in Case C, in which growth falls well below the record and owner earnings are taken at their lowest estimate.
- On the case I regard as central-conservative (B), the price is about 42% below value.
- If the record simply continues, the price is about one-third of value.

The price makes sense even if the future is considerably worse than the past.

**What would change this:**
- **A large drop in the dollar's contribution.** About 76% of operating income is earned abroad.
- **A permanent setback to the trademark.** The 1985 reformulation episode shows the brand can be mishandled, though it recovered.
- **A discount rate well above 10%.** If long Treasury yields were, say, 11–12% (not in the packet), Case B would fall to roughly $60–65. That is still a margin of safety, but a thinner one.

## 6. Catalyst / timing

- **No catalyst needed.** "The market may ignore business success for a while, but eventually will confirm it" (`brk-letter-1987`). The case rests on the business compounding owner earnings, not on a corporate event.
- **Not a work-out or a control situation** (`bpl-letter-1957`, `bpl-letter-1963`). There is no announced transaction. At a $16.5B market value, control is impossible, so this would be a permanent minority holding of the kind in `brk-letter-1987#18`.
- **Mr. Market is there to serve, not to guide** (`brk-letter-1987`). The stock fell from $53.13 (August 1987) to $28.96 in the October 1987 crash, traded at $35–40 for much of 1988, and is now $46.56, near its 52-week high of $49.12. That record gives no reason to wait. The stock has bought at 14–18x earnings for five years while business value compounded.
- **Fear and greed** (`brk-letter-1986#16`). At 16.3x trailing earnings, the stock is below its 1987 peak of about 22x ($53.13 / $2.43) and shows no sign of the "chain-letter" mania described in `bpl-letter-1968-mid#2`. Investors are not obviously greedy here, so the rule gives no reason to hold back.
- **No market forecasting** (`bpl-letter-1966-mid`, `fortune-1977-inflation`). The judgment is about valuation. The packet's evidence on the general market is irrelevant to it.
- **One practical point for Stage 5.** A large buyer will move the price of the stock itself. With roughly 355M shares outstanding and heavy trading, the stock is liquid enough to accumulate a large stake over months, but the purchase price will rise as the buyer accumulates.

## Stage 4 conclusion

- **Working-capital test:** fails decisively. The investor's 1987–89 writings put that method aside for outstanding businesses at sensible prices.
- **Test in force in 1989** (does the price make sense if the future resembles the past, measured by owner earnings): passes clearly.
  - At $46.56 the market prices owner earnings of about $2.60 to grow only about 3–4% a year forever.
  - The business has compounded at 12–16%, and its international runway is long.
  - Intrinsic value is about **$81** on conservative assumptions, a 42% margin of safety, about **$150** if the record continues, and about **$49** only under pessimistic assumptions.
- **Timing:** no catalyst is needed and the market gives no reason to wait.

**Missing inputs:**
- the long-term Treasury yield on the as-of date;
- the split of capital spending into maintenance and growth;
- constant-currency earnings history.
