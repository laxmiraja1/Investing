# Stage 3: Management and capital allocation (Framework §4)

The section has three rules, taken in order:
- honest, able management already in place, with candid reporting (`brk-letter-1979`, expanded in `brk-letter-1983#2`);
- buybacks only well below intrinsic value (`brk-letter-1984#4`);
- each $1 retained should produce at least $1 of market value (`brk-letter-1983`).

I also use the related share-issuance standard in `brk-letter-1982#17`, because it bears directly on this company's record.

---

## 1. Honest, able management in place; candid reporting

### Management is in place and has a record
The current team was assembled in 1981 and has run the company since then under a written "Strategy for the 1980s" (1987 AR). Its stated framework is economic rather than about size:
- "Management's primary objective is to increase shareholder value."
- It uses an explicit after-tax hurdle: a 12% cost of capital for soft-drink-like risk.
- It aims to divest "low return and non-strategic assets."

On ability, the Stage 2 results are the record: return on equity went from 19.6% (1983) to 33.3% (1988), and world share went from under 38% to about 45%.

### Strategic record: one detour, now largely reversed
**Into entertainment.** In 1982 the company bought a film studio. It added television production through acquisitions: Embassy and Tandem in 1985, about $445M combined, and Merv Griffin in 1986, about $200M. In 1986 it said it was "not satisfied" with the studio and changed its leadership.

**Out of entertainment.** In 1987 management folded the whole sector into a 49%-owned affiliate and distributed shares to stockholders (an in-kind dividend carried at $335M). Other exits:
- plastics, sold 1985 for $112M;
- pasta, 1984;
- South Africa, 1986;
- coffee, agreed in 1988 and closed January 1989.

**Remaining low-return area.** Foods got a $36M restructuring and new senior management in 1987. Operating income rose 34% in 1988 against weak industry volumes.

**Assessment.** Moving into a business outside the core was a capital-allocation error. But management recognized it and largely undid it within about five years. That is the quality the framework values in operating managers who "define with extraordinary realism their area of special competence" (`brk-letter-1984#7`). By 1988 more than 90% of operating income came from concentrate.

### Bottler investments
The company bought bottlers temporarily and sold them on at carrying value: Miami, Rainwater and Memphis. It created 49% affiliates in the U.S. and Canada, and took 20–49% stakes elsewhere. Management's stated reasoning is that it is strengthening the distribution system, which is what protects the trademark.

As stand-alone investments these earn little. The U.S. bottling affiliate earned about 8% on its equity in 1988, and cash dividends to Company A were only about $12.5M against $48M of equity income. On a market basis, though, the publicly traded investees were worth about $291M more than their carrying value at the end of 1988. So far the capital put into them has held its value.

### Candor (`brk-letter-1979`, `brk-letter-1983#2`)
The standard is to report "the business facts that we would want to know if our positions were reversed," and not to send "a public relations document."

**On the numbers, the reports are candid:**
- The 1986 report gives the effect of each one-time item on EPS: +$0.68 from the bottling IPO gain, −$0.33 from provisions.
- The 1987 report presents growth excluding those items.
- Currency gains are disclosed every year, by currency.
- They separate shipments from consumer sales when the two diverge:
  - Australia's 37% volume jump is explained as a shipping-pattern effect, and the report says the 11% retail figure "more accurately reflects" the market.
  - U.S. 1987 shipments rose 1% against 6% retail growth, because bottlers cut inventory.
- The inflation note says current-cost earnings would be lower, and that gains from holding net monetary liabilities "do not represent an increase in funds available for distribution."
- The FASB 96 note discloses a possible $175M hit to equity.

**The narrative sections read like promotion:**
- "potential appears limitless," "incredible growth potential," "phenomenal" (1986);
- a claimed "$250 billion added to the world economy" using a multiplier of five (1987);
- an anecdote-heavy 1988 report;
- repeated emphasis on the stock's total return against the S&P 500.

Frequent restatements, as businesses moved to the equity method, also make year-to-year comparison harder, though each one is disclosed.

**Verdict on candor:** acceptable. The financial review is straight and lets an owner reconstruct the economics. The glossy sections are a style point, not concealment.

### Compensation (from the notes only)
- **Restricted stock** under a 3M-share plan: 883,500 shares granted in 1984, falling to 178,500 in 1987, then 511,500 in 1988. Shares are forfeited if the employee leaves. In 1988 a change-of-control exception was added to the forfeiture rules.
- **Stock options** under an 8M-share plan:
  - 4.18M options granted in 1988, against 1.1M in 1987;
  - 8.66M outstanding at year-end 1988, about 2.4% of shares;
  - stock appreciation rights attached, which allow settlement in cash.
- **Performance units** (1985) pay on the gain in 555,000 shares above $20.63. **Incentive units** (1988) pay on the full value of 300,000 shares.

All of these pay off on stock price, with no return-on-capital hurdle. The jump in 1988 option grants is notable. The dilution is small relative to buybacks: about 1M shares a year exercised against 15–19M repurchased. Without the proxy statement I cannot judge the amounts paid or who received them.

## 2. Buybacks only well below intrinsic value (`brk-letter-1984#4`)

`brk-letter-1984#4` explicitly names the large repurchases at its "largest investments" as encouraging. Here is the record:

| Year | Shares bought (M) | Cost ($M) | Avg. price | P/E on that year's EPS | Note |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1984 | 18.4 | 336 | $18.24 | 11.6x ($1.57) | |
| 1985 | 15.0 | 380 | $25.33 | 14.7x ($1.72) | |
| 1986 | 2.9 | 110 | $38.37 | 18.5x ($2.07 ex-items) | bought little when expensive |
| 1987 | 14.2 | 605 | $42.70 | 17.6x ($2.43) | new 40M-share program, July 1987 |
| 1988 | 19.0 | 758 | $39.94 | 14.0x ($2.85); ~15.4x on $2.60 owner earnings | heaviest year, at lower prices |
| **1984–88** | **69.4** | **~$2.19B** | **~$31.55** | | current price $46.56 |

**Shares outstanding** fell from about 409M (January 1984) to 354.8M (December 1988), down 13%.

**Was the price well below value?** The full test needs the Stage 4 valuation, but the evidence points one way:
- In 1984–85 the company paid roughly 12–15x earnings for a business earning about 29% after tax on incremental capital and growing per-share earnings in the mid-teens.
- At those prices, owners got far more than $1 of value per $1 spent. Every block bought before 1988 is below today's price, and so are most 1988 purchases.
- Buying volume tracked price sensibly: little in 1986 at about 18.5x, the most in 1988 at about 14x.

**Two qualifications:**
1. **The stated reason is excess cash, not price against value.** Purchases began "after management determined that... available cash and debt capacity exceeded the funds required." They continue "as long as investing in our own stock remains one of the best uses of our excess cash." The 1984 passage stresses a "demonstrated" orientation. Here the behavior shows that orientation, but the stated rule could keep buying even at a full price.
2. **Part was paid for with debt.** The 1987 jump in short-term debt was partly "to fund the share repurchase program." This was deliberate: management raised its net debt target to 35% of capital to lower its cost of capital. Coverage is comfortable, with operating income of $1,598M against $230M of interest expense (about 7x) and net interest of only $31M. Still, this is a choice to use leverage, which the framework prefers to avoid.

### Share issuance (`brk-letter-1982#17`)
The 1982 studio purchase raised average shares from 372M to 408M by 1983, about 10% more shares, to buy a business of lower quality. In 1985 the Embassy deal used about 7.1M treasury shares, carried at about $24 a share, roughly half today's price. By the standard "we will issue shares only if our owners receive in business value as much as we give," these were probably unfavorable. The packet does not give the studio's purchase price, so I cannot measure it.

Since 1985 there has been no material issuance, and repurchases have been many times larger.

## 3. Each $1 retained should create at least $1 of market value (`brk-letter-1983`)

I use the window January 1984 to December 1988, which the packet fully documents.

**Total-dollar version:**
- Net income 1984–88: $4,246M.
- Less cash dividends (about $2,018M, including preferred) and the in-kind spin-off ($335M).
- Earnings retained: about **$1,893M**. This matches the change in reinvested earnings, $2,494M → $4,385M.
- Market value rose from about $7.29B (409.1M shares × $17.83) to $15.83B (354.8M × $44.63), a gain of **+$8.54B**. That gain came after about $1.84B was returned through net buybacks.
- That is about **$4.50 of market value per $1 retained**. Counting the buybacks and the spin-off as value delivered, it is about $5.70.

**Per-share version:**
- Earnings retained per share 1984–88: EPS of $11.13, less cash dividends of $5.27, less the $0.90 in-kind dividend, leaves about **$4.96**.
- The share price rose **$26.80**, from $17.83 to $44.63.
- That is about **5.4x**.

**Correction for a higher market multiple.** The P/E rose from about 13.0x ($17.83 / $1.37) to 15.7x. If the 1983 multiple is held constant, 13 × $2.85 = $37.05, a rise of $19.22 per share, or about **3.9x**.

**Correction for leverage.** Total debt rose about $1.6B over the period. Even charging that against the gain, the ratio stays well above 1:1.

The test is passed by a wide margin. Management's plan to cut the payout ratio from about 65% (1983) toward 40% fits this record. Retaining more makes sense when retained dollars have produced several dollars of value each. The 13.3% dividend increase for 1989 shows the policy is not hoarding.

## What the investor would rely on that the packet lacks

1. **Meeting management.** The framework's character test rests on first-hand judgment of the people ("people we like and admire," `brk-letter-1987#18`). The packet gives only reports and behavior.
2. **Proxy statement:** salaries, bonus formulas, who received the 1988 grant of 4.2M options, and above all **how much stock management and directors own** ("we eat our own cooking," `brk-letter-1983#0`).
3. **How management decides when to buy back stock.** Does it estimate intrinsic value, or just apply surplus cash? Asking directly would settle qualification 1 above.
4. **Board independence.** Directors include the chiefs of two banks, an investment banker and a partner at a law firm, all possible service providers. The packet doesn't show their dealings with the company or their share ownership.
5. **The returns on the 1982–86 entertainment acquisitions**, including the studio's purchase price, to measure the value given up through share issuance.
6. **Succession plans**, and how capital budgeting works inside the company.

## Stage 3 conclusion

Management passes §4.

- **Retained-earnings test:** strongly passed, at about 4–5x market value per dollar retained.
- **Buybacks:** large, made at multiples well below what the business's returns justify, and slowed when the price was high. The record shows the shareholder orientation the framework rewards.
- **Strategic record:** the main error, the stock-funded move into entertainment, was recognized and largely reversed. The company is now concentrated on its high-return core.
- **Reporting:** the financial disclosure is candid, though the narrative is promotional.

**Points to watch:**
- The buyback rule is framed around excess cash rather than price against value.
- Leverage is used deliberately.
- Option grants rose sharply in 1988.
- Insider ownership and compensation details are not in the packet.

None of these breaks a rule. A personal meeting and the proxy would be needed to confirm the integrity side of "honest, able."
