# Stage 3: Management and capital allocation (Framework §4)

The section has three rules, applied in order:
1. Honest, able management already in place, with candid reporting (`brk-letter-1979`).
2. Buybacks only well below intrinsic value (`brk-letter-1984#4`).
3. Each $1 retained must create at least $1 of market value (`brk-letter-1983`).

Share issuance and compensation fall under the same owner-orientation test (`brk-letter-1982#17`), so I treat them alongside.

---

## 4.1 Honest, able management in place; candid reporting

**In place.** The current Chairman/CEO and President/COO team was assembled in 1981 (`ar-1987`, "When we assembled our present management team in 1981"). Their record is seven-plus years: ROE rose from about 20% to 33%, net income grew from $482M (1981) to $1.04B (1988), and the business was refocused so that soft drinks are more than 95% of operating income. The framework's requirement that management already be there, because Berkshire can't supply it (`brk-letter-1982`), is met.

**Ability: operating record and mistakes.**
- **New formula, 1985 (largest error).** Management replaced the flagship formula, then reintroduced the original as "classic" within the same year. By 1986 that product was the top-selling US soft drink. The packet does not frame this as an admitted mistake, but it shows a willingness to reverse quickly rather than defend the error.
- **Film studio.** Management said plainly that it was "not satisfied with the performance of the film studio's feature films in 1986" and changed studio leadership (`ar-1986`).
- **Foods.** Management called the business "obviously in transition," took a $36M provision and changed its senior management (`ar-1987`).
- **Focus.** The 1987 report reorients the whole company around the core: "first and foremost a marketer of soft drinks." That is the right conclusion, reached after a costly diversification detour (see 4.3).

**Candor: the substance is good, the tone is promotional.** Under `brk-letter-1979` and `brk-letter-1983#2`, the test is whether owners are told the business facts we would want if positions were reversed.

*On the plus side, management disclosed:*
- The per-share effect of each unusual item: the 1986 IPO gain was +68¢, the provisions −33¢, net +35¢. The 1987 letter then restates growth "excluding 35 cents per share of unusual items in 1986" (+17.4% EPS, not +0.4%).
- That currency aided results ("aided by a 16 percent strengthening of key currencies"), with a table of exchange-rate moves.
- That current-cost earnings "would be less than reported."
- That purchasing-power gains from net monetary liabilities "do not represent an increase in funds available for distribution."
- Off-balance-sheet guarantees ($394M), receivables sold with recourse ($325M for CPE), and the coming CCFC consolidation adding about $300M of debt.

These are the disclosures an owner needs, and they were made.

*On the minus side:*
- The reports are glossy marketing documents. One example is the "$250 billion added to the world economy" multiplier claim.
- The letters lead with stock-price total return ("38 percent in 1986… 30 percent compounded since 1981") and with "increasing shareholder value." The framework itself calls that phrase "this season's favorite" and says the test is "demonstrated" action rather than words (`brk-letter-1984#4`).
- The 1986 Financial Highlights headline growth figures (+37.9% income, +40.7% EPS) include the non-operating IPO gain.

**Verdict on 4.1:** Management is able and in place. Reporting is promotional in tone but honest in substance. Nothing in the packet suggests concealment. Judging character, which the framework weights heavily, requires information I don't have (see the end of this stage).

## 4.2 Buybacks: only well below intrinsic value

**Record (from the shareholders' equity statements):**

| Year | Shares bought | Cost | Average price | P/E on that year's EPS |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1984 | 18.4M | $336M | ~$18.24 | ~11.6x |
| 1985 | 15.0M | $380M | ~$25.33 | ~14.7x (cont. ops) |
| 1986 | 2.9M | $110M | ~$38.37 | ~15.9x (reported; ~18x ex-gain) |
| 1987 | 14.1M | $605M | ~$42.79 | ~17.6x |
| 1988 | 18.9M | not in packet | est. $35–45 range | ~12–16x |

- Average shares fell from 408M (1983) to 377M (1987). Shares at end-1988 are roughly 355M (my estimate: 372.4M − 18.9M + about 1.3M option exercises). That is a reduction of about 13% in five years.
- A further 14.5M shares remain authorized under the 40M program through 1990.

**Did they buy well below intrinsic value?** Final judgment depends on the Stage 4 valuation, but Stage 2's evidence gives a preliminary read. The business earns about 50% on core capital, grows earnings in the low-to-mid teens, and has owner earnings near reported earnings.
- **1984–85 purchases** at 11–15x earnings look clearly below the value of such a franchise. These are the "$2 of present value for $1" kind the framework praises in `brk-letter-1984#4`.
- **1987 purchases** at about $43 were made near the pre-crash highs ($53 peak). The price fell to $29 in October. Measured against 1988 EPS of $2.85, $43 is about 15x, which is probably still below intrinsic value but not "well below" with a wide margin.
- **1988 purchases** were made in the depressed post-crash range ($35–45), better aligned with the rule. The packet has no cost figure to confirm the average.

**Stated motive.** The reports justify buybacks mainly as a use of excess cash and debt capacity: "after management determined that… available cash and debt capacity exceeded the funds required" (`ar-1986`, `ar-1987`). The 1987 report adds "often the best use of excess cash… has been to invest in our own stock," which comes closer to a price-versus-value rationale. Management never states its own estimate of intrinsic value. In the framework's terms, the evidence is *demonstrated* action: over $2B repurchased in five years, which a manager indifferent to owners would not do. But the price discipline is not articulated.

**Funding.** The 1987 buybacks and bottler stakes were financed largely with short-term borrowing. Loans and notes rose from $698M to $1,685M. The net debt-to-capital target was raised to 35% explicitly to "reduce the weighted average cost of capital." This is reasonable financial engineering for a business that needs no capital, but it trends away from the framework's preference for little or no debt (§2). The 1988 drop in net interest (−52%) suggests cash flow is paying it down.

**Verdict on 4.2:** Buybacks pass on balance. They are large, persistent and mostly at prices plausibly well below value. The 1987 tranche is the weak spot, and price discipline is not explicit.

## 4.3 Each $1 retained must create at least $1 of market value

**Aggregate test, 1981 through Feb 1989 (my computation):**

| Item | Per share (adjusted) |
|---|---|
| Net income per share, 1981–1988 (1.30 + 1.32 + 1.37 + 1.59 + 1.84 + 2.42 + 2.43 + 2.85) | $17.12 |
| Cash dividends (.77 + .83 + .89 + .92 + .99 + 1.04 + 1.12 + 1.20) | $(8.76) |
| In-kind CPE dividend, 1987 | $(0.90) |
| **Earnings retained per share** | **≈ $7.46** |
| Market price: end-1980 $11.13 → 24 Feb 1989 $46.56 | **+$35.43** |

- **Market value gained per $1 retained is about $4.75.** In company totals: market value went from about $4.1B (372M × $11.13) to about $16.5B (about 355M shares × $46.56), even after roughly $2.2B of stock was retired, against about $2.5–2.8B of earnings retained.
- **Stripping multiple expansion.** The P/E rose from about 9.8x (on 1980 EPS of $1.14) to 16.3x. At an unchanged 9.8x, the 1988 EPS of $2.85 would give a price of about $27.90, a gain of $16.77, or **about $2.25 per $1 retained.**

The test is passed comfortably on both measures.

**Where the retained dollars actually went.** The aggregate test hides a mixed allocation record.

*Good uses:*
- **Buybacks** (above).
- **International system investment** (coolers, fountain, bottler joint ventures in the Philippines, Taiwan, Indonesia, China, Britain). This is the source of the 77% international share of soft-drink profit.
- **Bottler restructuring.** Bottlers were bought and resold at roughly carrying value (Rainwater, Miami, and Memphis to CCE), with "proceeds… approximated the Company's carrying value." That means no loss and a stronger system.

*Poor uses:*
- **Entertainment.** The film studio was bought in 1982. Embassy and Tandem followed in 1985 (about $445M), then Merv Griffin in 1986 (about $200M). By end-1986 the sector tied up about $1.44B and contributed to equity income running at about 5%. In 1988, CPE's write-downs cut the company's equity income by 57%. That is far below management's own stated 12% after-tax cost of capital.
- **Diversification outside soft drinks generally.** Plastics (Presto, sold 1985), pasta (Ronco, sold 1984), and Foods, which needed restructuring in 1987.
- **The CCE stake ($749M) itself.** It earns little, though it may be justified strategically to protect the concentrate franchise.

Management has since corrected course. It spun out 51% of entertainment, deconsolidated bottling, and declared the soft-drink focus. Under `brk-letter-1981#3`, the outside investments are "category (2) miscalculations," errors about the future economics of a business, and were survivable only because the core earns so much.

**Dividend policy going forward.** The payout target is being cut to 40% so that more is retained. Under the $1-retained test, that is right only if the retained money finds returns like the core's. The core needs little capital, so the incremental retention will mostly go to buybacks and bottler stakes. Buybacks below value pass the test. Bottler stakes and any renewed diversification are the risk.

## 4.4 Share issuance and compensation (owner orientation, `brk-letter-1982#17`)

**Share issuance for acquisitions.** In 1985 the company issued about 7.1M treasury shares to buy Embassy, at about $23.83/share. That is about 14x earnings and 3x book, for a TV and film business. This gave away claims on the franchise at what was very likely below its intrinsic value, in exchange for a mediocre business: exactly the "value-diluting" issuance the 1982 passage warns against. It was small (about 1.8% of shares) and has not recurred. Since then, issuance has been limited to options.

**Compensation (only plan terms; no pay amounts in the packet):**
- **Options:** 5.6–6.1M outstanding (about 1.5% of shares). Grants are 1.1–1.8M a year and exercises about 1.2–1.3M a year, offsetting roughly 7–9% of buyback volume. The new 1987 plan covers 8M shares.
- **Restricted stock:** a plan of up to 3M shares; dividends and votes are paid on unvested shares.
- **Performance units:** cash awards on 555,000 shares based on the stock price above $20.63. At $46.56 these are worth about $14.4M to the officers.

Pay is tied almost entirely to the **stock price**, not to return on capital or to business results. The framework judges managers by return on equity, not by the market price, so this is a mismatch. Dilution is modest, and the record shows managers did deliver ROE. The packet does not give pay levels or insider holdings.

## Stage 3 verdict

**Pass, with reservations.**
- Management is in place and able, and the core business improved sharply under it.
- Reporting is candid on substance and promotional in tone.
- Buybacks are large and mostly well-priced, with 1987 the weak spot.
- The $1-retained test is passed by a wide margin (about $2–4.75 of market value per $1 retained).

**Main reservations:**
1. The 1982–86 diversification into entertainment and foods earned well below the company's own cost of capital, including a share issuance at a low price.
2. Buybacks are increasingly debt-funded, and management's language centers on stock price.
3. Compensation is tied to stock price rather than business returns.

These do not disqualify the investment. The recent direction (focus on the core, spin-outs, buybacks) is the correct one, and the franchise is strong enough to have absorbed the errors.

## What the framework would rely on that the packet does not contain

1. **Meeting management.** Judging character and whether they are "likeable" partners (`brk-letter-1987#18`) on the standard used for Murphy/Burke, Mrs. B and Snyder/Simpson. Above all: does the CEO think in terms of per-share intrinsic value, and would he have bought back stock with a stated price discipline?
2. **The proxy statement:** executive pay levels, director and officer share ownership (do they "eat their own cooking," `brk-letter-1983#0`), and option grant terms.
3. **The cost and timing of the 1988 buybacks,** and management's own view of intrinsic value at the time.
4. **Management's after-the-fact assessment of the entertainment acquisitions.** Have they admitted the mistake in the way `brk-letter-1985#6` treats textiles, or only reorganized around it?
5. **Board dynamics:** how independent directors oversaw the diversification and the leverage policy.
6. **The 1988 annual report and letter:** current tone, how much debt was paid down, and any new acquisition plans for the roughly $1.2B of stated borrowing capacity.
