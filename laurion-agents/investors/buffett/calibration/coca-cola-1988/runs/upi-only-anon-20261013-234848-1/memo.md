**Memo: Purchase of a Long-Term Stake in Company A (AAA), as of February 27, 1989**

We recommend buying Company A at about $46.56 a share. We would accumulate over several months up to roughly $52, and aim for a position of about a quarter of the portfolio. We intend to hold it permanently. The reasons follow the order of our analysis: what the business is, why it passes our tests, what it is worth, and what would cause us to reconsider.

The business sells soft-drink syrup and concentrate to a worldwide network of mostly independent bottlers and to fountain customers. Selling concentrate requires little capital and earns gross margins of about 63%. The company owns the most widely recognized trademark in its category. Its volume is twice that of its nearest competitor, and its brands account for about 44% of soft drinks sold worldwide outside the Soviet Union and China. In the United States it holds more than 40% of the market and about 59% of fountain sales, outselling its main rival in that channel by more than two to one. Soft drinks produce over 95% of operating income. A foods business and minority stakes in bottlers and an entertainment company are the remainder.

The company passes each of our gatekeeping tests. It is not a turnaround: net income has risen every year from $310 million in 1977 to $1.04 billion in 1988, and dividends have been paid since 1920. The business is simple enough that the question that matters over the next decade can be stated plainly. International per-capita consumption is about one-sixth of the American level, so the question is whether that gap keeps narrowing. The company is also large enough for a position to matter to us.

The primary measure of business quality, return on equity earned without heavy borrowing, is strong. From 1977 to 1981 the company earned about 20% on equity while carrying almost no debt. That is the clean baseline. Reported returns have since risen to 27–33%, partly from buybacks and some leverage. They also understate the core business, because about $2.3 billion of capital sits in bottling and entertainment affiliates that earn around 5%. Excluding those stakes, the concentrate business earns roughly 50% on its tangible capital. Its advantage is durable. The clearest evidence is 1985, when management replaced the flagship formula, a mistake that could have damaged a weaker franchise. The original formula, relaunched, became the best-selling soft drink in the country within a year. Gross margins have widened since then. The company has kept the benefit of lower corporate tax rates rather than passing it on to customers, which is a sign of pricing power.

Management has been in place since 1981 and has a mixed but improving record in deploying capital. Spending on entertainment and foods between 1982 and 1986 earned far below the company's own stated 12% cost of capital. That included issuing about 7.1 million shares at roughly 14 times earnings to buy a television producer. Since then management has spun off most of entertainment, moved bottling into separately capitalized affiliates, and repurchased about 13% of the shares, much of it at prices well below what we think the business is worth. Over the period, each dollar of retained earnings has produced between two and nearly five dollars of market value, depending on whether the rise in the stock's multiple is counted. Annual reports are promotional in tone, but they do disclose one-time gains, currency effects and guarantees. Executive pay is tied to the stock price rather than to returns on capital, which we would prefer were otherwise.

On value, the company's net working capital is about five cents a share, so the stock fails the old bargain test completely. That test was designed for a different kind of investment and does not apply to a franchise of this quality. Instead we value the business as a private owner would. Owner earnings are reported earnings plus depreciation, less the capital spending needed to keep the business competitive. We estimate them at $2.24 to $2.71 a share for 1988, excluding affiliate income. We add the affiliate stakes at about $6.48 a share, based on their reported market value. If owner earnings grow 8% a year for a decade and 4% thereafter, discounted at 10%, the business is worth about $64 a share. If the 1981–88 growth rate of roughly 13% continues, it is worth about $85. Only in a harsh case, a 12% discount rate combined with the low end of the earnings estimate, does value fall to the current price. At $46.56, a buyer receives an owner-earnings yield of about 6% on the core business that can grow without much new capital. This is a sensible price for an exceptional business, which is what we are looking for. It is not a deep discount. That is why we are sizing at about 25% rather than the maximum.

Several things would change our view. Reported growth has been helped by a weaker dollar, and that could reverse. Borrowing is rising to fund buybacks: total debt reached 45.6% of capital at the end of 1987, although net debt was only 15.3%. A return to diversification outside soft drinks would be a serious concern. We would also reconsider if share or pricing power eroded in the fountain channel or in key foreign markets, or if unit growth fell to the rate of inflation. We have not yet seen the 1988 annual report or met management. If both confirm disciplined buybacks and falling net debt, we would consider enlarging the position. We would sell only if we had misjudged the franchise's economics or its managers. A higher stock price alone would not be a reason to sell.
