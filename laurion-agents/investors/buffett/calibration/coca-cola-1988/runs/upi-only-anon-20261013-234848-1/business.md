# Stage 2: Business analysis (Framework §3)

The framework names three measures, in this order: (1) return on equity "without undue leverage, accounting gimmickry, etc." (`brk-letter-1979`); (2) the moat (`brk-letter-1986#16`); (3) owner earnings (`brk-letter-1986`). Figures are from `ar-1986`, `ar-1987`, `fin-summary` and the two UPI stories. Computations are mine and flagged as estimates.

---

## 3.1 Return on equity is the primary test, not EPS growth

**Reported ROE (income from continuing operations to average equity):**

| Period | ROE | Total debt / total capital |
|---|---|---|
| 1977–1981 | 19.9–21.2% | 3.5–9.1% |
| 1982–1983 | 19.4–19.6% | ~15–17% |
| 1984 | 21.8% | ~31–32% |
| 1985 | 23.5% | ~28–30% |
| 1986 | 28.8% | ~31–33% |
| 1987 | 27.2% | 45.6% (15.3% net) |
| 1988 | 33% (company-reported, `upi-1989-01-31`) | n/a |

The test has three parts.

**(a) The baseline is clean.** From 1977 to 1981 the company earned about 20% on equity with debt at 3.5–9% of capital. It was not carrying leverage then and was not using the "innovative structures" it uses now. That is already above-average performance. On the `brk-letter-1987#1` benchmark, Fortune found only six of 1,000 large companies averaging over 30% for a decade, and 20% sustained for years is well above the average American corporation.

**(b) Is the rise to 27–33% from undue leverage?** Mostly not.
- At year-end 1987 gross debt was $2,702M. Against it the company held $1,268M of excess cash, $545M of CPE notes (paid in cash January 1988) and $308M of temporary investments (sold January 1988). Net debt was about $581M, or 15.3% of capital.
- Net interest cost was small: $279M expense less $207M income, about $72M pretax, against $1,410M of pretax income.
- In 1988 net interest expense fell another 52% (`upi-1989-01-31`).
- Buybacks shrink the equity base and lift ROE: treasury stock went from $604M to $1,309M in two years, and 18.9M more shares were bought in 1988. That is a capital-allocation effect, examined in §4. It is not borrowing that the operations need.

**(c) Accounting gimmickry: how much of the reported number is real?** Several items need adjusting.
- **Gains on subsidiaries' stock sales.** These were $375M pretax in 1986 (CCE IPO) and $40M in 1987 (TCC IPO). They are non-cash to the company and non-recurring. In 1986 the net of this gain and the $180M provisions added 35¢ to $2.42 of EPS. Stripping both, 1986 underlying net income is about $800M, roughly 25% ROE rather than 28.8%.
- **Deconsolidation.** Bottling (CCE, TCC) and entertainment (CPE) were moved to 49%-owned equity affiliates. Comparisons across years now require restated bases: 1986 revenue is $8,669M originally and $6,977M restated. This also moves debt off the balance sheet. CCE carried $2.72B of liabilities at end-1987, which the company has not guaranteed. The company did guarantee $394M of bottler, CCFC and other debt, plus $325M of CPE receivables sold with recourse. CCFC consolidation in 1988 adds about $300M of debt. These items are disclosed, but they make the parent look leaner than the system is.
- **The affiliates drag ROE down, not up.** At end-1986 the affiliate stakes were carried at about $2.45B (CPE $1,437M, CCE $709M, TCC $88M, other $212M). They produced $118.5M of equity income in 1987, about 4.8%. That figure is already after the investees' own taxes and interest.

**The core business on its own (my estimate, 1987):**
- Equity of $3,224M plus net debt of $581M, less affiliate carrying values of $2,258M, leaves about $1.55B of capital in the core.
- Core earnings: net income $916M, less equity income ($118M), less the TCC gain ($40M), plus the after-tax restructuring charge (~$24M), plus after-tax net interest (~$47M). That gives about $830M.
- Return on core capital is roughly 50%+ after tax.
- A cruder cross-check: soft-drink operating income of $1,432.5M over soft-drink identifiable assets of $4,174M is 34% pretax on gross assets. International alone earns 52% ($1,108.9M on $2,126.7M). US earns 16%, but its 1987 asset base is swollen by bottlers held for resale (Miami CCBC, sold January 1988).
- The trademark is not on the balance sheet (goodwill is only $74M). As at See's, the economic goodwill is invisible in book value, so these returns are on historical tangible capital.

**Conclusion on 3.1:** The primary test is passed strongly. Consolidated ROE of 27–33% *understates* the concentrate business, because about $2.3B of capital sits in low-return bottling and entertainment stakes. Leverage and buybacks explain some of the rise from 20% to 33%. Margin expansion explains the rest:

| Year (restated basis) | Gross margin | Operating margin, before provisions |
|---|---|---|
| 1985 | 50.5% | 13.7% |
| 1986 | 50.5% | 15.4% |
| 1987 | 52.6% | 17.8% |
| 1988 | ~55% (UPI $4.6B on $8.3B) | n/a |

EPS growth is secondary under this rule. I note only that per-share figures are inflated relative to the business by buybacks and currency, which matters at valuation.

## 3.2 Durable competitive advantage (the moat)

The GEICO standard asks whether there is a measurable, widening advantage that rivals cannot close. The evidence in the packet:

**Scale and share leadership**
- World volume is double the nearest competitor's.
- Company brands are 44% of soft drinks sold worldwide, excluding the USSR and China (`ar-1987`).
- US share was over 39% in 1986, 9 points ahead of the primary competitor, and over 40% in 1988.
- In 1986 the company took more than 60% of US industry growth and three-quarters of sugar-cola growth (`ar-1986`).

**Fountain lock-in**
- US fountain share is 59%, outselling the nearest rival more than 2:1.
- Three-quarters of US fountain volume is chain and fast-food accounts.
- The company captured nearly three-quarters of fountain-segment growth over four years.
- International fountain had its 12th straight year of double-digit growth.
- Fountain contracts with large chains are sticky. The packet does not give contract terms.

**Pricing power and gross margin**
- Average gross margin on concentrate sold to US bottlers was about 63% (`ar-1986`).
- New bottler contracts allow "flexible pricing … at its discretion." The company used this in October 1986 to raise prices to CCE.
- Management states it "is able to adjust prices to compensate for increasing costs" (`ar-1986`, `ar-1987` inflation sections). That matters under the inflation concern in `brk-letter-1979#2` and `fortune-1977-inflation`.

**Tax-cut pass-through test (`brk-letter-1986#22`)**
- The statutory rate fell from 46% to 40% (1987) and then to 34% (1988).
- Over the same period net margin on restated revenue rose from about 11.5% (1985) to 12.0% (1987) and about 12.5% (1988).
- The benefit appears to have stayed with the company rather than going to customers, which marks a strong franchise. Currency and the shift to concentrate (lower revenue, higher margin percentage) confound this, so it is supporting evidence, not proof.

**Low-cost producer**
- The company cites its "status as low-cost producer in most of the 155 countries" (`ar-1987`) and says the core business has "low capital requirements and high return on investment."

**Survived a management error**
- In April 1985 the company replaced its flagship formula. It reintroduced the original as "classic," and by 1986 that was the best-selling US soft drink, with record trademark awareness and preference scores (`ar-1986`).
- A franchise that absorbs a blunder of that size without losing share is the opposite of a fragile one. It is the strongest moat evidence in the packet.

**Runway (is the castle getting bigger?)**
- International per-capita consumption was 16% of the US rate (`ar-1986`).
- International markets produce 65% of volume and about 77% of soft-drink operating income.
- US per-capita consumption itself rose from 203 to 274 servings between 1980 and 1987, so even the mature market grows.
- Volume growth was 10% (1986), 6% (1987) and about 6–7% (1988).

**Moat risks the packet shows**
- **Currency inflates reported growth.** Hard currencies strengthened 27% (1986), 16% (1987) and 10% (1988). The 25% international operating income gain in 1987 was "aided by" the 16% currency move. Underlying growth is closer to unit volume plus pricing, perhaps low-to-mid teens. That is still excellent.
- **The US is "intensely competitive."** The primary competitor spends heavily, and efficiencies are "passed along to consumers" in price. The US earns lower returns on assets than international.
- **Latin America** has "tremendous unit volume and low profit margins" under hyperinflation.
- **Bottler health.** The system depends on independent bottlers. CCE earned $88M on $3.3B of revenue with heavy debt. If bottlers are squeezed, concentrate pricing power has a ceiling.
- **Side businesses are not moats.** Foods has falling orange juice volume and restructuring. Entertainment (CPE) took write-downs that cut equity income 57% in 1988. Both are small relative to soft drinks.

**Conclusion on 3.2:** This is a wide, demonstrated moat. The brand-plus-bottler-system franchise holds leading share in every major channel, earns high gross margins, has pricing power, has survived a self-inflicted error, and has a long international runway. Unlike GEICO's expense ratio, I cannot measure the moat's width against competitors' economics, because the packet has no competitor figures.

## 3.3 Owner earnings

Per the framework: (a) reported earnings, plus (b) D&A and other non-cash charges, less (c) average annual capitalized expenditures for plant and equipment. All figures use the 1987 restated basis, $ millions.

| Item | 1987 |
|---|---|
| (a) Net income | 916.1 |
| + Depreciation | 151.9 |
| + Goodwill amortization | 1.6 |
| + Restructuring provision (non-cash) | 36.4 |
| − Non-cash gain, TCC stock sale | (39.7) |
| − (c) Average capex 1985–87 ($412, $346, $300) | (352.7) |
| **Owner earnings, version 1** | **≈ 714 (≈ $1.89/share on 377.4M)** |
| − Undistributed affiliate earnings (equity income net of dividends) | (79.7) |
| **Owner earnings, version 2 (cash-only on affiliates)** | **≈ 634 (≈ $1.68/share)** |

**The capex line overstates the "required" spending in (c):**
- It includes growth investment in international infrastructure.
- It includes fixed assets of purchased companies.
- It includes corporate building construction: corporate capex was $74–98M a year.
- The fuller statement of the concept in `brk-letter-1986` asks only for the capex needed to maintain competitive position and unit volume. The packet does not split maintenance from growth.
- If maintenance capex is roughly equal to depreciation (about $152M), owner earnings approach adjusted net income: about $913M, or $2.42/share.

**Cash-flow cross-check:** 1987 working capital provided by operations was $959M, less capex of $300M, which leaves about $659M. That is consistent with the range above.

**1987 owner earnings: about $1.70–$2.40/share. The truth is probably toward the upper end,** given the growth content in capex and the "low capital requirements" of concentrate.

**1988:**
- Reported EPS was $2.85, and that figure is *depressed* by CPE write-downs (equity income −57%).
- I estimate operating income at about $1.6B. Soft drinks: international $1,109M × 1.21 plus US $324M × 1.09, about $1.69B; Foods +34%, about $89M; corporate, about −$175M or more. This is my estimate, not a reported figure.
- If the 1987 ratio of owner earnings to net income (0.78–1.0) holds, 1988 owner earnings are roughly $2.20–2.85/share. The packet has no 1988 cash flow statement, so this cannot be confirmed.

**1986:** Too noisy to use. It has the $375M non-cash gain, $180M of provisions, entertainment-film capitalization, and restatement.

## Stage 2 verdict

This is a business the framework would rate as excellent. ROE was about 20% before any leverage and is now in the high 20s to 33%. The concentrate core earns about 50% on its tangible capital, which the consolidated figure hides because of low-return affiliate stakes. The moat is wide and still widening abroad. Owner earnings are close to reported earnings, because the core needs little reinvestment to maintain itself. Two cautions carry forward:
1. Strip currency, subsidiary-IPO gains and buyback effects before extrapolating growth.
2. Rising leverage and off-balance-sheet bottler debt should be watched at the management and capital-allocation stage (§4).

## Facts the framework wants that the packet lacks

1. **1988 annual report:** balance sheet (year-end equity, debt, cash), cash flow statement, capex, D&A, deferred taxes, and the cost of the 18.9M shares repurchased. Without these, 1988 ROE and owner earnings rest on press releases.
2. **Maintenance vs. growth capex split**, needed for a proper (c) in owner earnings.
3. **Constant-currency operating income** by region, to measure real growth.
4. **Competitor economics** (margins, expense ratios, advertising per case) to measure moat width the way GEICO's 23.5% expense ratio was compared with peers.
5. **Concentrate price per unit over time**, in real terms, as direct evidence of pricing power.
6. **Bottler economics:** CCE's debt service capacity and returns, and independent bottlers' health, which bear on how far concentrate prices can rise.
7. **International market shares by country** and contract terms for major fountain accounts.
8. **CPE's 1988 results** and the size of the write-downs.
9. **Return on assets by segment, excluding temporary holdings.** The US 1987 figure is distorted by bottlers held for resale.
