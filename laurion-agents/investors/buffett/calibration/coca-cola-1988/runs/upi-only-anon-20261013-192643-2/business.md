# Stage 2: Business analysis (framework §3)

The core concentrate business clears all three §3 tests by a wide margin. Return on equity is high and has been earned without undue leverage. There is clear evidence of a durable franchise. Owner earnings are close to reported earnings. The weaker parts are the side businesses (Foods, and the bottling and entertainment affiliates), plus a currency tailwind that flatters recent growth.

## 3.1 Return on equity is the primary test, not EPS growth

The 1979 letter sets the test as "a high earnings rate on equity capital employed (without undue leverage, accounting gimmickry, etc.) and not the achievement of consistent gains in earnings per share" (`brk-letter-1979`). Each part of that sentence needs checking.

**Reported return on equity and gross leverage** (`ar-1987` 11-year table, `upi-1989-01-31`):

| Year | Net income ($M) | ROE (company, avg equity) | Total debt / total capital |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1977 | 310 | 20.6% | 3.5% |
| 1981 | 432 | 19.9% | 9.1% |
| 1983 | 553 | 19.4% | 15.1% |
| 1985 | 678 | 23.5% | 27.7% |
| 1986 | 934 | 28.8% | 31.4% |
| 1987 | 916 | 27.2% | 45.6% |
| 1988 | 1,040 | ~33% | not in packet |

**Leverage.** From 1977 to 1983 the company earned about 20% on equity with almost no debt. That is the cleanest evidence of the franchise's underlying earning power. Since 1984, return on equity has risen alongside gross debt, so I have to ask whether the improvement comes from the business or from the balance sheet.

- Net of excess cash, notes receivable from the entertainment affiliate, and temporary investments, debt was 15.3% of capital at year-end 1987 (`ar-1987`).
- Net interest expense in 1987 was about $72M ($279M expense less $207M income). Against $1,324M of operating income, that is coverage of about 18 times.
- Net interest expense fell 52% in 1988 (`upi-1989-01-31`).
- Most of the shrinkage in the equity base came from buybacks funded by cash flow: $605M in 1987 and 18.9M shares in 1988. Leverage did not cause it.

I conclude leverage is a minor contributor, not "undue."

**One-off items.** The reported years contain large non-recurring items:
- 1986 includes a $375M pretax gain from selling stock in the bottling affiliate (+$262M after tax) and $180M of provisions (−$128M after tax). Normalized 1986 net income is about $800M. On average equity of about $3,247M, that is roughly **24.6%**, not 28.8%.
- In 1987, the $40M gain on the Canadian bottler roughly offsets the $36M Foods provision, so about 27% stands.
- Pension accounting changes cut expense by $7M in 1986 and $13M in 1987. These are small.
- I see no gimmickry that changes the conclusion. The constant restatements and deconsolidations do make year-to-year comparisons harder. I come back to this under Stage 3.

**Return on the core business.** Consolidated equity mixes the concentrate business with about $2.26B of equity-method stakes. Those stakes are the bottling affiliate ($749M), the entertainment affiliate ($989M), the Canadian bottler ($84M) and others ($435M). The segment data (`ar-1987` Note 16) separates the core:

| Pretax operating income ÷ identifiable assets | 1985 | 1986 (ex-provisions) | 1987 |
|---|---|---|---|
| International soft drinks | 39.1% | 47.7% | 52.1% |
| USA soft drinks | 14.6% | 20.6% | 15.8% (≈18.6% excl. $308M bottlers held for resale) |
| Soft drinks combined | 26.5% | 31.6% | 34.3% |
| Foods (1987 ex-$36M provision) | 24.2% | 20.3% | 16.4% |

Soft drinks combined earn about 34% pretax, or roughly 22% after a 35% tax rate, on *gross* assets with no leverage at all. On net operating capital the figure would be considerably higher, but the packet gives no segment liabilities. The international concentrate business earns over 50% pretax on its assets. This is the kind of business the 1987 letter describes, where returns this high are seldom seen (`brk-letter-1987#1`).

**Foods.** The returns are falling. Frozen orange juice volume fell 22% in 1988 and the Foods business went through restructuring in 1987. It is small: about 5–8% of operating income.

**Equity affiliates.** These earn poor returns.
- The bottling affiliate earned $88M on $1,526M of net assets in 1987, about 5.8%. In 1986 it earned $27.8M on $1,448M, about 1.9%.
- Equity income fell 57% in 1988 because of write-downs at the entertainment affiliate.
- These stakes add little to the return on equity that matters.

**EPS growth, a secondary check.** EPS rose from $1.35 in 1983 to $2.85 in 1988, about 16% a year. Net income grew about 13.5% a year over the same period. The gap comes from buybacks: average shares fell from 408M to about 365M. EPS growth is real, but the return on equity above is the better evidence of quality.

**Margins** (1987 restated basis, provisions excluded):

| | 1985 | 1986 | 1987 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gross margin | 50.5% | 50.5% | 52.6% |
| Operating margin | 13.7% | 15.4% | 17.8% |

Margins are rising as revenue shifts from syrup to concentrate. Gross margin on concentrate sold to US bottlers was about 63% in 1986.

## 3.2 Durable competitive advantage: is there a moat?

The GEICO passage defines a moat as a measurable advantage over competitors that protects a valuable franchise, and that management keeps widening (`brk-letter-1986#16`). The packet's evidence:

**Share and scale advantage**
- Company brands hold over 44% of soft drinks sold worldwide, excluding the USSR and China. 1987 volume was double the nearest competitor's (`ar-1987`).
- US share was 39%+ in 1986, nine points ahead of the primary competitor, and over 40% in 1988 (`upi-1989-01-31`).
- In US fountain sales the company has a 59% share, outsells its nearest rival more than 2-to-1, and captured about three-quarters of segment growth over four years.
- Management describes the company as "low-cost producer in most of the 155 countries where we do business."

**Brand durability**
- The packet calls Brand A the world's best-known trademark, with record awareness and preference scores in 1986.
- When the formula was changed in April 1985, the company brought back the original as Brand A classic. By 1986 classic was the best-selling US soft drink.
- The 1985 change itself is a mistake to note. What followed shows how attached consumers are to the original product.

**Pricing power**
- The new bottler contracts allow flexible pricing, and the company used them in October 1986.
- Management states the company "is able to adjust prices to compensate for increasing costs" (`ar-1986`, `ar-1987` inflation sections).
- In Latin America, price increases were kept in line with inflation. In 1988 operating income there grew 17% on 1% volume growth.
- This matters because the 1977 inflation article and the 1986 tax passage separate franchises that keep the benefit of cost or tax changes from price-competitive industries that pass them through (`fortune-1977-inflation#6`, `brk-letter-1986#22`). The packet places this company in the first group.

**Low capital needs**
- Core soft-drink capital spending in 1987 was $170M against operating income of over $1.4B.
- Compare Burlington Industries (`brk-letter-1985#9`), where heavy capital spending kept a commodity business alive while returns fell. The economics here are the opposite.

**Long growth runway**
- International per-capita consumption was 16% of the US rate in 1986.
- US per-capita consumption itself rose 35% between 1980 and 1987.
- 65% of volume and 74% of operating income already come from outside the US.

**Gaps in the moat**
- *US pricing limits.* Standard contracts with most US bottlers other than the bottling affiliate fix Brand A syrup prices by formula. This partly explains why US returns are lower.
- *Dependence on bottlers.* The franchise relies on a bottling system that earns low returns (about 6% at the bottling affiliate). Management keeps propping it up with equity investments: the bottling affiliate, the Canadian bottler, Consolidated, Johnston, Philippines, Taiwan and Indonesia.
- *Currency effects.* Key hard currencies strengthened against the dollar by 27% in 1986, 16% in 1987 and 10% in 1988. Some of the 21–25% international operating income growth in those years is translation, not business improvement.
- *Puerto Rico tax exemption.* It cut the effective tax rate by about 4 points in 1986 and expires in 1995.
- *Comparative costs.* The packet gives no competitor cost or margin data. For GEICO the framework could measure the moat directly against competitors' expense ratios. I cannot do the same here.

## 3.3 Owner earnings

Owner earnings are reported earnings, plus depreciation, amortization and other non-cash charges, minus *average* capital expenditure needed to maintain the business (`brk-letter-1986`). The packet does not split capital spending into maintenance and growth, so I give a range. I also adjust for affiliate earnings that are booked but not received in cash, given those affiliates' low returns.

**1987** (`ar-1987` funds statement)

| | Floor (all capex treated as maintenance) | Upper (maintenance capex ≈ depreciation) |
|---|---|---|
| Net income | 916.1 | 916.1 |
| + Depreciation | 151.9 | 151.9 |
| + Goodwill amortization | 1.6 | 1.6 |
| − Capital expenditure | (299.6) | (151.9) |
| = Owner earnings | **770.0** ($2.04/sh) | **917.7** ($2.43/sh) |
| − Undistributed equity income | (79.7) | (79.7) |
| = Excluding retained affiliate earnings | **690.3** ($1.83/sh) | **838.0** ($2.22/sh) |

Per-share figures use 377.4M average shares. The $40M gain and $36M provision in 1987 are both non-cash and roughly offset, so I ignore them.

I treat the floor as too harsh. Capital spending above depreciation is going into international growth infrastructure and corporate buildings: corporate capex was $74M in 1987 and $98M in 1986, including a new office building. Neither is maintenance. My working estimate for 1987 is about **$2.15–2.30 per share**, roughly 90% of reported EPS.

**1988.** Net income was $1,040M, or $2.85 per share. Capital spending, depreciation and cash flow are not in the packet. Equity income fell 57%, so the affiliate adjustment shrinks. Applying the 1987 relationships, I assume 1988 owner earnings of roughly **$2.40–2.80 per share**. This is an estimate, not a figure from the packet.

**What this means.** Reported earnings and owner earnings are close, so most of what is earned is genuinely available to owners. Cash provided by operations was $1,231M in 1987. That figure includes a $273M working-capital release that should not be counted on every year.

## Facts the framework would want that the packet lacks

1. The **1988 annual report**: year-end equity, debt, cash, capital spending, depreciation, segment assets and share count. 1988 is known only from two wire stories.
2. The **split of capital spending between maintenance and growth**, by segment.
3. **Segment liabilities**, to measure return on net operating capital rather than gross assets.
4. **Concentrate price per unit over time**, in real terms, to measure pricing power directly.
5. **Currency-neutral operating income growth** for international.
6. **The primary competitor's share, costs and margins**, especially internationally, to measure the moat the way GEICO's expense-ratio gap was measured.
7. **Bottler-system economics and contract terms**: which US bottlers are on formula pricing and when those contracts can be renegotiated.
8. **Size of the 1988 write-down at the entertainment affiliate**, and the market value versus carrying value of all affiliate stakes in 1988. At year-end 1987 market value exceeded carrying value by $44M.
9. **Tax rate after the Puerto Rico exemption expires** in 1995.
10. **Contingent exposures** in 1988: $394M of guarantees and $325M of receivables sold with recourse at the entertainment affiliate, both at year-end 1987.
