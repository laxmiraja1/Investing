**Company A: Recommendation to Buy, Building to About 25% of the Portfolio**

Company A sells soft-drink syrups and concentrates to independent and part-owned bottlers in more than 155 countries, under what its reports call the world's best-known trademark. The bottlers handle the capital-heavy work of bottling, canning and delivery. The company supplies the flavour base, the brand and the marketing. In 1987 the soft-drink business produced over 95% of operating income. Two side activities remain: a foods business built mainly on orange juice, and 49% stakes in a large US bottler, a Canadian bottler and a film-and-television company. All three stakes were moved out of the consolidated accounts in 1986 and 1987.

The company passes every screen we apply. It is not a turnaround: earnings per share rose every year from $0.84 in 1977 to $2.85 in 1988. Return on equity ran at about 20% through 1983, when the company carried almost no debt. It has since risen to 27% in 1987 and about 33% in 1988. We checked whether that rise came from borrowing. Gross debt has grown, but after excess cash and short-term receivables, net debt was only about 15% of capital at the end of 1987. Net interest expense fell by half in 1988.

The core economics are better than the consolidated figures suggest. In 1987 the soft-drink segment earned about 34% pretax on its identifiable assets, and the international side earned over 50%. Capital spending in soft drinks was $170 million against more than $1.4 billion of segment operating income.

There is real evidence of a lasting advantage. Company brands account for over 44% of soft drinks sold worldwide, outside the Soviet Union and China. That is twice the volume of the nearest competitor. The company holds over 40% of the US market and 59% of US fountain sales. When it changed the flagship formula in 1985, consumers pushed back. The original was reinstated and became the country's best-selling soft drink within a year. That episode shows how attached customers are to the product. Management says it can raise prices to offset rising costs, and the 1988 results support this. In Latin America, operating income rose 17% on volume growth of 1%. Outside the US, people drink only about one-sixth as many soft drinks per head as Americans do, which leaves a long runway for growth.

The record on reinvesting earnings is strong, though not flawless. From 1982 to 1988 the company kept about $5.93 per share of earnings after dividends. Over the same period the share price rose by about $35. Even if the stock still traded at its 1981 multiple of earnings, each retained dollar would have produced more than two dollars of value. Since 1984 the company has bought back about 69 million shares, roughly 13% of the company, mostly at 12 to 17 times earnings.

There were two lapses. Management diversified into film, television and foods. It also issued shares to pay for some of those purchases, apparently at prices below what the shares were worth. Those businesses now earn far less than the core: the entertainment company earned about 4% on its net assets in 1987, and the US bottler about 6%. Since 1985 the direction has been encouraging. Unrelated businesses have been sold or spun off, cash has gone into buybacks, and management now says it prefers to invest within its existing businesses. The annual reports are candid about one-off items and currency effects, although their tone is more promotional than we would like.

On value, the stock looks nothing like a classic bargain. Net working capital is essentially zero. We value it instead on owner earnings: what the business could pay out while keeping its competitive position. We estimate 1988 owner earnings at about $2.60 per share, against reported earnings of $2.85. At the recent price of $46.56, that is a yield of about 5.6%. To match a long government bond, which we assume yields about 9%, that yield would need to grow by only about 3.5% a year indefinitely. Per-share earnings have grown about 16% a year over the past five years, and management expects mid-teens growth in 1989.

Our base case assumes 13% annual growth for ten years, followed by a valuation of 15 times owner earnings. On that basis the shares are worth about $54 to $64, and an owner buying today would earn about 13% a year. If the past record continues, value is higher, around $64 to $77. If growth slows to 10%, the shares are roughly fairly priced today.

The margin of safety, then, comes from how durable and predictable the franchise is, not from a deep discount. That is the reason we stop at about a quarter of the portfolio rather than the larger share our guidelines allow when both the facts and the price are compelling. We would add meaningfully if the price fell toward $40 while the business stayed intact. No catalyst is needed. We expect the result to come from earnings compounding inside the company, and we intend to hold for as long as the economics and the management remain what they appear to be.

Several developments would change our view:
- Evidence that recent growth came mostly from the weak dollar and the shrinking share count, rather than from volume and pricing.
- Sustained loss of market share to the main competitor.
- A large acquisition outside soft drinks, especially one paid for in stock.
- Buybacks at prices well above the business's value, or a move toward the 35% net-debt ceiling for financial engineering rather than operating need.

Our information also has gaps. We do not have the 1988 annual report, proxy data on insider ownership and pay, a breakdown of capital spending between maintenance and growth, or the cost structure of competitors. We would want to meet management and close those gaps as we build the position. A higher share price on its own would not be a reason to sell.
