# Stage 4: Valuation (framework §5) and catalyst/timing (§6)

At $46.56 the stock fails the only valuation rule written into §5, the net-working-capital test, and fails it completely. Elsewhere, though, the framework says it has moved away from that rule for businesses like this one. Judged instead on the standard it uses by 1989, an outstanding business at a sensible price, the price is sensible. The margin of safety is moderate, about 15–30% under base-case assumptions. It depends almost entirely on per-share owner earnings growing at 11% a year or better. No catalyst is needed.

## 5.1 The rule as written: buy below net working capital

The framework's only §5 rule is the 1965 bargain test: net working capital "before placing any value on the plants and equipment" (`bpl-letter-1965`). The latest balance sheet in the packet is year-end 1987 (`ar-1987`):

| ($ millions) | 31 Dec 1987 |
|---|---|
| Current assets | 4,136.2 |
| Current liabilities | (4,118.5) |
| **Net working capital** | **17.7** (about $0.05 per share) |
| Long-term debt | (803.4) |
| Deferred income taxes | (209.9) |
| **Net working capital less all long-term liabilities** | **about ($996)** (about −$2.70 per share) |

Against a price of $46.56, a market value of about $17.3B, and roughly 355M shares after the 1988 buybacks, the test fails. No margin of safety exists on this measure.

The framework also explains why it no longer weights this test for a business like this one. The 1987 letter: "Our goal is to find an outstanding business at a sensible price, not a mediocre business at a bargain price." It adds that the search for "bargains" produced "an education in the economics of short-line farm implement manufacturers, third-place department stores, and New England textile manufacturers" (`brk-letter-1987#18`). The 1981 letter sets the operative test: a price that makes sense "even should the future of the acquired enterprise develop much along the lines of its past" (`brk-letter-1981#3`). Value here sits in the trademark and the earning power, not in the balance sheet. I therefore value on owner earnings (§3), which is the measure the framework itself defines.

## 5.2 Valuation on owner earnings: does the price make sense if the future resembles the past?

**Inputs**

- **1988 owner earnings: about $2.60 per share**, the midpoint of the $2.40–2.80 range from Stage 2. This is an estimate, because 1988 capital spending and depreciation are not in the packet.
- **1988 reported EPS:** $2.85. Dividend $1.20.
- **Record:** EPS grew about 16% a year from 1983 to 1988. Net income grew about 13.5%. Management guides to "mid-teens" EPS growth for 1989 (`upi-1989-01-31`). Underlying growth, before currency gains and buybacks, is probably about 10–13%.
- **Long government bond yield on the as-of date: not in the packet.** I assume about 9%. The company's own long-term debt carries 9⅞–11⅜% coupons, with about 10% on other notes, so 9% is a reasonable risk-free proxy. Management's stated after-tax cost of capital is 12%.

**1. Current yield against bonds.** Owner earnings of $2.60 on $46.56 is a **5.6% yield**, range 5.2–6.0%. That is below the assumed 9% bond yield. The stock therefore beats bonds only if per-share owner earnings grow over time.

Treated as a growing stream, the price needs about **3.5% a year of perpetual growth** to match a 9% bond (9% minus 5.6%), or about **6.5%** to match management's 12% cost of capital. Against a 12-year record of 13–16% earnings growth, those required rates are low. That gap is where the margin of safety lies.

**2. Ten-year owner's return at today's price.** Owner earnings grow at rate g for 10 years. The stock is then valued at 12x or 15x owner earnings, below today's roughly 18x on owner earnings and 16.3x on reported EPS. Dividends add about 2.5 points a year.

| Per-share owner-earnings growth | OE in 1998 | Value at 15x / 12x | Annual return, 15x / 12x |
|---|---|---|---|
| 15% (the record) | $10.52 | $158 / $126 | about 15.5% / 13% |
| 13% (base) | $8.83 | $132 / $106 | about 13.5% / 11% |
| 10% (cautious) | $6.74 | $101 / $81 | about 10.5% / 8% |
| 7% (franchise stalls) | $5.11 | $77 / $61 | about 7.5% / 5.5% |

**3. Price that earns a target return**, using the 15x exit:

| Growth | Value for a 10% return (bonds + 1 pt) | Value for a 12% return (management's hurdle) |
|---|---|---|
| 15% | about $77 | about $64 |
| 13% | about $64 | about $54 |
| 10% | about $49 | about $41 |

**Not counted above, and a modest cushion:** the 49% bottling and entertainment stakes plus other investees, carried at about $2.26B at year-end 1987, roughly $5–6 per share. Their market value was about $44M above carrying value at year-end 1987, so carrying value is broadly confirmed. Only cash dividends from these stakes are captured in owner earnings. The entertainment write-down in 1988 lowers the figure by an amount the packet does not give.

**A risk pulling the other way:** about 74% of operating income comes from abroad. The weak dollar helped results in 1986–88 (key currencies strengthened 27%, 16% and 10% against it). A stronger dollar would reduce reported owner earnings for a time. It would not reduce the franchise's value in local currencies.

## 5.3 Margin of safety against the hurdle

**Hurdle.** As of this date the framework sets no numeric hurdle for a business of this quality. The 1962 letter's "comfortable margin of safety" applies to the "generals" (`bpl-letter-1962#4`). I use the 1981 test made concrete: under growth assumptions slower than the record, expected return should comfortably exceed the bond rate and reach roughly 12%.

| Case | Intrinsic value per share | Margin of safety at $46.56 |
|---|---|---|
| Net working capital (literal §5 rule) | about $0 | Fails |
| Record continues (15%) | $64–77 | 27–40% |
| **Base (13%)** | **$54–64** | **14–27%** |
| Cautious (10%) | $41–49 | −13% to +5% (about fair) |
| Franchise stalls (7%) | below $41 | Negative |

**Verdict.** On the 1981 standard, the price is sensible. Under base and record-like growth, the expected return of about 11–15% clears both the assumed 9% bond yield and the 12% cost of capital. If growth falls to about 10%, the stock is roughly fairly priced and the return comes in only modestly above bonds.

The margin of safety is therefore smaller than the old bargain approach demanded. It rests on how durable the franchise is, which Stage 2 found strong, and not on the balance sheet. That makes the size of the position a question for Stage 5 under the "extremely high probability that our facts and reasoning are correct" test (`bpl-letter-1965`).

**Cross-check with Stage 3.** On these values, the 1984–85 buybacks at $18–25 were well below intrinsic value. The 1987 purchases at about $42.60 and the estimated 1988 purchases at about $39–40 were at or below the low end. The management buyback record passes.

## 6. Catalyst and timing

- **No catalyst needed.** "The market may ignore business success for a while, but eventually will confirm it" (`brk-letter-1987`). The case rests on owner earnings compounding. It does not depend on any event revaluing the shares.
- **Not a work-out.** No sale, merger, liquidation or tender is pending. The 1986–87 corporate actions, the bottler IPOs and the entertainment spin-off, are already done (`bpl-letter-1957`).
- **Not a control situation.** At about $17B of market value, a stake large enough to influence the company is neither available nor needed (`bpl-letter-1963`). Management is already acting in owners' interests.
- **Market behaviour.** "Mr. Market is there to serve you, not to guide you" (`brk-letter-1987`).
  - The stock is near its 52-week high ($35.04–49.12) and has re-rated from about 9x EPS in 1981 to about 16x.
  - The best entry was the post-crash period of 1988, around $35–38, or about 12–13x EPS.
  - The framework does not forecast markets (`bpl-letter-1966-mid`), so missing that window is not a reason to wait.
  - The price-sensitive rule is the temperament rule in §9 (`brk-letter-1986`). Nothing in the packet suggests investor greed about this stock now. It trades below its 1987 peak of $53.
- **Helpful mechanics.** About 14.5M shares remain under the company's 40M-share buyback program, which runs through 1990. Continued buybacks at or below value keep raising per-share value for any owner who holds.
