# Stage 3: Management and capital allocation (framework §4)

Management passes. Since 1981 every dollar kept in the business is associated with several dollars of market value, and buybacks since 1984 have been large, steady, and mostly made at modest multiples of earnings. Against that, the 1980s record includes two things the framework disapproves of: diversifying into lower-return businesses (film and TV, foods, stakes in bottlers), and apparently issuing stock to pay for one of those moves. The reports are also more promotional than this framework likes. The direction since 1985 is clearly better: businesses sold, entertainment moved out of the company, cash sent to buybacks, and a stated preference for staying within existing lines of business. I apply the three rules of §4 in order, then list what the packet cannot tell me.

## 4.1 Honest, able management already in place; candid reporting

**In place and able.** The current team was assembled in 1981 (`ar-1987` letter). Under it:
- Return on equity rose from about 20% to about 33%, with net debt around 15% of capital (Stage 2).
- International soft drinks earn over 50% pretax on identifiable assets.
- US share rose above 40% and fountain share to 59%.
- Germany's bottling system was consolidated from 102 to 68 bottlers, and US bottlers were moved from syrup to concentrate, which lowers costs.

The packet shows one large operating mistake: the April 1985 formula change. It also shows the fix. The original formula came back as Brand A classic, which was the best-selling US soft drink by 1986. Management corrected the mistake quickly instead of defending it.

**Candor.** The framework's standard is that reports should not be "a public relations document" (`brk-letter-1979`). Managers should tell owners "the business facts that we would want to know if our positions were reversed" (`brk-letter-1983#2`).

Where the reports meet that standard:
- One-off items are quantified and stripped out. The 1986 letter separates the $375M gain from the $180M of provisions. The 1987 letter states growth "excluding 35 cents per share of unusual items."
- Currency effects are quantified: key currencies strengthened 27% against the dollar in 1986 and 16% in 1987. Management does not present that as operating success.
- Weak spots are named. "We were not satisfied with the performance of the film studio's feature films." Foods "is obviously in transition."
- Management discloses its own hurdle rate: about 12% after tax.
- It discloses that current-cost income would be lower than reported, and that gains from being a net monetary debtor "do not necessarily imply that incurring more debt would be beneficial." That is unusually frank.
- Contingent liabilities, receivables sold with recourse, and a 63% gross margin on concentrate are all disclosed.

Where they fall short:
- Much of the tone is promotional: a "rich portfolio of opportunities," potential that "appears limitless," and a claimed "$250 billion" contribution to the world economy using "the commonly accepted multiplier factor of five."
- The language of "increasing shareholder value" runs throughout. The 1984 letter describes this kind of phrase as the "public relations-inspired" season's favorite and says the test is what a manager actually does, not what he says (`brk-letter-1984#4`). Judged by actions, this management mostly passes (see 4.2).
- "Innovative business structures" produced reported gains from selling stock in subsidiaries: $375M in 1986 and $40M in 1987. They also forced restatements that make year-to-year comparison hard. These gains are fully disclosed, so this is not gimmickry, but the reports promote financial engineering more than this framework would.
- The 1986 letter has a section headed "Using Financial Leverage" that presents borrowing capacity as an opportunity in its own right.

Overall: candid on the numbers, promotional in tone.

## 4.2 Buybacks only well below intrinsic value

The framework's rule is that "major repurchases at prices well below per-share intrinsic business value immediately increase... that value" (`brk-letter-1984#4`). The record:

| Year | Shares bought (M) | Cost ($M) | Avg price | Price / that year's EPS |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1984 | 18.4 | 335.7 | $18.24 | 11.6x ($1.57) |
| 1985 | 15.0 | 379.9 | $25.33 | 14.7x ($1.72) |
| 1986 | 2.9 | 110.5 | $38.10 | 15.7x reported; ~18x on normalized ~$2.07 |
| 1987 | 14.2 | 605.1 | $42.61 | 17.5x ($2.43) |
| 1988 | 18.9 | not in packet | ~$39–40 assumed | ~14x ($2.85) |
| **Total** | **~69** | **~$2.2B** (1988 estimated) | | |

The 1988 cost is my assumption. The 1988 price range was about $35–45, so I assume roughly $740M.

Assessment:
- **Scale.** Net of option issuance, the share count fell from about 410M at the start of 1984 to about 355M. That is roughly 13% of the company returned to continuing owners. This is "demonstrated" behavior, the word the 1984 letter stresses.
- **Price paid against value.** Stage 2 put owner earnings at about $2.15–2.30 per share in 1987. The core business earns over 30% pretax on its assets and is growing in the low-to-mid teens. On that basis the 1984–85 purchases at 11–15 times earnings were almost certainly well below intrinsic value. The 1987 purchases at about 17.5 times, some made near the year's high, are less clearly so. Whether those prices were "well below" value depends on the Stage 4 valuation.
- **Stated rationale.** Management says repurchases began "after management determined that the Company's available cash and debt capacity exceeded the funds required." That is an excess-cash rationale. It is not stated as a price-against-value judgement, which is what the framework wants. The 1987 report goes further: "Often the best use of excess cash generated by our businesses has been to invest in our own stock." I'd want to hear directly how management decides what price is too high (see the final section).
- **Debt-funded buybacks.** The net debt ceiling was raised to 35% partly to fund buybacks. Actual net debt stayed around 15%, and net interest expense fell 52% in 1988. The intent is still something to watch.
- **Offsetting dilution.** Options exercised add about 1.2–1.3M shares a year. Restricted stock, a new 8M-share option plan, and cash-settled performance units on 555k shares also exist. This is small next to the buybacks: roughly 0.3–0.4% of shares a year.

## 4.3 Each $1 retained must create at least $1 of market value

The test is "whether retention, over time, delivers shareholders at least $1 of market value for each $1 retained" (`brk-letter-1983`). I measure per share from year-end 1981 (the team's first full year) to the last close.

| | Per share |
|---|---|
| Net income per share 1982–1988 (1.32 + 1.37 + 1.59 + 1.84 + 2.42 + 2.43 + 2.85) | $13.82 |
| Less cash dividends (0.83 + 0.89 + 0.92 + 0.99 + 1.04 + 1.12 + 1.20) | ($6.99) |
| Less in-kind dividend of entertainment-affiliate stock, at carrying value | ($0.90) |
| **Retained per share** | **≈ $5.93** |
| Price at year-end 1981 → 1989-02-24 | $11.58 → $46.56 |
| **Market value gained per share** | **≈ $34.98** |

Each retained dollar is associated with about **$5.90 of market value**, far above the $1 hurdle.

Part of that gain is the market paying a higher multiple: about 8.9 times 1981 EPS of $1.30, against 16.3 times now. The test should not rest on the market changing its mind. Holding the multiple at 8.9 times, 1988 EPS would support about $25.40, a gain of about $13.80, which is still about 2.3 times retained. On an earnings basis, EPS rose $1.55 on $5.93 retained, roughly a 26% return on retained capital. Some of that came from buybacks, a lower US tax rate and the weak dollar, but the margin over the hurdle is wide.

**Where the retained capital went.** The total passes, but not every use earned its keep.

| Use | Evidence in packet | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| Core soft-drink reinvestment | Capex about $170M in 1987; segment returns 34–52% pretax | Excellent |
| Buybacks | About 69M shares, about $2.2B | Good; mostly at modest multiples |
| Entertainment: 1982 film studio, 1985 Embassy/Tandem (about $178M cash plus 7.1M shares), 1986 Merv Griffin (about $200M), theatres | Operating income grew 30%+ a year 1982–86, but the affiliate earned about $50.7M on $1.14B of net assets in 1987 (about 4.4%); 1988 write-downs; market value of public investees only $44M above carrying value at YE1987 | Far below the core's returns and below management's own 12% hurdle; now 49%-owned at arm's length |
| Share issuance for acquisitions | Average shares rose from 372M (1981) to 408M (1983), around the June 1982 film-studio purchase; 7.1M shares issued for Embassy in 1985 | Probably issued at 8–15 times earnings of a franchise now plainly worth more. This is the dilution the 1982 letter warns against (`brk-letter-1982#17`). The packet does not state the 1982 terms, so this is inferred from share counts. |
| Bottler stakes: bottling affiliate about $749M, Canadian bottler $84M, Consolidated $63M, Johnston $54M, Asian joint ventures | Bottling affiliate earned about 1.9% (1986) and 5.8% (1987) on net assets | Fails management's own 12% hurdle when judged alone; justified only as support for concentrate volume, which the packet cannot verify |
| Foods | Return on assets 24% (1985) → 16% (1987); restructuring; frozen orange juice volume down 22% in 1988 | Mediocre; small (about 5–8% of operating income) |
| Divestitures | Plastics sold for $112M (1985), pasta (1984), Embassy Pictures and home video, South Africa | Shows willingness to sell weak businesses |

The 1983 letter warns that "major additional investment in a terrible industry usually is about as rewarding as struggling in quicksand" (`brk-letter-1983#2`). None of these side businesses is that bad, but none comes close to the core. The 1987 report signals a narrowing: "preferring investments within our existing lines of business." Since the core needs little capital, the safest use of future retained earnings is more buybacks at sensible prices.

**Dividend policy.** The target payout was cut from about 65% (1983) toward 40%: 46% in 1987 and about 42% in 1988. Keeping more earnings is justified only if the retained dollars earn their keep. Historically they have, mainly through the core and buybacks.

## What the investor would have relied on that the packet lacks

1. **Meeting management directly.** The framework judges managers largely by personal assessment, as in the comments on Mrs. B and on Murphy and Burke (`brk-letter-1984#7`, `brk-letter-1984#31`). Specific questions:
   - How do the chairman and president decide what price is too high for buybacks?
   - What do they plan for future excess cash: more buybacks, or more diversification?
   - How do they now see the entertainment detour?
2. **Insider ownership.** The proxy statement is not in the packet. The framework values managers who "eat our own cooking" (`brk-letter-1983#0`).
3. **Executive compensation.** Salaries, bonus formulas, and whether incentives reward returns on capital or just growth and share price. The packet shows only plan sizes and the performance units tied to share price.
4. **Terms of the 1982 film-studio acquisition.** How much was paid in stock, and at what implied value of the shares.
5. **The 1988 annual report.** Actual buyback cost, year-end net debt, size of the entertainment write-down, and the year's capital allocation.
6. **The board's role and succession.** The packet lists directors but says nothing about how independent they are or about management depth below the top two.

## Carried forward to valuation

- I take management as **able and, on the evidence of what it does, owner-oriented**. The main risks are a return to diversification and leverage used for financial structuring rather than operating need.
- The buyback assessment depends on a per-share **intrinsic value** estimate. That is the next stage, using owner earnings of about $2.40–2.80 per share for 1988 and the business's growth runway.
