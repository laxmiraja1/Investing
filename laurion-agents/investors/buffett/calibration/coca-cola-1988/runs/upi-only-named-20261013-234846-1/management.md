# Stage 3: Management and capital allocation (§4), Coca-Cola as of 1989-02-27

I apply §4's three rules in order:
- 4a. Honest, able management already in place, with candid reporting.
- 4b. Buybacks only well below intrinsic value.
- 4c. Each $1 retained must create at least $1 of market value.

The reinvestment and acquisition record is the substance behind 4c, so I cover it there.

---

## 4a. Honest, able management in place; candid reporting

### Is management in place?

Yes. Roberto Goizueta (Chairman and CEO) and Donald Keough (President and COO) put the current team together in 1981 and wrote the "Strategy for the 1980s" (`ar-1987`). M. Douglas Ivester is CFO. The framework requires management already in place because "we can't supply it" (`brk-letter-1982`), and this condition is met. The packet says nothing about succession.

### Is management able?

The record over 1981–1988 is good:

| Measure | 1980–81 | 1987–88 | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| ROE | ~20% (1979–83 range 19.4–21.1%) | 27.2% (1987), 33% (1988) | `ar-1986`, `upi-1989-01-31` |
| US soft drink share | n/a | >39% (1986), >40% (1988), record highs | `ar-1986`, `upi-1989-01-31` |
| Worldwide share | n/a | 43% (1986), 44% (1987) | `ar-1986`, `ar-1987` |
| Soft drink operating margin (restated) | 18.3% (1985) | 23.0% (1987) | `ar-1987` Note 16 |
| Net income | $482M (1981) | $1,040M (1988) | `ar-1987`, `upi-1989-01-31` |

They have also cleared out unrelated businesses:
- sold Ronco pasta (1984);
- sold Presto and Winkler plastics for $112M (1985);
- sold Embassy Home Entertainment (1986);
- moved the entertainment business into a 49%-owned affiliate and distributed shares of it to Coca-Cola shareholders (1987);
- changed the senior management of Foods after it started to slip (1987).

This shows they can recognize that a business is weak and act on it, which `brk-letter-1984#7` praises as defining "with extraordinary realism their area of special competence."

### Is the reporting candid?

The framework wants managers who tell owners "the business facts that we would want to know if our positions were reversed," not "a public relations document" (`brk-letter-1983#2`, `brk-letter-1979`). The reports are mixed.

**Where the reports are candid:**
- **Currency effects are quantified every year.** The reports give tables of exchange-rate movements by country (+27% in 1986, +16% in 1987) and say plainly that results were "aided by a weakening dollar."
- **Weaknesses are admitted.** 1986: "we were not satisfied with the performance of Columbia Pictures' feature films," followed by a leadership change. 1987: "Our foods business is obviously in transition."
- **Inflation disclosure.** Current-cost income "would be less than reported." Gains from the net monetary liability position "do not represent an increase in funds available for distribution to shareholders and do not necessarily imply that incurring more debt would be beneficial." This is unusually honest and reads close to Berkshire's own thinking.
- **Useful operating detail:** the roughly 63% gross margin on concentrate, cooperative advertising as a percentage of bottler revenue, contingent guarantees ($394M) and CPE receivables sold with recourse ($325M).

**Where the reports read as public relations:**
- **The 1985 reformulation is never called a mistake.** The 1986 report only says the new taste "stabilized" and is "positioned directly against the primary competitive cola." A Berkshire-style report would have discussed the error directly.
- **Promotional figures.** For example: "$50 billion in sales added an estimated $250 billion to the world economy" using a "multiplier factor of five."
- **"Excluding unusual items" is the lead number.** Operating income "climbed 23 percent" on that basis. The reported figures are disclosed alongside, so nothing is hidden, but the adjusted figure is the headline.
- **Accounting gains from affiliates' stock sales.** The $375M (CCE) and $40M (TCC) gains are non-cash and are booked when the affiliates sell newly issued shares. They are fully disclosed but flatter reported earnings.
- **Frequent restatements.** Deconsolidations make year-to-year revenue comparisons unreliable. For example, 1986 revenue is $8,669M in one report and $6,977M in the next.

**Verdict on candor:** the reports are substantive and quantitative and fall short of Berkshire's standard mainly in tone. I found nothing misleading. The strongest evidence of integrity is that inconvenient facts (currency, Foods, film, inflation) are disclosed without being asked for.

### Compensation (from the packet only)

- **Restricted stock:** a 3M-share plan. Grants were 883.5k (1984), 282k (1985), 210k (1986) and 178.5k (1987).
- **Stock options:** a 6M-share plan (1983) and an 8M-share plan (1987). About 5.6M options were outstanding at the end of 1987, with 1.1–1.3M exercised each year.
- **Performance Units:** cash awards based on 555,000 shares, paying the stock price minus $20.63.

Assessment:
- Dilution is modest, roughly 1.5% of shares outstanding, and buybacks more than offset it.
- Pay is tied to the share price, not to returns on capital or business results. The stock-price link aligns management with owners in the long run, but it rewards market moves as well as business results.
- The packet does not give salary levels, bonus formulas, how much stock the top officers personally own, or what the top two received. I can't judge whether pay is reasonable relative to value created. This needs the proxy statement.

## 4b. Buybacks only well below intrinsic value

The rule is that repurchases add value when made "at prices well below per-share intrinsic business value" (`brk-letter-1984`).

### The record

| Year | Shares bought | Cost | Avg price | P/E on that year's EPS | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1984 | 18.4M | $335.7M | ~$18.24 | 11.6x ($1.57) | `ar-1986` |
| 1985 | 15.0M | $379.9M | ~$25.33 | 14.7x ($1.72) | `ar-1986` |
| 1986 | 2.9M | $110.5M | ~$38.37 | 15.9x reported, ~18.5x on ~$2.07 normalized | `ar-1986` |
| 1987 | 14.2M | $605.1M | ~$42.61 | 17.5x ($2.43) | `ar-1987` |
| 1988 | 18.9M | not disclosed; est. ~$740M at ~$39 avg | ~$39 (est.) | ~13.7x ($2.85) | `upi-1989-01-31`, `prices` |
| **Total** | **~69.4M** | **~$2.17B** | **~$31** | | |

At today's $46.56 those shares are worth about $3.23B. The 1984–85 purchases were made at about 40–55% of today's price. The current 40M-share program (more than 10% of shares) was 25.5M complete at the end of 1988.

### Were they "well below intrinsic value"?

Probably yes for most of the dollars spent, but with a qualification.
- From Stage 2, the business earns about 34% pretax on its core assets, needs little capital and grows earnings in the mid-teens. Buying at 12–15x earnings (1984–85 and 1988) means paying an after-tax earnings yield of about 7–8.5% on a stream growing about 15% a year. That is very likely well below what such a business is worth. Stage 4 has to confirm this with a value estimate.
- The 1987 purchases, at about 17.5x and partly at the pre-crash highs (Q3 1987 high $53.13), had a thinner margin.

**The qualification is the stated reason for buying.** Management justifies buybacks on available cash, not on price versus value:
- "Initiated after management determined that... the Company's available cash and debt capacity would exceed the funds required" (`ar-1986`).
- "Often the best use of excess cash generated by our businesses has been to invest in our own stock" (`ar-1987`).

Neither report says the shares were bought because they were cheap relative to value. The results look good, but the packet does not show a price discipline that would stop purchases if the stock became expensive. This is the question I would most want to put to management directly.

**Leverage:** the net debt-to-capital ceiling was raised to 35% partly to fund buybacks, to "reduce the weighted average cost of capital" (`ar-1986`). Actual net debt stayed around 15% (`ar-1987`), and net interest expense fell 52% in 1988 (`upi-1989-01-31`). The business does not need debt (Stage 1), so this is a choice made with a modest balance sheet, not a sign of strain. It still deserves watching, given the framework's dislike of leverage.

**An offsetting issuance:** in 1985 the company issued about 7.1M treasury shares toward the Embassy purchase (`ar-1986` Note 12) while buying back 15M shares at about $25. Issuing stock at the same price at which management judged buybacks attractive is the kind of value-diluting issuance `brk-letter-1982#17` warns against. It was small relative to everything else, but it shows the discipline wasn't consistent then.

## 4c. Each $1 retained must create at least $1 of market value

The test is whether "retention, over time, delivers shareholders at least $1 of market value for each $1 retained" (`brk-letter-1983`). The period is the current management's tenure, from the end of 1980 to today.

### Per-share calculation (split-adjusted, `ar-1986`, `ar-1987`, `upi-1989-01-31`, `prices`)

| | Per share |
|---|---|
| Net income 1981–1988 ($1.30 + 1.32 + 1.37 + 1.59 + 1.84 + 2.42 + 2.43 + 2.85) | $15.12 |
| Cash dividends ($0.77 + 0.83 + 0.89 + 0.92 + 0.99 + 1.04 + 1.12 + 1.20) | ($7.76) |
| CPE in-kind dividend (1987, at carrying value) | ($0.90) |
| **Earnings retained** | **$6.46** |
| Price at end-1980 | $11.13 |
| Price today (1989-02-24) | $46.56 |
| **Market value gained** | **$35.43** |
| **Market value per $1 retained** | **~$5.5** |

This understates the result, because shareholders also kept the CPE shares, which were worth more than their carrying value according to the end-1987 note on public investees.

**Checking for re-rating:** the P/E rose from about 9.8x in 1980 ($11.13 / $1.14) to 16.3x today. If the stock still traded at 9.8x, it would be about $27.90, a gain of $16.77, or about **$2.60 per $1 retained**. The test is passed comfortably with or without the re-rating.

### Where the retained dollars went

The aggregate test passes, but the framework also asks whether the capital was well directed. It went to four places:

1. **International soft drink reinvestment** (coolers, vending, bottling joint ventures in the Philippines, Indonesia, Taiwan and China): excellent. International soft drinks earned about 52% pretax on identifiable assets in 1987 (Stage 2), with volume growth of 7–12% a year. Most of the value under the $1 test came from here.

2. **Entertainment** (Columbia Pictures 1982, Embassy and Tandem 1985 for about $445M, Merv Griffin 1986 for $200M): weak returns.
   - In 1986 the segment earned $235M of operating income on $2.17B of identifiable assets, about 11% pretax, against 34% in soft drinks (`ar-1986` Note 17).
   - CPE now sits on the books at $989M and earns little. CPE write-downs cut Coca-Cola's equity income 57% in 1988 (`upi-1989-01-31`).
   - Management has stopped consolidating the business and returned part of it to shareholders. That is the right correction, but roughly $2B of capital was put outside the franchise at low returns. This is the clearest capital allocation mistake in the packet.

3. **Foods** (Minute Maid and Hi-C; Nutri-Foods for $30M; $113.8M of Foods capex in 1985): mediocre.
   - 1987 operating margin was 4.7% (about 7% before the $36M provision). Frozen orange juice volume fell 22% in 1988.
   - These are commodity-exposed businesses. `brk-letter-1983#2` warns against putting major new capital into them.
   - Management at least is not putting large new amounts in and has changed the people running it.

4. **Bottling affiliates** (CCE, TCC, the UK joint venture, Consolidated, Johnston): defensible strategically, low return directly.
   - About $2.26B of affiliate investments earned about 5% in 1987. CCE's ROE was about 6% (`ar-1987` Note 3).
   - The reasons are sound: strengthen the distribution system and let capital-intensive bottling carry its own debt. The concentrate franchise depends on bottlers being healthy and efficient.
   - Shareholders should still understand that part of the parent's high ROE comes from moving low-return bottling capital into 49%-owned affiliates, and that the parent still carries guarantees.

**Stated hurdle rate:** management says it invests only where "long-term cash returns... exceed the Company's weighted average cost of capital," which it estimates at 12% after tax for soft-drink-like risks (`ar-1986`, `ar-1987`). Raising retention by cutting the payout ratio toward 40% (65% in 1983, 46% in 1987, about 42% in 1988) is justified only if retained dollars keep earning high returns. Given the international runway, they should, as long as the money goes to soft drinks and not to another Columbia.

## What the investor would have relied on that this packet lacks

The framework puts heavy weight on the people: "the quality of the people we will be joining" (`brk-letter-1986#10`), and "high-grade, talented and likeable managers" (`brk-letter-1987#18`). These judgments came from direct contact, which I don't have. Specifically:

1. **Meeting Goizueta and Keough:** how they think about price versus value in buybacks; whether they would stop buying at high prices; whether they now see entertainment and foods as mistakes; their attitude toward a large, passive outside shareholder.
2. **The proxy statement:** officer salaries and bonuses, option grants to the top executives, and how much Coca-Cola stock Goizueta and Keough own personally. This bears on alignment and on "we eat our own cooking" (`brk-letter-1983#0`).
3. **The 1988 annual report:** actual buyback dollars and average price, equity, the 1988 Foods and CPE figures, and the letter to shareholders, to see whether candor about 1988's write-downs improved.
4. **The full economics of the Columbia investment:** total cost against value received, to measure the size of the mistake.
5. **Board and succession:** independence in practice, and who succeeds the current team.
6. **Character evidence from third parties** (bottlers, former employees, counterparties), of the kind the investor gathered for businesses like NFM (`brk-letter-1984#7`).

---

## Conclusion for Stage 3

Management **passes §4**, with specific reservations.

- **Able and in place:** under the 1981 team, ROE rose from about 20% to 33%, share reached records, and non-core businesses are being sold or spun off.
- **$1 retained test:** passed decisively, at about $5.50 of market value per $1 retained, or about $2.60 with no re-rating.
- **Buybacks:** about $2.2B over five years, mostly at 12–15x earnings of a 30%-ROE business. They are likely well below intrinsic value, to be confirmed in Stage 4. The stated rationale, however, is excess cash rather than price.
- **Candor:** substantive and quantitative, with an overly promotional tone.

**Reservations to carry forward:**
- About $2B of earlier diversification into entertainment, plus Foods, earned poor returns. This was the main capital allocation mistake, now being corrected.
- About $2.3B sits in low-return affiliates.
- The 1985 share issuance happened while the company was buying back stock.
- Compensation is tied to share price, and pay levels are unknown.
- Management chose a higher leverage ceiling.
- No direct assessment of the people was possible from the packet.
