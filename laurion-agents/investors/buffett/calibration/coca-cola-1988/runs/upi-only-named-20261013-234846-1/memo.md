# Memo: Purchase of The Coca-Cola Company, February 1989

We recommend buying shares of The Coca-Cola Company at today's price of about $46.56, building toward roughly 20% of the portfolio and continuing to buy at prices up to about $50 a share. This memo explains what the business is, why it passes our tests, what we think it is worth, and what would make us reconsider. We relied only on the company's 1986 and 1987 annual reports, two wire reports on its 1988 results, and recent price history.

Coca-Cola's core business is simple. It sells syrup and concentrate to independent and partly owned bottlers, who make and distribute the finished drinks. This business produces more than 95% of operating income. It needs little capital and earns high margins: roughly 63% gross margin on concentrate sold in the United States. Company brands account for more than 44% of soft drinks sold worldwide, and 1987 volume was double that of the nearest competitor. In fountain sales to restaurants, Coca-Cola outsells its closest rival by more than two to one. US market share passed 40% in 1988.

The best evidence of the franchise's strength is what happened in 1985, when management changed the formula. The original was brought back as Coca-Cola classic, became the best-selling soft drink in America, and the company's total share reached a record the following year. When a business gains customer loyalty after its own owners make a mistake with the product, its competitive position is unusually secure.

The business passes our first screens easily. It is not a turnaround. Income from continuing operations rose every year from $278 million in 1976 to $916 million in 1987, and 1988 net income was $1.04 billion. Dividends have increased for 26 consecutive years. The business does not depend on borrowing. In the late 1970s it earned about 20% on equity with debt at about 3.5% of capital. Today it earns 27–33% on equity, with net debt, after setting aside surplus cash, at about 15% of capital. The return on the soft drink assets themselves is higher still: about 34% before tax overall in 1987, and about 52% internationally.

Management also passes. Our test for retained earnings is whether each dollar kept in the business has produced at least a dollar of market value. From 1981 through 1988, the company retained about $6.46 per share and the stock rose by about $35. Even if the stock still traded at its 1980 price-earnings ratio, the gain would be about $2.60 per dollar retained. Between 1984 and 1988 the company bought back about 69 million shares, mostly at 12 to 17 times earnings. For a business of this quality, those prices were very likely below what the shares were worth.

Management has made mistakes. It put roughly $2 billion into entertainment and juice businesses that earned poor returns. It has moved its capital-intensive bottling operations into 49%-owned affiliates that earn only about 5–6% on their equity, which flatters the parent company's reported returns. It has also raised its own ceiling on debt to 35% of capital. To its credit, management is now spinning off and restructuring these weaker businesses rather than adding to them. Its reports explain currency effects, weak segments, and inflation openly, although the tone is more promotional than we would like.

On value, we should be clear that Coca-Cola does not meet our older test of a bargain. Its net working capital is essentially zero, and the stock sells at more than five times book value. We think that test is the wrong one for a business like this. Our approach is to pay a sensible price for an outstanding business, provided the price still makes sense if the future simply resembles the past.

Owners can take out about $2.40 per share each year after funding the company's growth. Over 1985–87 the company paid about 90% of its earnings to shareholders without increasing its net debt. Stripping out currency gains, operating earnings have grown about 10% a year. If that continues for ten years and then slows to 5%, and we discount the cash at 12% (management's own estimate of its cost of capital), the business is worth about $57 a share, including about $5 for its affiliate stakes. Allowing for the per-share boost from buybacks raises the figure to about $65. At a 10% discount rate the value is about $80 to $90.

Put another way, today's price assumes owner earnings grow only about 6% a year forever. That is a modest assumption for a business whose sales abroad are still only one-sixth of American consumption per person. Our margin of safety is about 18–28% on conservative assumptions. That is moderate rather than wide. It depends partly on interest rates, which our materials do not show. For that reason we are limiting the position to about 20% rather than going higher. We expect to hold indefinitely and need no particular event to unlock the value.

We would reconsider under several conditions. On the numbers: if the 1988 cash flow figures show owner earnings well below $2.40, if growth after currency effects turns out below about 8%, or if long-term interest rates are high enough to push value below the price. On management: if it makes another large acquisition outside soft drinks, lets net debt stay above its 35% ceiling, or buys back stock well above its value. We would sell only if the franchise itself began to weaken, if retained earnings stopped producing market value, or if we lost confidence in management's candor.
