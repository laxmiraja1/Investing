# Stage 2: Business analysis (§3), Coca-Cola as of 1989-02-27

I follow §3's three measures in order: return on equity, the moat, and owner earnings.

---

## 3a. Return on equity is the primary test, not EPS growth

The framework asks for "a high earnings rate on equity capital employed (without undue leverage, accounting gimmickry, etc.)" (`brk-letter-1979`). I check the level of ROE first, then whether leverage produced it, then whether accounting items flatter it.

**Level and trend**

| Year | ROE (income from continuing ops ÷ avg. equity) | Total debt / capital | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1976–78 | 20.3%, 20.6%, 21.2% | 3.5–3.8% | `ar-1986` |
| 1979–83 | 21.1%, 19.7%, 19.9%, 19.6%, 19.4% | 6–17% | `ar-1986` |
| 1984 | 21.8% | 30.7%* | `ar-1987` |
| 1985 | 23.5% | 27.7%* | `ar-1987` |
| 1986 | 28.8% | 31.4%* | `ar-1987` |
| 1987 | 27.2% | 45.6%* (gross) / 15.3% net | `ar-1987` |
| 1988 | 33% ("return on average common shareholders' equity") | n/a | `upi-1989-01-31` |

*Gross debt, restated basis.

**Undue leverage?** No.
- From 1976 to 1978 the company earned about 20% on equity with debt at roughly 3.5% of capital. The franchise's base return clearly comes before any borrowing.
- The higher gross debt since 1984 mostly sits against cash. At the end of 1987, debt was offset by $1,468M of cash and securities, $545M of CPE notes (paid in cash 19 January 1988), and $308M of temporary investments (sold for cash 29 January 1988). That leaves net debt of about $380M. At the end of 1985, debt of $1,139M less cash of $835M gave about $300M. Net debt was therefore roughly flat over the two years.
- 1987 interest expense was $279M against interest income of $207M. Net interest cost was about $72M pretax on $1.41B of pretax income. In 1988, net interest expense fell 52% "due to higher cash flow" (`upi-1989-01-31`).

**Accounting gimmickry?** There are items to strip out, but they don't change the result.
- **1986:** a $375M pretax gain from CCE's sale of newly issued shares (+$262M net) and $180M of provisions (–$128M net). Net effect: +$134M. Excluding both, earnings were about $800M on average equity of about $3.25B, roughly **24.6%**, not 28.8%.
- **1987:** the $40M TCC gain and the $36M Foods provision roughly cancel, so **27.2% stands**.
- **Restatements:** each annual report deconsolidates businesses (CCE, CPE, TCC), so 1986 revenue appears as $8,669M in one report and $6,977M in the next. Revenue comparisons are unreliable. Net income and equity are unaffected (`ar-1987` Note 3), so ROE comparisons are valid.
- **Shrinking equity base:** equity has been cut by buybacks ($1.31B of treasury stock at end-1987) and a $335M in-kind dividend. This raises the ratio. However, the ratio still measures return on the capital actually left in the business, and only a highly profitable company can return that much capital and keep its earnings growing.

**What the consolidated ROE hides**

About $2.26B of the end-1987 balance sheet is investments in affiliates: CPE $989M, CCE $749M, TCC $84M, other $435M. These produced only $118.5M of equity income, about 5%. In 1986 the figure was about 6.4% ($155.8M on $2.45B). Excess cash earns only interest. The soft drink franchise therefore earns well above the 27–33% shown for the whole company.

Segment data confirm this:

| Soft drinks (restated, `ar-1987` Note 16) | 1985 | 1986 (before $180M provisions) | 1987 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Operating income ($M) | 830.0 | 1,181.3 | 1,432.5 |
| Identifiable assets ($M) | 3,055.4 | 3,287.0 | 4,174.1 |
| Pretax return on assets | 27% | 36% | 34% |
| Operating margin | 18.3% | ~21% | 23.0% |

In 1987, international soft drinks earned **$1,108.9M on $2,126.7M of assets, about 52% pretax**, at a 27% operating margin. The US earned 15% margins and roughly 16–21% on assets. The US asset base rose by about $620M in 1987, which may reflect bottling assets held temporarily; the packet doesn't explain it.

For comparison, Fortune found that only six of the 1,000 largest US companies averaged more than 30% ROE over the prior decade (`brk-letter-1987#1`). Coca-Cola is now in or near that group, and it earns about 34% pretax on the assets of its core business.

**EPS growth, noted only as secondary**

Restated EPS from continuing operations rose from $1.17 (1981) to $2.85 (1988), about 13.6% a year. Part of that came from:
- buybacks: average shares fell from 393M (1985) to 377M (1987), and another 18.9M were bought in 1988;
- a weaker dollar: key hard currencies strengthened 27% in 1986, 16% in 1987 and 10% in 1988 (`ar-1986`, `ar-1987`, `upi-1989-01-31`).

The framework would not give this figure much weight. ROE is the test.

**Result for 3a:** passes clearly. A 20% return with no debt for a decade has become 27–33% without leverage producing it.

## 3b. Durable competitive advantage: the moat

The test is whether some cost or franchise difference protects these returns from competition, as GEICO's cost gap did (`brk-letter-1986#16`). I separate what the packet shows from what management claims.

**Evidence of the moat**

1. **Share and relative scale.**
   - Company brands make up more than 44% of soft drinks sold worldwide (excluding the USSR and China), and 1987 volume was "double that of our nearest competitor" (`ar-1987`).
   - US share was above 39% in 1986, 9 points ahead of the main competitor (`ar-1986`), and above 40% in 1988 (`upi-1989-01-31`).
   - Fountain share is 59% (1986), outselling the nearest rival by more than 2 to 1. The company captured "nearly three-fourths" of fountain segment growth over four years (`ar-1987`).
   - Vending share is 49%.
   - Volume has grown faster than the industry every year shown.

2. **Pricing power and inflation protection.**
   - US concentrate gross margin was about 63% (`ar-1986`).
   - New CCE contracts allow price changes "at its discretion," and the company used them in October 1986.
   - Management states it "is able to adjust prices to compensate for increasing costs" (`ar-1986`, `ar-1987`).
   - The LIFO reserve is only $19M. Inventories, the material a business has to reinvest in when prices rise, are small.
   - This matches the profile in `fortune-1977-inflation#6`: "the highly profitable companies… require relatively little debt capital."

3. **Consumer franchise that survived a management error.** Changing the formula in April 1985 led to the original being reinstated as "Coca-Cola classic." Classic then became the best-selling US soft drink, and total share reached a record in 1986 (`ar-1986`). A franchise that grows share after its owner tampered with the product is unusually strong.

4. **Product stability.** The core product hasn't changed in a century. As `brk-letter-1987#3` puts it, "severe change and exceptional returns usually don't mix."

5. **Distribution system.** Products are sold in 155 countries through more than 500,000 full-time system jobs, and the company claims to be the "low-cost producer in most of the 155 countries" (`ar-1987`). The low-cost claim is management's assertion; the packet has no competitor cost data to verify it.

6. **Runway.** International per-capita consumption was 16% of the US rate in 1986 (`ar-1986`). US consumption rose from 203 to 274 servings per person from 1980 to 1987 (`ar-1987`). International volume grew 12% (1986), 9% (1987) and 7% (1988). The franchise can absorb more capital at high returns, which is what makes it valuable over ten years or more.

7. **Tax-cut test** (`brk-letter-1986#22`): a strong, unregulated franchise keeps the benefit of lower corporate tax rates. The effective rate fell from 38.2% (1986, original basis) to 35.0% (1987), net margin rose from about 11.5% to 12.0% on a restated, normalized basis, and earnings rose 14% in 1988 as the statutory rate fell to 34%. This points the right way, but the packet doesn't allow tax effects to be separated from currency and volume.

**What maintaining the moat costs, and where it is weak**

- **Advertising:** $720M of media spending in 1987, plus cooperative advertising of about 19–21% of related US bottler revenues (`ar-1986`). The franchise needs heavy, continuous spending. It is a cost of staying competitive, comparable to capex.
- **Bottling:** bottling is capital-intensive and earns low returns.
  - CCE earned $88M on $1.53B of net assets in 1987 (about 6%) and carries $2.25B of noncurrent liabilities.
  - The company has moved bottling into 49%-owned, leveraged affiliates. That flatters its own ROE.
  - The company remains contingently liable for $394M of guarantees and for $325M of CPE receivables sold with recourse.
- **Outside the moat:**
  - Foods earned 4.7% margins in 1987 (about 7% before the provision), and frozen orange juice volume fell 22% in 1988. This is a commodity-exposed business.
  - Entertainment is a hit-driven business. CPE's 1988 write-downs cut equity income 57%.
  - Neither business shares the soft drink franchise's protection, but both are now small relative to it.
- **Currency and emerging markets:** 74% of operating income comes from outside the US. Latin America has "tremendous unit volume and low profit margins" and hyperinflation. Three years of a falling dollar have raised reported growth.

**Result for 3b:** the moat is wide and well supported by figures in the packet: about twice the competitor's volume worldwide, 2-to-1 in fountain, around 63% concentrate margins, and pricing that keeps up with inflation. The castle is the soft drink concentrate business. Bottling, foods and film are not part of it.

## 3c. Owner earnings

Owner earnings are reported earnings plus depreciation, amortization and other non-cash charges, minus the average annual capex needed to keep the business's competitive position (`brk-letter-1986`).

**1987 calculation ($M)** (`ar-1987` statement of changes in financial position)

| Item | |
|---|---|
| Net income | 916.1 |
| + Depreciation and goodwill amortization | 153.5 |
| – Deferred taxes (taxes paid exceeded book expense) | (28.7) |
| + Non-cash restructuring provision | 36.4 |
| – Non-cash TCC gain | (39.7) |
| **Subtotal** | **1,037.6** |
| – Undistributed equity income (affiliates' earnings not received in cash) | (79.7) |
| **Before capex** | **957.9** |

**Capex (the deduction the framework requires)**

| | 1985 | 1986 | 1987 | 3-yr avg |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Gross capex (restated) | 411.8 | 345.8 | 299.6 | 352.4 |
| Disposals of PP&E | 31.3 | 155.0 | 124.5 | |
| Depreciation | 130.1 | 150.9 | 151.9 | |

| Assumption | Owner earnings | Per share (377.4M shares) |
|---|---|---|
| A. All average gross capex treated as maintenance (most conservative) | ~$606M | ~$1.61 |
| B. Maintenance capex ≈ depreciation, the rest treated as growth (international expansion, buildings, Foods citrus plants) | ~$806M | ~$2.14 |
| Reported EPS | $916M | $2.43 |

The packet doesn't split capex into maintenance and growth. Capex runs at about twice depreciation, with heavy spending on international equipment and office buildings, so B is closer to economic reality. That would put owner earnings at roughly 85–90% of reported earnings, before giving any credit for affiliates' retained earnings. Excluding affiliates' retained earnings is justified here because those affiliates (CCE, CPE) earn about 5–6% on their equity.

**Cash cross-check**

From 1985 to 1987, net income totaled $2,573M. The company paid $1,213M in cash dividends and $1,095M in buybacks, $2,308M in all, about 90% of earnings. Over the same period net debt rose only about $80M, and the company also invested in bottlers (Consolidated, Johnston, the UK joint venture). In 1988 it bought 18.9M shares (about $750M at the year's $35–45 price range, my estimate) and paid about $1.20 a share in dividends, while net interest expense fell by half. A company that can return about 90% of reported earnings without adding net debt has owner earnings close to reported earnings. For 1988 I'd estimate owner earnings of about **$0.85–1.0B ($2.35–2.70 a share)**, pending the 1988 cash flow statement.

**Result for 3c:** the business produces large amounts of cash. Reported earnings slightly overstate owner earnings, but not by much.

## Conclusion for Stage 2

The soft drink franchise meets all three tests. It earns about 34% pretax on its assets, and the company as a whole earned 27–33% on equity with almost no net debt. That rests on a demonstrable moat: about twice the nearest competitor's volume, 2-to-1 in fountain, around 63% concentrate margins, pricing that keeps up with inflation, and a consumer franchise strong enough to recover from the 1985 formula change. Owner earnings are close to reported earnings, and there is a long international runway. The weak parts (Foods, CPE, low-return bottling affiliates, currency tailwinds) are real, but they are peripheral to the franchise.

## Facts the framework would want that the packet lacks

1. **The 1988 annual report:** balance sheet, equity, segment and regional operating income, capex, depreciation, cash flow. 1988 is known only from two wire stories, and one of them contains a typo ("$4.6 million" gross profit).
2. **Maintenance versus growth capex**, the key input to owner earnings.
3. **Currency-neutral operating income growth**, and international price versus volume. Three years of a falling dollar make the reported numbers uncertain.
4. **Concentrate margins and returns by region outside the US**, and international bottler contract terms (described only as "generally do not contain restrictions").
5. **Competitor economics** (the main rival's margins, advertising-to-sales and share history), needed to confirm the claimed cost advantage and to measure the gap, as was done with GEICO.
6. **Bottler system health:** independent bottlers' profitability, the US formula-pricing contracts that limit Coca-Cola syrup pricing to most non-CCE bottlers, and CCE's debt capacity.
7. **Look-through earnings** of CCE, CPE and other affiliates, and the size of CPE's 1988 write-downs.
8. **Contingent liabilities** beyond the $394M of guarantees and the $325M of CPE recourse.
9. **Market share history over 10–20 years**, internationally as well as in the US, to judge durability rather than recent momentum.
10. **The tax-rate effect on margins**, isolated, to run the franchise-strength test in `brk-letter-1986#22` properly.
