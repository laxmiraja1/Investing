# Stage 3: Management and capital allocation (§4 of the framework)

Management has been in place since 1981 (the 1987 letter says "When we assembled our present management team in 1981…"). That gives a seven-year record to judge. I apply the three §4 rules in order: honest and able management with candid reporting, buybacks only well below value, and $1 of market value for each $1 retained. Several estimates below are reconstructions from the selected financial data; I label them as such.

---

## §4.1 Honest, able management already in place; candid reporting

The standard: "We don't expect a public relations document when our operating managers tell us what is going on" (`brk-letter-1979`), and "tell you the business facts that we would want to know if our positions were reversed" (`brk-letter-1983#2`).

**Ability, judged by results.** The record is strong in the core business:
- Return on equity rose from about 20% to 27–33%.
- Operating margins widened each year from 1985 to 1987 (13.7% to 15.4% to 17.8%, restated and excluding provisions).
- U.S. share rose from 39% to over 40%, and international volume grew 6–12% a year.
- Selling, administrative and general expense fell as a share of revenue.

This is the GEICO pattern: management widening an existing moat (`brk-letter-1986#16`). The bottler restructurings make the system more efficient: Germany went from 102 bottling entities to 68, and CCE and the Canadian bottler were consolidated. Each restructuring supports concentrate volume.

**Lapses in ability, as the packet shows them.**
- *The 1985 change to the flagship formula.* The packet records it neutrally: "The new taste of Brand A, introduced in April 1985… stabilized." By 1986, Brand A classic, the original formula brought back, was "the driving force behind the Company's momentum," and 1985 needed "extraordinarily high" marketing spending. The recovery was handled well. The original decision is never discussed as a mistake.
- *Entertainment.* This is a move outside the core into a hit-driven business. The studio was bought in 1982. Embassy and Tandem were added in 1985 for about $445M, and Merv Griffin in 1986 for about $200M. The 1986 report called entertainment "a leading force" and promised the company was "prepared for whatever direction the consumer takes the industry." Twelve months later it was folded into the 49%-owned CPE and partly distributed. In 1988, equity income fell 57% on CPE write-downs. These may be reasonable steps to cut back, but they amount to a quiet reversal.
- *Foods.* The 1986 report said the sector had "put in place substantially all the elements necessary to grow faster than the foods industry." In 1987 came a $36M restructuring "concurrent with a change in senior management," and in 1988 frozen orange juice volume fell 22%.

**Candor, judged from the reports.**

| Candid | Promotional |
|---|---|
| Separates out unusual items itself ("Excluding 35 cents per share of unusual items…") | Front sections read as marketing: "The potential appears limitless"; "$250 billion to the world economy" via a "multiplier factor of five" |
| Discloses currency effects by currency and admits they help reported growth | "Our 100 years of prosperity… a rich portfolio of opportunities" |
| Says current-cost income "would be less than reported" and that monetary gains "do not represent an increase in funds available for distribution" | The 1986 entertainment and foods outlooks proved too optimistic within a year, without a full accounting in 1987 |
| Admits "we were not satisfied with the performance of the film studio's feature films" and that Foods is "obviously in transition" | No discussion of returns earned on the roughly $1B-plus put into entertainment before it was restructured |

The back half of the report, the financial review and notes, meets the "facts we'd want to know" test reasonably well. The front half is the kind of public-relations document the 1979 letter says Berkshire does not want from its own managers. That is common for a large consumer company and is not disqualifying. It does mean the narrative cannot be taken at face value; the numbers have to be read instead.

**Verdict on §4.1:** able operators of the core franchise, with a record of mistakes outside it. Disclosures are honest and complete in the numbers, promotional in the narrative. Character cannot be judged from the packet (see the gaps below).

## §4.2 Buybacks only well below intrinsic value

The rule: "major repurchases at prices well below per-share intrinsic business value immediately increase, in a highly significant way, that value… The key word is 'demonstrated'" (`brk-letter-1984#4`).

**The record (shares split-adjusted, from the 1986 and 1987 reports and the 1989 UPI story).**

| Year | Shares bought | Cost | Implied average price | P/E on that year's EPS |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1984 | 18.4M | $336M | ≈ $18.3 | ≈ 11.6x |
| 1985 | 15.0M | $380M | ≈ $25.3 | ≈ 14.7x (on $1.72) |
| 1986 | 2.9M | $110M | ≈ $38.4 | ≈ 15.9x (≈ 19x on underlying earnings) |
| 1987 | 14.2M | $605M | ≈ $42.6 | ≈ 17.5x |
| 1988 | 18.9M | not in packet | (1988 range ≈ $35–45) | ≈ 13–16x on $2.85 |
| **Total** | **≈ 69M** | **≈ $2.2B+** | | |

The 1988 cost is an estimate: 18.9M shares at roughly $40 is about $750M.

**What this shows.**
- *Demonstrated, not just announced.* The company retired about 17% of the 1983 share count. A 40M-share program, more than 10% of shares, is underway, with 25.5M done. Management has written that "Often the best use of excess cash generated by our businesses has been to invest in our own stock" (`ar-1987`). On the "demonstrated pro-shareholder leanings" test, it passes.
- *But the stated criterion is spare capacity, not discount to value.* Repurchases "were initiated after management determined that… available cash and debt capacity would exceed the funds required" (`ar-1986`, repeated in `ar-1987`). That is a sensible reason to return cash. It is not the framework's test, which asks whether the price is well below value.
- *Timing has been uneven.* The 1984–85 purchases at about 12–15x earnings, for a franchise of this quality, were almost certainly well below value. The 1987 purchases, at an average of about $42.6, came close to the year's peak, before the October fall to $29. They were funded partly with short-term debt, which rose about $1.2B in 1987. Whether even these were "well below" value depends on the Stage 5 valuation. If intrinsic value is well above $40 per share, the 1987 purchases also pass.
- *Offsetting issuance.* Average shares rose from 372M (1981) to 390M (1982) to 408M (1983). This is consistent with stock issued in the 1982 studio purchase, though the packet does not give the consideration. Another 7.1M treasury shares went to Embassy in 1985. Stock was issued at roughly 10x earnings for a non-core business and later repurchased at 12–17x. That runs against the warning in `brk-letter-1982#17` about issuing shares on terms below value. The buybacks since then have more than undone the issuance.

**Verdict on §4.2:** passes on willingness and scale, which is the "demonstrated" test. It passes only conditionally on price discipline. The early purchases were clearly cheap. The 1987 purchases need the Stage 5 valuation, and the company's stated trigger is spare cash, not discount to value.

## §4.3 Each $1 retained must create at least $1 of market value

The test: "whether retention, over time, delivers shareholders at least $1 of market value for each $1 retained" (`brk-letter-1983#0`). I measure from end-1981, the first year-end under this management, to end-1988.

**Earnings retained, 1982–88.** Net income was 512 + 559 + 629 + 722 + 934 + 916 + 1,040 = **$5.31B**. Cash dividends were about **$2.70B**: about 324 in 1982 and 363 in 1983, both estimated from dividends per share times average shares, then 364, 389, 403 and 422 as reported, and about 438 in 1988 (estimated as $1.20 × about 365M). Retained earnings were therefore about **$2.6B**, or about **$2.3B** if the $335M CPE in-kind dividend is counted as a distribution.

**Market value created.**
- End-1981: $11.58 × about 372M shares ≈ **$4.3B**.
- End-1988: $44.64 × about 355M shares ≈ **$15.8B**. The 355M is my estimate: 372.4M at end-1987, less 18.9M bought, plus roughly 1.3M from option exercises.
- On top of the market value gain, shareholders received about $2.2B through buybacks and about $0.3B through the in-kind dividend (carrying value).
- Subtract capital put in by new shares, roughly $1B (the 1982 issuance, Embassy, and option exercises; estimated).
- Net value created ≈ 11.5 + 2.2 + 0.3 − 1.0 ≈ **$13B**, against about $2.3–2.6B retained. That is roughly **$5 of market value per $1 retained**.

**Stress test: strip out the re-rating.** The price-to-earnings multiple rose from about 9.9x ($11.58 / $1.17) to about 16x. Holding the 1981 multiple constant, the end-1988 value would be about 9.9 × $2.85 × 355M ≈ $10.0B. That gives about $5.7B of market value gain, plus $2.2B returned, less $1.0B issued, ≈ $6.9B, or roughly **$2.7 per $1 retained**. The test passes by a wide margin even without the re-rating. This happened because the concentrate business grew earnings 2.4x while equity barely grew, from $2.27B to about $3.15B average equity in 1988, because the business needs little capital.

**Where the retained dollars went, and which uses earned their keep.**
- *Concentrate and international infrastructure.* Low capital needs, high returns. This is clearly where the value came from.
- *Bottler equity stakes* (CCE, Consolidated, Johnston, the Canadian bottler, the British joint venture). Direct returns are around 5–6% on carrying value, well below the company's own stated after-tax hurdle of 12%. Management justifies them as strengthening the system that sells its concentrate. That may be right, but the packet does not show it.
- *Entertainment.* Profit grew 30% or more a year to 1986, then the business was restructured, and 1988 brought write-downs. CPE was carried at $989M at end-1987. Total public investees (CCE, CPE and the Canadian bottler) traded only about $44M above carrying value. On the packet's evidence, entertainment roughly returned its capital and no more.
- *Policy going forward.* Management plans to cut the payout to 40% and to borrow up to a 35% net-debt ratio, giving about $1.2B of capacity, "principally to fund investment opportunities." The 1987 statement that it prefers "investments within our existing lines of business" is reassuring. The 1981–87 diversification record is the reason to watch what that capacity is actually spent on.

**Verdict on §4.3:** passes by a wide margin, at about $2.7–5 of market value per $1 retained over seven years. Nearly all of that came from the core franchise. The diversifying uses of capital (entertainment, Foods, low-return bottler stakes) roughly broke even and pulled the average down.

## Compensation (shown in the packet, though not a separate framework rule)

- Restricted stock plan of 3M shares, with roughly 0.2M granted a year. The 1983 option plan covers 6M shares and the 1987 plan another 8M. Options outstanding were about 5.6M at end-1987, around 1.5% of shares. Exercises run about 1.1–1.3M a year, so dilution is about 0.3% a year, small next to the buybacks.
- Performance units pay cash on the share-price gain over $20.63 across 555,000 shares. Pay is tied to the stock price, not to returns on capital or per-share value. That is common, but it is a weaker alignment than the framework's owner-orientation (`brk-letter-1983#0`, "We eat our own cooking").
- The packet does not include the proxy statement, so salaries, bonuses and management's personal shareholdings are unknown.

## Information the investor would have relied on that the packet doesn't contain

1. **Meeting the CEO and President.** The framework's admiration for managers is personal: "tops in both ability and integrity" (`brk-letter-1984#31`) and "people we like and admire" (`brk-letter-1987#18`). Character, candor in private, and attitude to owners cannot be read from annual reports.
2. **The proxy statement:** executive pay, how options are priced and granted, and how many shares officers and directors own.
3. **Repurchase pricing discipline:** the 1988 cost and average price, and whether management estimates intrinsic value before buying.
4. **Management's own account** of the 1985 formula change and the entertainment diversification, including what they learned. This bears directly on the "honest about mistakes" standard (`brk-letter-1985#6`).
5. **Intended uses of the $1.2B debt capacity,** and whether any further diversification outside soft drinks is being considered.
6. **The case for the bottler investments in returns terms:** how much concentrate profit they protect or add, as against their roughly 5% direct return.
7. **Board independence and the owners' voice.** The board includes bankers and executives connected to the company. Their stakes and influence are unknown.
8. **Succession and ages** of the senior team.

---

## Stage 3 conclusion

- **§4.1 Management:** able and focused stewards of the core franchise since 1981. Their record outside the core is weaker: the formula change, entertainment and Foods. Reporting is honest in the numbers and promotional in the narrative. Integrity and owner-orientation need first-hand checking that the packet cannot supply.
- **§4.2 Buybacks:** a demonstrated and large repurchase record, about 17% of shares retired, which is the sign `brk-letter-1984#4` values. The stated trigger is spare cash rather than discount to value, and the 1987 purchases were near the year's high and partly debt-funded. Whether they were well below value is a Stage 5 question.
- **§4.3 Retention test:** passes strongly, at about $2.7–5 of market value per $1 retained, driven by the core business.

On the framework's capital-allocation tests, management passes. The main risk to carry forward is that a team willing to diversify in 1982–86, and now holding $1.2B of borrowing capacity, could again spend the franchise's cash on businesses without a moat.
