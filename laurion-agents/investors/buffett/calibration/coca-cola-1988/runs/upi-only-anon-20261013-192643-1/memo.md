**Investment Memo: Company A (AAA), purchase recommendation as of February 27, 1989**

We recommend buying Company A at $46.56 per share as a long-term core holding. We would build the position over time to roughly a quarter of net worth. What follows sets out the reasoning in the order our process requires: what the business is, whether it passes our tests, what it is worth, and what would make us change course.

Company A's core business is selling soft drink syrups and concentrates to independent bottlers and fountain customers in more than 155 countries, under what its 1987 report calls the world's best-known trademark. That core accounts for over 95% of operating income. The company does not do the capital-heavy bottling itself; it sells a low-volume, high-margin input, with gross margin on U.S. concentrate of about 63%. The company also owns 49% stakes in large bottlers and in an entertainment company, plus a smaller juice business. These sit outside the core and have earned much less.

The company clears every filter we apply. It is not a turnaround: earnings per share rose every year from 1977 to 1988, reaching $2.85 last year. The business is simple and involves no technology we would need to understand. Management has been in place since 1981. It is also large enough to matter, with roughly $1 billion of annual earnings and a market value near $16.5 billion. Debt deserves a closer look. Headline debt reached 45.6% of capital at the end of 1987, but net of excess cash and short-term items it was 15.3%. The core business generates far more cash than it needs. Cash from operations of $1.23 billion in 1987 covered capital spending about four times.

Our primary test is return on equity achieved without undue leverage, and the record is unusually strong. Returns were roughly 20% every year from 1977 to 1983, then rose to 27% in 1987 and 33% in 1988. The consolidated figure understates the core business, because about $2 billion of the balance sheet sits in affiliates earning around 5%. The franchise also appears to be getting stronger. U.S. share rose past 40%, against a principal competitor nine points behind in 1986. Fountain share is 59%, and worldwide volume is double the nearest rival's. Operating margins widened from 13.7% to 17.8% between 1985 and 1987. Outside the United States, per-capita consumption is about one-sixth the U.S. level, so the room for growth is long.

Management passes our capital-allocation tests with qualifications. Since 1981, each dollar retained has produced several dollars of market value, about $2.70 even if we strip out the rise in the market multiple. The company has bought back about 17% of its shares since 1984 and is partway through a further 40-million-share program. The weaknesses are real, though. Management diversified into entertainment and foods, neither of which has a moat, and both have since been restructured. Its stated reason for buying back stock is spare cash, not a clear discount to value; some 1987 purchases were made near the year's high and partly with borrowed money. The annual reports are factual in the financial sections but promotional elsewhere. We would want to meet the senior team and read the proxy statement before treating their judgment as settled.

On value, the old test of buying below net working capital does not apply. Net working capital here is about five cents a share. For a business of this quality the relevant measure is owner earnings: reported earnings adjusted for non-cash items, less the capital spending needed to sustain the business. We estimate 1988 owner earnings at about $2.30 to $2.70 per share. At today's price that is a yield of roughly 5.4%, on earnings that have compounded around 13% a year since 1981 and need little reinvestment to grow.

We assumed 12% growth for ten years, slowing to 5% thereafter, and discounted at the company's own stated 12% cost of capital. Adding the affiliate stakes net of debt, that gives intrinsic value of about $55 to $65 per share. That is a margin of safety of roughly 18 to 28%. In a conservative case of 8% growth, the stock is close to fair value. If the market multiple fell back to its 1981 level, our central case would still return about 11% a year.

This is a sensible price for an outstanding business, not a bargain. The stock sits near its 52-week high and well above the $29 it touched in October 1987. That is why we propose a large position but not the maximum. No catalyst is needed; the return should come from the business compounding. A lower price would widen the margin and justify buying more.

Several gaps in the record limit our confidence. We do not have the long-term government bond yield, which would test our discount rate. We also lack the 1988 balance sheet and cash-flow statement, and the split between maintenance and growth capital spending.

The main risks are management spending its $1.2 billion of borrowing capacity outside the core, rising leverage, and currency. About three-quarters of operating income is earned abroad, and a weaker dollar flattered results in 1986 through 1988.

We would reconsider if market share or soft drink margins began to slip for reasons other than currency. We would also reconsider if management issued shares below value, made a large acquisition outside soft drinks, or pushed debt past its stated ceiling to fund financial engineering. A high stock price alone would not be a reason to sell. Absent those developments, we intend to hold indefinitely.
