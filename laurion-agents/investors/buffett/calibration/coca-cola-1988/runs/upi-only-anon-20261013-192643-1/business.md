# Stage 2: Business analysis (§3 of the framework)

I apply the three §3 tests in the order the framework lists them: return on equity, the moat, then owner earnings. All figures are $ millions unless marked per share.

---

## §3.1 Return on equity, not EPS growth, is the primary test

The test is "a high earnings rate on equity capital employed (without undue leverage, accounting gimmickry, etc.)" (`brk-letter-1979`). Each of its parts gets checked below.

**The raw record** (income from continuing operations ÷ average equity, from the Selected Financial Data in `ar-1986`, `ar-1987` and `upi-1989-01-31`):

| 1977–83 | 1984 | 1985 | 1986 | 1987 | 1988 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 19.4–21.2% every year | 21.8% | 23.5% | 28.8% | 27.2% | 33% (company-reported) |

Two things stand out. Returns were about 20% for at least seven straight years, a level few large companies reach; `brk-letter-1987#1` says only six of Fortune's 1,000 largest averaged more than 30% for a decade. The step up toward 30% then coincides with the current management's strategy since 1981.

**Strip out the one-offs.** 1986 includes a $375M pretax gain from the bottler IPO, worth about $262M after tax, and $180M of provisions, about $128M after tax. Net, that adds roughly $134M. Underlying 1986 income was therefore about $800M on average equity of $3,247M, or **about 24.6%**. In 1987 the $40M gain on the Canadian bottler IPO and the $36M Foods provision roughly offset, so 27.2% stands. 1988's 33% carries no flagged one-offs in the press release.

**Undue leverage?** Gross total debt to total capital rose from 9.1% (1981) to 45.6% (1987). Net of excess cash, temporary investments and the CPE notes repaid in January 1988, it was **15.3%** (`ar-1987`). The rise in ROE therefore did not come mainly from borrowing. It came from two other sources:
- **A shrinking denominator.** Treasury stock grew from $604M (1985) to $1,309M (1987). An in-kind dividend took out another $335M. In 1988 about 18.9M more shares were bought back. A 33% return on 1988 net income of $1,040M implies average equity of about $3.15B, which is *below* the 1987 year-end figure of $3,224M even though the company earned $1B. Buybacks funded from surplus cash are legitimate. Some of the 1987 buybacks, though, were funded with short-term borrowings, which rose about $1.2B. That is exactly what management means by "using financial leverage" (`ar-1986`), and the trend needs watching.
- **Deconsolidation of capital-heavy pieces.** Bottling and entertainment now sit as 49% equity investments. CCE carries $2.72B of liabilities against $1.53B of net assets, none of it guaranteed by Company A. That improves Company A's balance sheet but not the system's economics.

**Accounting gimmickry?** Nothing fraudulent appears, but several items flatter or obscure the numbers. Gains from affiliate stock sales are booked as income. Receivables are sold with recourse ($618M uncollected at end-1986, and another $325M of CPE receivables guaranteed at end-1987). The finance subsidiary CCFC is unconsolidated until 1988. Reporting changes have restated prior years twice in two years. None of this touches the core concentrate earnings, but it means reported net income is a noisier signal than the business underneath it.

**Separate the castle from the investments.** At end-1987 Company A carried about $2.26B of equity-method investments (CPE $989M, CCE $749M, the Canadian bottler $84M, others $435M). These produced $118.5M of equity income, about **5%** on carrying value. CCE alone earned $43M on $749M. Most of the remaining roughly $1B of shareholders' equity therefore supports roughly $750–800M of after-tax core earnings. The concentrate business is earning returns on its own capital comparable to the 57% that `brk-letter-1987#1` reports for Berkshire's best units. The consolidated 27–33% *understates* the core and is dragged down by the low-return affiliates.

**EPS growth, secondary by the framework's rule.** EPS went from $1.17 (1981) to $2.85 (1988), about 13.6% a year, helped by a falling share count (408M average in 1983, about 365M implied in 1988). I note it only because the framework ranks it below ROE.

**Verdict on §3.1:** passes clearly. Returns are high for the long run and even higher in the core business, with modest net leverage. The caveats are a gradual drift toward borrowing for buybacks and low returns in the affiliates.

## §3.2 Durable competitive advantage: the moat around the castle

The framework's example is GEICO's cost gap over competitors, which management keeps widening (`brk-letter-1986#16`). I ask the same three questions here: what is the moat, is it measurable, and is it widening?

**What the moat is.** It is brand plus distribution, rather than cost alone:
- **Share leadership, measured.** The company has more than 40% of the U.S. carbonated soft drink market (1988), up from 39% in 1986, when it was 9 share points ahead of the primary competitor. It holds 59% of U.S. fountain and outsells the nearest rival more than 2-to-1, with growth in chain and fast-food accounts more than three times the rival's. Worldwide it has 44% of soft drinks sold (excluding the USSR and China) and volume double that of the nearest competitor. Its brands hold five of the top ten U.S. soft drinks (`ar-1986`, `ar-1987`).
- **Economics of a toll-taker.** The company sells concentrate, which carries about **63% gross margin** in the U.S. (`ar-1986`). The capital-heavy bottling is done by others. Media advertising of $720M a year (1987) is a scale cost no challenger can easily match.
- **Low cost.** Management claims "low-cost producer in most of the 155 countries" (`ar-1987`). This is the GEICO-type claim, but the packet gives no competitor cost data to verify it.

**Is the moat widening?** On the packet's numbers, yes:

| Operating margin, restated basis | 1985 | 1986 (ex-provisions) | 1987 (ex-provisions) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Consolidated | 13.7% | 15.4% | 17.8% |
| U.S. soft drinks | 11.6% | 14.5% | 15.3% |
| International soft drinks | 22.9% | 24.5% | 27.0% |

Selling, administrative and general expense fell from 36.8% to 34.8% of revenue over 1985–87. 1988 gross margin was about 55%, against 52.6% in 1987. U.S. share rose each year. This is the GEICO pattern of a leader extending its lead.

**Pricing power and who keeps the benefits.** `brk-letter-1986#22` says the strength of the franchise decides whether a company or its customers capture cost changes. The evidence here is mixed:
- *For pricing power:* in October 1986 the company raised concentrate prices to CCE under new flexible-pricing contracts. International contracts "generally do not contain restrictions." Management says it can "adjust prices to compensate for increasing costs" (`ar-1986`, `ar-1987`). Latin American margins rose 17% in 1988 on 1% volume growth, which shows price taken in high-inflation markets.
- *Against:* "Standard contracts with most other bottlers in the United States for Brand A syrup/concentrate provide for formula pricing" (`ar-1986`). In the flagship product's home market, pricing to non-CCE bottlers is contractually constrained. The company also gives back part of its price increases through cooperative advertising: $81M to CCE in 1986, 21% of related revenue.

**Long runway.** International per-capita consumption was **16% of the U.S. rate** in 1986, and international is already 65% of volume and about 77% of soft drink operating income. U.S. per-capita consumption of the company's drinks rose 35% from 1980 to 1987. The castle is growing, not just holding its value.

**Weak parts of the estate.** These lie outside the moat and dilute it:
- *Foods.* Orange juice behaves like a commodity: results swing with orange-solids prices, and in 1988 frozen volume fell 22% and chilled volume 6%. Operating income was $66.6M in 1987, against $1.43B for soft drinks.
- *Entertainment.* Now the 49%-owned CPE, a hit-driven business. Equity income fell 57% in 1988 on write-downs.
- *Bottling.* Capital-intensive with thin returns (CCE about 6% on Company A's carrying value).

These were capital-allocation choices, which are a §4 question. They show the franchise generating cash that was put into businesses without a moat.

**Currency caveat.** Key currencies strengthened against the dollar by 27% (1986), 16% (1987) and 10% (1988). Part of the international profit growth is translation and could reverse. Underlying unit volume growth of 6–10% a year is the durable part.

**Verdict on §3.2:** the moat is wide and measurably widening in the core soft drink business, with a long international runway. U.S. syrup pricing to most bottlers is contractually limited, and the non-soft-drink businesses have no comparable moat.

## §3.3 Owner earnings

The definition (`brk-letter-1986#22` series, `brk-letter-1986`): (a) reported earnings, plus (b) depreciation, amortization and other non-cash charges, less (c) the average capital expenditure needed to maintain competitive position and unit volume. The restated 1987 funds statement is the cleanest basis.

**Step 1: earnings after non-cash items, 1987.**

| Item | $M |
|---|---|
| Reported net income | 916.1 |
| + Depreciation | 151.9 |
| + Goodwill amortization | 1.6 |
| + Restructuring provision (non-cash) | 36.4 |
| − Gain on stock sale by former subsidiary (non-cash) | (39.7) |
| − Equity income not received in cash | (79.7) |
| − Deferred tax credit | (28.7) |
| **= Working capital provided by operations** | **958.8** |

**Step 2: deduct capex.**

| Version | Calculation | 1987 owner earnings | Per share (377.4M avg) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Conservative: all capex treated as maintenance | 958.8 − 299.6 | **≈ $659M** | **≈ $1.75** |
| Using 1985–87 average gross capex ($353M) | 958.8 − 353 | ≈ $606M | ≈ $1.61 |
| Using net capex after disposals (1987: $175M) | 958.8 − 175 | ≈ $784M | ≈ $2.08 |
| If maintenance capex ≈ depreciation | 958.8 − 152 | ≈ $807M | ≈ $2.14 |

**Trend on the conservative definition (working capital from operations minus gross capex):** 1985 ≈ $359M, 1986 ≈ $464M, 1987 ≈ $659M. Owner earnings are rising faster than reported earnings.

**Interpretation.** Some of the capex is clearly for growth rather than maintenance: international infrastructure, coolers and fountain equipment. The framework deducts only what is needed to hold position and volume, so the honest range for 1987 is about **$660–810M, or $1.75–2.15 per share**, against reported EPS of $2.43. The gap is mostly undistributed equity income from the affiliates, which I deliberately exclude, plus capex. The shortfall from reported earnings comes from the investments, not from the concentrate business. Cash from operations of $1.23B in 1987 covered capex roughly four times. As `fortune-1977-inflation` argues, the most profitable companies need relatively little capital, and this business fits that description.

**1988 owner earnings cannot be computed from the packet.** Reported net income was $1,040M ($2.85). Lower equity income in 1988 (down 57%) means less of reported earnings is non-cash affiliate income. If capex and depreciation stayed near 1987 levels, 1988 owner earnings would plausibly be around **$850M–1.0B, or about $2.30–2.70 per share**. That is an assumption, not a packet figure.

## Facts the framework would want that the packet lacks

1. **1988 annual report figures:** year-end equity, debt, cash, capex, depreciation and funds statement. Without them I cannot compute 1988 owner earnings, confirm the 33% ROE, or check how much of 1988's buyback was debt-funded.
2. **Maintenance versus growth capex split.** This is essential for §3.3. I bracketed it rather than guessing.
3. **Competitor cost and margin data** (the primary U.S. competitor's concentrate margins and SG&A). This is the GEICO-style comparison §3.2 calls for. The low-cost-producer claim is unverified.
4. **Concentrate price history against inflation and bottler cost.** This is the direct test of pricing power, along with the full terms of U.S. formula-pricing contracts (their escalators and how many bottlers remain on them).
5. **Bottler economics** across the system, not just CCE. The moat depends on bottlers staying healthy enough to invest.
6. **Look-through earnings of the affiliates** (Company A's share of their retained earnings and whether they ever pay out). This decides how much of reported EPS is owner-available.
7. **Profit by country and currency.** The per-capita table in `ar-1987` is garbled in the packet. I need to separate volume and price growth from translation gains.
8. **CPE's prospects and any further entertainment exposure,** for example the guarantees on CPE receivables.

## Stage 2 conclusion

- **ROE (§3.1):** passes. Returns are high for the long run (about 20% for years, 27–33% recently) and higher still in the core, with net leverage of only about 15%. Watch the drift toward borrowing for buybacks and the low-return equity investments.
- **Moat (§3.2):** a wide, measurable and widening brand-and-distribution franchise in soft drink concentrate. Margins and share are rising and the international runway is long. It is limited in places by U.S. formula pricing, and it is diluted by Foods, entertainment and bottling, none of which have a moat.
- **Owner earnings (§3.3):** about **$660–810M for 1987 ($1.75–2.15 per share)**, growing rapidly, from a business whose core needs little capital. I estimate 1988 at about **$2.30–2.70 per share**, pending the missing figures.

On these three tests the core is the kind of business the framework is looking for: "an outstanding business," per `brk-letter-1987#18`. Whether the *price* is sensible, and whether management's allocation of the surplus cash meets the $1-for-$1 retention test, are questions for §4 and §5.
